# Astronomy

I want a four-article series on observational astronomy for a physics or STEM undergraduate: someone who knows calculus, mechanics and a little thermodynamics, but has never taken an astronomy course. Each article is long and carries four or five figures, and each figure computes what it shows, from real data wherever the claim is about the real sky.

The through-line is that astronomy learns everything from light arriving at one point on Earth. That light carries four things we can measure: direction, brightness, color (spectrum) and timing. Each article pulls one more thing out of that light, and each depends on the one before. The first article is about direction and time: the geometry of the sky as seen from where the reader stands. The second turns angles and brightness into distance. The third uses those distances to put stars on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram and read their physics and life stories from it. The fourth uses what we now know about stars (their radii, masses and brightness) to find planets orbiting them from tiny dips and wobbles in their light.

By the end the reader should be able to say how each number in an astronomy headline was actually measured, which earlier measurement it rests on, and where its error bars come from.

Orbital mechanics is already covered elsewhere in the gallery: Kepler's hidden symmetry in the Noether series (article 7) and symplectic integration of a Kepler orbit in Simulating on a Lattice. Link to those rather than re-deriving them. Solving Kepler's equation for positions is fine to use without ceremony.

## Articles

### 1. The Sky From Where You Stand
The naked-eye sky as a problem in spherical geometry and clocks.
- **The celestial sphere.** A draggable orthographic globe with the horizon, celestial equator, ecliptic and the Sun, plus the bright stars from the Yale Bright Star Catalogue coloured by blackbody temperature from B-V. The reader sets latitude, date and clock time and watches circumpolar stars stay up while others set; holding the clock and dragging the date shows the four-minute gap between sidereal and solar days. A year slider spanning 26,000 years precesses the pole from Thuban through Polaris toward Vega, with the pole's path drawn among the stars. Computes: alt/az from RA/Dec through local sidereal time (from the mean Sun), long-term precession (the Vondrak-Capitaine-Wallace model, not a constant rate), and each star's proper motion.
- **The analemma.** The Sun's position at the same clock time each day for a year traces a figure eight, linked to the equation of time through the year with its two causes drawn separately. The reader moves obliquity and eccentricity sliders and sees the figure eight become a symmetric eight (eccentricity zero) or open into an ellipse (small tilt, since the tilt term goes as its square). Computes: the equation of time from Kepler's equation plus the projection of the ecliptic onto the equator.
- **Retrograde motion.** A heliocentric top view with the Earth-to-planet sightline carried out to a ring of distant stars, linked to a strip of the planet's geocentric ecliptic longitude over three years, driven by real orbital elements. Scrubbing time shows the backward stretch happen at opposition. A planet selector, plus a small chart of every planet's average retrograde spell (days, and degrees lost) against orbital radius. Computes: apparent longitude from the two heliocentric positions, and the spells found in it.
- **Why eclipses repeat.** Two figures. First, the synodic, draconic and anomalistic months (and the day) as four dials for a chosen number of synodic months, with every count up to 700 plotted by how far the Moon sits from a node, so the saros (223), the semester, the inex and the exeligmos stand out. Then every eclipse in NASA's Five Millennium Canon on one row per saros series, with the chosen family's gamma drifting pole to pole and its greatest-eclipse points on a map jumping about 120 degrees west. Computes: the commensurability residuals, and each eclipse's saros number from its date alone (checked against the canon's own numbers on the page).
The article ends on what angles alone can't give us: how far away anything is. The one exception, parallax, opens article 2.

### 2. The Distance Ladder
How a telescope that measures only angles and brightness gets to distances of billions of light years, one calibrated rung at a time.
- **Parallax, and why you can't just invert it.** Two figures. First, Earth's orbit from above linked to a real nearby star's path on the sky over three years, from its Gaia DR3 position, parallax and proper motion: stars near the ecliptic pole trace circles, stars near the ecliptic trace lines, and proper motion pulls the loop into a wave (61 Cygni, Bessel's star, for the extreme case). Computes: the parallax offsets from Earth's position through a low-precision solar ephemeris, and the ellipse's axes against parallax times the sine of ecliptic latitude. Second, a cluster whose members share one distance (M67, members picked by position and proper motion only, so parallax errors stay whole): the reader slides from precise to poor parallaxes and watches the histogram of 1/p skew, with the distribution predicted from the catalogue errors drawn over it. For one star, prior times likelihood gives a posterior; sliding the prior's scale shows that for poor parallaxes the prior decides, and that the prior is a claim about the population. Computes: the cluster distance from its bright members, the predicted 1/p distribution, and the posterior under a Gaussian parallax error with an exponentially decreasing space density prior.
- **The HR diagram from Gaia.** Tens of thousands of stars within 100 pc, absolute magnitude against color. The reader brushes the main sequence, the giants and the white dwarfs, then loosens the parallax-quality cut and watches the sequences blur. Computes: absolute magnitude from apparent magnitude and parallax, live, for every star.
- **Leavitt's law.** Three figures. Real OGLE light curves of LMC Cepheids (periods away from whole days, so nightly sampling covers every phase): the reader drags a trial period and the fold snaps into place at a dip in a phase-dispersion periodogram computed in the page, with aliases visible. Then the period-luminosity relation for every fundamental-mode Cepheid in both Magellanic Clouds, in V, I and the Wesenheit index, fitted in the page the way OGLE did and checked against their published fit; the SMC's offset gives its distance relative to the LMC, compared with its eclipsing-binary distance. Finally the reader places Cepheids of a given period and apparent brightness in a distant galaxy and sees the distance's error budget: scatter falling as one over root N in each band, flattening on the LMC anchor's 1%. Computes: the PDM periodogram, the clipped PL fits, the SMC distance, and the distance with its uncertainty.
- **Standardizing Type Ia supernovae and the Hubble diagram.** Pantheon+ supernovae in the SH0ES Hubble-flow sample: residuals against stretch and against colour, with the reader's stretch and colour coefficients (and a least-squares best fit) shrinking the scatter from about 0.26 to 0.13 mag. Then the Hubble diagram with the Cepheid-host calibrators and the Hubble flow, H0 computed live from the calibrators' absolute magnitude and the flow's intercept (second-order luminosity distance), beside published values (SH0ES, a community distance-network combination, CCHP, Planck). The reader shifts every Cepheid distance modulus and watches H0 move, with a button for the shift that would reach Planck. It ends on the Hubble tension, presented as live.
- **The ladder itself.** A log-distance chart with the range each method covers and where the ranges overlap, which is where each rung is calibrated. Computes: the ranges from the data used above, not from a textbook table.

### 3. Lives of Stars
With distances in hand, the HR diagram becomes physics: stars are balls of gas in hydrostatic balance, burning fuel at a rate set by their mass.
- **Hydrostatic balance.** The Lane-Emden equation integrated in the page. The reader varies the polytropic index and sees density, pressure and enclosed-mass profiles grow more centrally concentrated, beside a curve of central-to-mean density against index, with n = 3 (Eddington's standard model) compared to a standard solar model (Bahcall-Serenelli 2005). The readout scales the polytrope to the Sun and gives its central pressure and ideal-gas central temperature against the solar model's. Computes: the Lane-Emden solution by numerical integration, checked against the closed forms at n = 0, 1 and 5.
- **Two ways to burn hydrogen.** Three panels driven by one mass slider, which sets the central temperature and density from the MIST model of that mass halfway through its main sequence: the Gamow peaks of p + p and p + 14N at that temperature, energy generation against temperature on log-log axes for the pp chain and the CNO cycle (Solar Fusion II S-factors, the Sun's central composition), and MIST's own CNO share of hydrogen burning against mass, which passes one half near 1.5 solar masses. Computes: each rate from its Gamow-peak form, the temperature exponents, the crossover, and the two formulas summed over the solar model (about 0.8 of the Sun's luminosity, 0.6% from CNO).
- **Tracks and clusters.** Two figures. MIST evolutionary tracks for eleven masses on the HR diagram; a mass selector and a clock move a star along its track, a bar shows the same life to scale in time, and a second panel plots main-sequence lifetime against mass for every MIST track. Then two Gaia DR3 open clusters with Hunt & Reffert (2023) membership, the Pleiades and M67: the reader slides an isochrone's age, with distance from the members' mean parallax and reddening from the Gaia DR2 HR-diagram paper, beside a fit statistic against age. M67's fit is sharp; the Pleiades' is broad, and its lithium and eclipsing-binary ages are given for contrast. Computes: the isochrone at the chosen age (interpolated between MIST's tabulated ages), the fit statistic at every tabulated age, and each cluster's age from the fit.
- **How stars end.** Initial mass against final core mass from the ends of the MIST tracks, then the white dwarf mass-radius relation from a degenerate electron gas going relativistic, with four white dwarfs of measured mass and radius (Bond et al. 2017). The reader raises the central density and watches the radius shrink toward zero as the mass approaches the Chandrasekhar limit. Computes: the mass-radius curve by integrating hydrostatic balance with the exact degenerate pressure, the limit from it (checked against Chandrasekhar's closed form), and the initial mass at which MIST's cores cross it.

### 4. Finding Other Worlds
Planets are too faint to see next to their stars, so almost every one is found through its effect on the star's light, and each method sees a different slice of the population.
- **Transits.** The limb-darkened disc of HD 209458 with the planet crossing it, beside eight TESS transits stacked on the period and a strip of the whole 28-day sector. Planet radius, impact parameter and orbital period change the dip's depth, shape and duration; the reader fits by dragging or with a fit button that also frees the star's density. The depth gives the planet's radius only because the star's radius comes from article 3's method (temperature from Gaia colour, luminosity from parallax and a bolometric correction), and its mass from the MIST tracks. Computes: the transit model (quadratic limb darkening from published TESS-band tables), the fit, the planet radius, and the stellar density the transit shape implies.
- **Radial velocity.** Two figures. First, a star and planet orbiting their barycenter seen from above, with the Doppler curve for an eccentric orbit; sliders for mass, period, eccentricity, orientation and inclination, presets for 51 Peg b, Jupiter and Earth. Then published Keck velocities of 51 Pegasi: a generalised Lomb-Scargle periodogram with its sidereal-day alias, the fold at a trial period, a Keplerian fit, m sin i for a model stellar mass, and the chance the true mass exceeds the minimum for random orientations, marked with 51 Peg b's true mass measured from the planet's own orbital velocity. Computes: the periodogram and a Levenberg-Marquardt Keplerian fit in the page.
- **Microlensing.** The sky around a lens star with a planet: Einstein ring, caustics, the source's path and its lensed images, beside the magnification curve with the planet's share of it and a source-plane zoom where the planet matters most. Presets for OGLE-2005-BLG-390 (its published mass ratio, separation, closest approach and source size) and a Jupiter mass ratio at high magnification. Computes: the finite-source binary-lens light curve by inverse ray shooting, against the point-lens curve, and the caustics from the critical-curve quartic.
- **Who we find, and who we miss.** The NASA Exoplanet Archive population (measured values only) in period and radius or mass, marked by discovery method, with the Solar System's planets. Toggles draw each method's limit: a Kepler-like transit threshold with the transit probability R*/a on the top axis, a radial-velocity semi-amplitude threshold, and the microlensing lensing zone (near the snow line of small stars). A histogram of Kepler radii with a precision cut shows the radius valley between super-Earths and sub-Neptunes. Computes: the boundaries from the formulas above, drawn over the real catalog.

## What to get right

- Every dataset is fetched from its primary archive by a script kept in the repo, saved under the series' `shared/data/`, trimmed to what the figure needs, and cited on the page: Yale Bright Star Catalogue, NASA's Five Millennium Canon of eclipses, JPL's approximate planetary elements, Gaia DR3 (the Gaia Catalogue of Nearby Stars for the 100 pc sample), OGLE Cepheids, Pantheon+/SH0ES, a standard solar model, MIST tracks and isochrones, a Gaia DR3 cluster membership list, measured white dwarf masses and radii, a Kepler/TESS light curve from MAST, published 51 Peg radial velocities, and the NASA Exoplanet Archive. Nothing is simulated "from the distributions of" a real catalog.
- The equation of time has two sign conventions; pick one and label it. Sidereal and solar days differ by about four minutes; the reader should be able to see where that comes from in figure 1.
- Gaia parallaxes carry a known zero-point offset of a few tens of microarcseconds; apply the published correction (the Lindegren et al. 2021 recipe, which matters at a cluster's parallax of about 1 mas) or say plainly why it doesn't matter at the distances shown. Magnitudes are backwards (brighter is smaller) and colors are differences of magnitudes; say so once, early, and put brighter at the top of every HR diagram.
- The Hubble tension numbers change as new analyses appear, so check the current SH0ES, JWST (CCHP) and Planck values when writing, and present the tension as live, not settled.
- Stellar colors should be drawn from blackbody temperatures (resolved to hex, not CSS variables, since they go through a scale), not a rainbow. On the HR diagram, temperature runs from hot on the left to cool on the right, following convention.
- Facts drafts get wrong: 51 Peg b's discovery was 1995 (Mayor and Queloz), and radial velocity gives m sin i, not m; the transit probability is about R*/a, so most planets never transit; the Chandrasekhar limit is about 1.4 solar masses for a carbon-oxygen dwarf; the Sun makes only about one percent of its energy by the CNO cycle; there are 223 synodic months in a saros, about 18 years 11 days, with the eclipse shifting about a third of the way around the Earth.
- Series colors: observed data in the ink color, models and fits in one accent, uncertainty as a lighter band of that accent, the observer or Earth in one blue, and stars in their blackbody color. Keep those four roles fixed across all four articles.
