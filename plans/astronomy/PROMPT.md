# Astronomy

I want a four-article series on observational astronomy for a physics or STEM undergraduate: someone who knows calculus, mechanics and a little thermodynamics, but has never taken an astronomy course. Each article is long and carries four or five figures, and each figure computes what it shows, from real data wherever the claim is about the real sky.

The through-line is that astronomy learns everything from light arriving at one point on Earth. That light carries four things we can measure: direction, brightness, color (spectrum) and timing. Each article pulls one more thing out of that light, and each depends on the one before. The first article is about direction and time: the geometry of the sky as seen from where the reader stands. The second turns angles and brightness into distance. The third uses those distances to put stars on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram and read their physics and life stories from it. The fourth uses what we now know about stars (their radii, masses and brightness) to find planets orbiting them from tiny dips and wobbles in their light.

By the end the reader should be able to say how each number in an astronomy headline was actually measured, which earlier measurement it rests on, and where its error bars come from.

Orbital mechanics is already covered elsewhere in the gallery: Kepler's hidden symmetry in the Noether series (article 7) and symplectic integration of a Kepler orbit in Simulating on a Lattice. Link to those rather than re-deriving them. Solving Kepler's equation for positions is fine to use without ceremony.

## Articles

### 1. The Sky From Where You Stand
The naked-eye sky as a problem in spherical geometry and clocks.
- **The celestial sphere.** A draggable 3D orthographic sphere with the horizon, celestial equator and ecliptic, plus the bright stars from the Yale Bright Star Catalogue. The reader sets latitude and time of night and watches circumpolar stars stay up while others set. A year slider spanning 26,000 years precesses the pole from Polaris toward Vega. Computes: alt/az from RA/Dec through local sidereal time, and the precession of the equinoxes.
- **The analemma.** The Sun's position at the same clock time each day for a year traces a figure eight. The reader moves obliquity and eccentricity sliders and sees the equation of time split into its two causes, with the figure eight becoming an ellipse or a symmetric eight as each cause goes to zero. Computes: the equation of time from Kepler's equation plus the projection of the ecliptic onto the equator.
- **Retrograde motion.** A heliocentric top view linked to a geocentric strip of Mars's ecliptic longitude over three years, driven by real orbital elements. Scrubbing time shows the loop happen at opposition. A planet selector shows how retrograde duration and loop size change with orbital radius. Computes: apparent longitude from the two heliocentric positions.
- **Why eclipses repeat.** The synodic, draconic and anomalistic months as three clocks that almost line up after 223 synodic months, the saros. A grid of real eclipses from the Five Millennium Canon, arranged by saros series, shows each family drifting and then dying out. Computes: the commensurability residuals, and each eclipse's saros number from its date.
The article ends on what angles alone can't give us: how far away anything is. The one exception, parallax, opens article 2.

### 2. The Distance Ladder
How a telescope that measures only angles and brightness gets to distances of billions of light years, one calibrated rung at a time.
- **Parallax, and why you can't just invert it.** Two figures. First, Earth's orbit from above linked to a real nearby star's path on the sky over three years, from its Gaia DR3 position, parallax and proper motion: stars near the ecliptic pole trace circles, stars near the ecliptic trace lines, and proper motion pulls the loop into a wave (61 Cygni, Bessel's star, for the extreme case). Computes: the parallax offsets from Earth's position through a low-precision solar ephemeris, and the ellipse's axes against parallax times the sine of ecliptic latitude. Second, a cluster whose members share one distance (M67, members picked by position and proper motion only, so parallax errors stay whole): the reader slides from precise to poor parallaxes and watches the histogram of 1/p skew, with the distribution predicted from the catalogue errors drawn over it. For one star, prior times likelihood gives a posterior; sliding the prior's scale shows that for poor parallaxes the prior decides, and that the prior is a claim about the population. Computes: the cluster distance from its bright members, the predicted 1/p distribution, and the posterior under a Gaussian parallax error with an exponentially decreasing space density prior.
- **The HR diagram from Gaia.** Tens of thousands of stars within 100 pc, absolute magnitude against color. The reader brushes the main sequence, the giants and the white dwarfs, then loosens the parallax-quality cut and watches the sequences blur. Computes: absolute magnitude from apparent magnitude and parallax, live, for every star.
- **Leavitt's law.** Three figures. Real OGLE light curves of LMC Cepheids (periods away from whole days, so nightly sampling covers every phase): the reader drags a trial period and the fold snaps into place at a dip in a phase-dispersion periodogram computed in the page, with aliases visible. Then the period-luminosity relation for every fundamental-mode Cepheid in both Magellanic Clouds, in V, I and the Wesenheit index, fitted in the page the way OGLE did and checked against their published fit; the SMC's offset gives its distance relative to the LMC, compared with its eclipsing-binary distance. Finally the reader places Cepheids of a given period and apparent brightness in a distant galaxy and sees the distance's error budget: scatter falling as one over root N in each band, flattening on the LMC anchor's 1%. Computes: the PDM periodogram, the clipped PL fits, the SMC distance, and the distance with its uncertainty.
- **Standardizing Type Ia supernovae and the Hubble diagram.** Pantheon+ supernovae in the SH0ES Hubble-flow sample: residuals against stretch and against colour, with the reader's stretch and colour coefficients (and a least-squares best fit) shrinking the scatter from about 0.26 to 0.13 mag. Then the Hubble diagram with the Cepheid-host calibrators and the Hubble flow, H0 computed live from the calibrators' absolute magnitude and the flow's intercept (second-order luminosity distance), beside published values (SH0ES, a community distance-network combination, CCHP, Planck). The reader shifts every Cepheid distance modulus and watches H0 move, with a button for the shift that would reach Planck. It ends on the Hubble tension, presented as live.
- **The ladder itself.** A log-distance chart with the range each method covers and where the ranges overlap, which is where each rung is calibrated. Computes: the ranges from the data used above, not from a textbook table.

### 3. Lives of Stars
With distances in hand, the HR diagram becomes physics: stars are balls of gas in hydrostatic balance, burning fuel at a rate set by their mass.
- **Hydrostatic balance.** The Lane-Emden equation integrated in the page. The reader varies the polytropic index and sees density and pressure profiles grow more centrally concentrated, with n = 3 (Eddington's standard model) compared to a standard solar model. Computes: the Lane-Emden solution by numerical integration, checked against the closed forms at n = 0, 1 and 5.
- **Two ways to burn hydrogen.** Energy generation rate against central temperature for the pp chain and the CNO cycle on log-log axes. The reader drags a stellar mass, which sets a central temperature, and sees which process dominates and why the crossover sits a little above the Sun's mass. Computes: the temperature dependence of each rate from its Gamow-peak form.
- **Tracks and clusters.** Stellar evolution tracks (MIST) on the HR diagram. A mass slider and a clock animate a star through the main sequence, the giant branch and beyond, with lifetime falling steeply with mass. Then a real Gaia open cluster (such as M67 or the Pleiades) is overlaid, and the reader slides an isochrone's age until its turnoff matches the cluster's. Computes: the isochrone at the chosen age, and the cluster's age from the fit.
- **How stars end.** Initial mass against end state, then the white dwarf mass-radius relation from a degenerate electron gas going relativistic. The reader adds mass and watches the radius shrink to zero at the Chandrasekhar limit. Computes: the mass-radius curve from the degenerate equation of state, and the limit from it.

### 4. Finding Other Worlds
Planets are too faint to see next to their stars, so almost every one is found through its effect on the star's light, and each method sees a different slice of the population.
- **Transits.** A model light curve with limb darkening, where planet radius, impact parameter and orbital period change the dip's depth, shape and duration. The reader fits a real Kepler or TESS light curve by dragging, and the depth gives the planet's radius only because article 3 gave us the star's. Computes: the transit model (quadratic limb darkening), and radius from depth and stellar radius.
- **Radial velocity.** The star and planet orbiting their barycenter, with the Doppler velocity curve for an eccentric orbit. The reader runs a periodogram on real 51 Pegasi radial velocities and finds the 4.2-day signal, then reads off m sin i and sees why it's only a lower bound. Computes: a Lomb-Scargle periodogram and a Keplerian fit in the page.
- **Microlensing.** A foreground star with a planet drifting across a background source. The reader drags the source's path relative to the Einstein ring and sees the smooth magnification curve and the brief planetary spike. Computes: the point-lens magnification, plus a binary-lens caustic crossing for the planet.
- **Who we find, and who we miss.** The NASA Exoplanet Archive population in period and radius (or mass), colored by discovery method. The reader switches on each method's detection boundary (transit probability of about R*/a, the radial-velocity semi-amplitude threshold, the microlensing sweet spot near the snow line) and sees that the empty regions have the shape of the methods. A histogram shows the radius valley between super-Earths and sub-Neptunes. Computes: the boundaries from the formulas above, drawn over the real catalog.

## What to get right

- Every dataset is fetched from its primary archive by a script kept in the repo, saved under the series' `shared/data/`, trimmed to what the figure needs, and cited on the page: Yale Bright Star Catalogue, NASA's Five Millennium Canon of eclipses, JPL's approximate planetary elements, Gaia DR3 (the Gaia Catalogue of Nearby Stars for the 100 pc sample), OGLE Cepheids, Pantheon+/SH0ES, MIST, a Gaia DR3 cluster membership list, a Kepler/TESS light curve from MAST, published 51 Peg radial velocities, and the NASA Exoplanet Archive. Nothing is simulated "from the distributions of" a real catalog.
- The equation of time has two sign conventions; pick one and label it. Sidereal and solar days differ by about four minutes; the reader should be able to see where that comes from in figure 1.
- Gaia parallaxes carry a known zero-point offset of a few tens of microarcseconds; apply the published correction (the Lindegren et al. 2021 recipe, which matters at a cluster's parallax of about 1 mas) or say plainly why it doesn't matter at the distances shown. Magnitudes are backwards (brighter is smaller) and colors are differences of magnitudes; say so once, early, and put brighter at the top of every HR diagram.
- The Hubble tension numbers change as new analyses appear, so check the current SH0ES, JWST (CCHP) and Planck values when writing, and present the tension as live, not settled.
- Stellar colors should be drawn from blackbody temperatures (resolved to hex, not CSS variables, since they go through a scale), not a rainbow. On the HR diagram, temperature runs from hot on the left to cool on the right, following convention.
- Facts drafts get wrong: 51 Peg b's discovery was 1995 (Mayor and Queloz), and radial velocity gives m sin i, not m; the transit probability is about R*/a, so most planets never transit; the Chandrasekhar limit is about 1.4 solar masses for a carbon-oxygen dwarf; the Sun makes only about one percent of its energy by the CNO cycle; there are 223 synodic months in a saros, about 18 years 11 days, with the eclipse shifting about a third of the way around the Earth.
- Series colors: observed data in the ink color, models and fits in one accent, uncertainty as a lighter band of that accent, the observer or Earth in one blue, and stars in their blackbody color. Keep those four roles fixed across all four articles.
