These are verified or partially verified quote anchors for the twelve explainers. The **Tier** column indicates confidence:

- **A** — Project edition or scan checked by the author.
- **B** — Reputable excerpt with consistent source context (Wikiquote with page refs, archived full text, multiple independent secondary citations with consistent wording).
- **C** — Quote aggregator, editorial paraphrase, or secondary article. Useful for locating a passage; wording must be verified against the chosen edition before any page uses exact text.

All fragments should be confirmed against the chosen English edition before final page publication. Translations of Weil vary significantly between Crawford (1951), Rees, Wills, and later Routledge editions.

## Concept Clusters

These clusters group fragments and enrichment passages for figure and caption work. Verify all exact wording against the chosen edition before final page publication.

### Gravity / Grace / Void

Use *Gravity and Grace* to connect `pesanteur`, imagination, void, and grace. The page should paraphrase the surrounding notebook cluster: gravity pulls mechanically; imagination fills voids; grace is not produced by effort.

**Anchor fragments:**
- "All the natural movements of the soul are controlled by laws analogous to those of physical gravity. Grace is the only exception."
- "Grace fills empty spaces, but it can only enter where there is a void to receive it, and it is grace itself which makes this void."

**Enrichment:**
- "The imagination is continually at work filling up all the fissures through which grace might pass." — *Gravity and Grace*
  *Why:* Completes the mechanism — gravity pulls down, imagination pre-empts grace by filling the void it would need. Three forces in the field: gravity, imagination, grace.

### Attention / Waiting / Neighbor

Use the school-studies essay for attention as disciplined emptiness and the Bousquet letter for attention as generosity. The connection to another person should matter as much as the connection to truth.

**Anchor fragments:**
- "Attention consists of suspending our thought, leaving it detached, empty and ready to be penetrated by the object."
- "Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity." (Letter to Bousquet, April 13, 1942)

**Enrichment:**
- "In every school exercise there is a special way of waiting upon truth, setting our hearts upon it, yet not allowing ourselves to go out in search of it." — school-studies essay in *Waiting for God*
  *Why:* Names the waiting movement precisely: the direction is receptive, not acquisitive. Useful for the spotlight figure — the beam waits rather than hunts.
- "The key to a Christian conception of studies is the realization that prayer consists of attention." — school-studies essay
  *Why:* Makes the essay's structural bridge explicit: developing attention through schoolwork IS training for prayer. Shows why the essay title connects school studies to love of God.
- "What are you going through?" — school-studies essay, on attention to the afflicted neighbor (Tier B; verify exact English phrasing against Crawford translation; French near `Quel est ton tourment?`)
  *Why:* The culminating image of neighbor-attention in the essay — the question a person trained in true attention can ask, rather than projecting onto the sufferer.

### Force / War

Use the Iliad essay as the main source. Keep the language sober: force makes persons thing-like, but Weil is not asserting moral equivalence between aggressor and victim.

**Anchor fragments:**
- "The true hero, the true subject, the center of the Iliad is force."
- "To define force — it is that x that turns anybody who is subjected to it into a thing. Exercised to the limit, it turns man into a thing in the most literal sense: it makes a corpse out of him."
- "Force is as pitiless to the man who possesses it, or thinks he does, as it is to its victims; the second it crushes, the first it intoxicates. The truth is, nobody really possesses it."
- "The man who does not wear the armor of the lie cannot experience force without being touched by it to the very soul."

**Enrichment:**
- "Yet what is impossible in logic becomes true in life, and the contradiction lodged within the soul tears it to shreds." (also used in explainer 05)
  *Why:* Shows how force produces lived contradiction: a soul containing both its former power and its current subjugation is torn rather than resolved.

### Oppression / Bureaucracy / Technique

Use *Oppression and Liberty* for social machinery, revolution, technocracy, and means/end reversal. The key bridge is the separation of thought from action.

**Anchor fragments:**
- "…separation between thought and action, the mechanization of thought itself." (*Oppression and Liberty*)
- "The essential evil besetting humanity is the substitution of means for ends." (*Oppression and Liberty*)
- "Nothing in the world can prevent us from thinking clearly." (*Oppression and Liberty*)

**Enrichment:**
- "In a free society means and ends are rightfully in a relation of equilibrium." — *Oppression and Liberty*
  *Why:* Gives the positive counterpart to the means/end reversal: a free society is defined by equilibrium, not domination of one by the other. Useful for the split diagram.

### Work / Factory / Method

Use factory writings for fatigue, humiliation, rhythm, and obedience. Use *The Need for Roots* to preserve the positive side of work: work can be contact with necessity when method and attention remain joined.

**Anchor fragments:**
- "the class of people who do not count in the eyes of anyone" (close paraphrase; Tier C — verify)
- "Physical labour is a daily death." (*The Need for Roots*)

**Enrichment:**
- "It's only on Saturday afternoon and Sunday that a few memories and shreds of ideas return to me, and I remember that I am also a thinking being." — Factory Journal (1934), *La condition ouvrière*
  *Why:* Shows the destruction of attention by factory rhythm directly — consciousness survives only in the margins of the working week. Connects to vocab-connection bridge on factories destroying attention.
- "Slavery has made me entirely lose the feeling of having any rights." — Factory Journal (1934)
  *Why:* Bridge from factory experience → affliction's social dimension → loss of standing as a rights-bearing person. Shows how labor becomes `malheur` when it strips the social layer.

### Liberty / Obligation / Roots

Use *The Need for Roots* for obligation, rootedness, and reconstruction. The final page should not end with hagiography; it should hold the political insight and the reception problem together.

**Anchor fragments:**
- "A right is not effective on its own but solely in relation to the obligation to which it corresponds."
- "A human being has roots by virtue of his real, active, and natural participation in the life of a community which preserves in living shape certain particular treasures of the past and certain particular expectations for the future."

**Enrichment:**
- "To be rooted is perhaps the most important and least recognized need of the human soul." — *The Need for Roots* (opening sentence)
  *Why:* The series-wide framing statement for the finale. Roots are a need, not a sentiment — the same kind of structural claim as necessity itself. Places the finale in dialogue with explainer 01.

### Sign and Referent / The Three Monsters

This is the diagnostic frame for Act II. The "Algebra" chapter of *Gravity and Grace* names a single mechanism — sign cut loose from referent — and shows it operating in three civilizational forms (money, mechanization, algebra). Force, factory, bureaucracy, war, and technique are then five domain-specific instances of the same pathology. The cluster anchors explainer 11 (Technique and Method) but cross-references the entire second half.

**Anchor fragments:**
- "Money, mechanization, algebra. The three monsters of contemporary civilization." — *Gravity and Grace*, "Algebra" chapter
- "the relation of the sign to the thing signified is being destroyed, the game of exchanges between signs is being multiplied of itself and for itself." — *Gravity and Grace*, "Algebra" chapter
- "collective thought cannot exist as thought; it passes into things (signs, machines…), hence the paradox: it is the thing which thinks and the man who is reduced to the state of a thing." — *Gravity and Grace*, "Algebra" chapter

**Enrichment / cross-thinker context:**
- Weil treats geometry and algebra as morally opposed: "geometry leads from ruthless passion to temperate perception in accord with the human condition; algebra leads in the reverse direction, to excess and oppression" (paraphrasing Heifetz, *Global Philosophy*, 2022, on the *Algebra* chapter).
- The Pythagorean line Weil gestures at: "None enters here unless he is a geometer." Geometry is for Weil a `metaxu` — a sign that retains contact with form. Algebra is the false interpretive frame.
- Weil's critique was provoked specifically by Bourbaki, the formalist program her brother André co-founded. Her 1940 letters to him questioned whether his rarefied algebraic geometry retained any contact with lived reality. The triangle (Noether / André Weil / Simone) is a sibling argument as well as a convergence.

**How it threads through Act II:**

| Explainer | Sign | Severed referent |
|---|---|---|
| 07 Force | "thing" | the person |
| 08 Factory | the standardized motion + the wage | the worker's thought, body, and labor |
| 09 Bureaucracy | the rule, the form, the policy variable | the people governed |
| 10 War | victory, prestige, body count | the human cost |
| 11 Technique & Method | the means | the end |

**Source-caution:** Weil's algebra-critique is sometimes weaponized by anti-modern conservatives into a wholesale rejection of formal reasoning. The series should make clear: her critique is specific (sign cut from referent), not Luddite. She admired Greek geometry, Pythagorean math, and the parts of formal reasoning that remained in contact with their objects. Flag this in the explainer 11 source card.

## Translation note

Most widely cited passages come from Emma Craufurd's 1951 translation of *Waiting for God* and Arthur Wills's translation of *Gravity and Grace*. The Routledge Classics editions (2002–) are currently the most accessible print editions. The Anarchist Library hosts public-domain-era texts. Where passages appear in multiple Tier B sources with identical wording, that wording is sufficiently stable for page use — but always note the translator in the source card.
