# Source Map

## Primary Works And Where They Serve The Series

### *Gravity and Grace* / *La pesanteur et la grace*

Use for gravity, grace, void, decreation, contradiction, detachment, imagination, beauty, metaxu, the social imprint, and the Great Beast.

Edition caution: posthumous topical selection from notebooks arranged by Gustave Thibon. Do not present it as a book Weil designed.

Working web anchors:

- Wikiquote, *Gravity and Grace*: https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Gravity_and_Grace
- Routledge edition anchor: https://www.routledge.com/Gravity-and-Grace/Weil/p/book/9780415290005

### *Waiting for God* / *Attente de Dieu*

Use for attention, waiting, school studies, prayer, obedience, neighbor-love, affliction, and consent.

Edition caution: posthumous collection of letters and essays. Separate the school-studies essay, letters to Perrin, and reflections on affliction when citing. Most widely cited English translation is Emma Craufurd (1951), still the standard for "The Love of God and Affliction" and "Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies."

Key essays and their primary quotes:
- "Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies with a View to the Love of God" — attention as suspended thought, waiting upon truth, "What are you going through?", attention as substance of prayer.
- "The Love of God and Affliction" — definitional passage on malheur: "There is not real affliction unless the event that has seized and uprooted a life attacks it, directly or indirectly, in all its parts, social, psychological, and physical." Also: "Affliction takes possession of the soul and marks it through and through with its own particular mark, the mark of slavery."

### Letter To Joë Bousquet / Correspondence

Use for attention as generosity. This is a concise source for attention as a relation to another person, not only an intellectual practice.

Exact quote confirmed: "Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity." French: "L'attention est la forme la plus rare et la plus pure de la générosité." — Letter to Joë Bousquet, April 13, 1942. Published in *Correspondance* (Lausanne: Editions l'Age d'Homme, 1982), p. 18. Also appears in *Seventy Letters* (Oxford University Press, trans. Richard Rees).

Working web anchor:

- Quotepark source note for the Bousquet letter: https://quotepark.com/quotes/1536018-simone-weil-attention-is-the-rarest-and-purest-form-of-generos/
- WIST Quotations, Bousquet letter: https://wist.info/weil-simone/41284/

### "The Iliad, or the Poem of Force"

Use for force, war, thingification, victory, prestige, and the deformation of both victims and agents. Also the source for the contradiction (explainer 05) passage about life making logically impossible states real.

Edition caution: English translation wording varies. The essay is outside the two organizing titles but required for Act II. The Mary McCarthy translation (1945) is the most widely cited; the James Holoka critical edition (Peter Lang, 2003) includes the French facing text. *War and the Iliad* (NYRB Classics, 2005) pairs it with Rachel Bespaloff.

Key passages confirmed:
- "The true hero, the true subject, the center of the Iliad is force." (opening)
- "To define force — it is that x that turns anybody who is subjected to it into a thing. Exercised to the limit, it turns man into a thing in the most literal sense: it makes a corpse out of him."
- "Force is as pitiless to the man who possesses it, or thinks he does, as it is to its victims; the second it crushes, the first it intoxicates. The truth is, nobody really possesses it."
- "The man who does not wear the armor of the lie cannot experience force without being touched by it to the very soul."
- "Yet what is impossible in logic becomes true in life, and the contradiction lodged within the soul tears it to shreds."

### *Oppression and Liberty*

Use for oppression, social machinery, revolution, bureaucracy, technocracy, power, means/end reversal, and liberty as thought joined to action.

Edition caution: posthumous political collection, centered on "Reflections Concerning the Causes of Liberty and Social Oppression." Translated by Arthur Wills and John Petrie (Routledge, 1958 / Routledge Classics, 2001).

Key passages confirmed:
- "The essential evil besetting humanity is the substitution of means for ends." (*Reflections Concerning the Causes of Liberty and Social Oppression*)
- "…bureaucratic organization, reversal of the relationship between means and ends, contempt for the individual, separation between thought and action, the mechanization of thought itself." (ibid.)
- "Nothing in the world can prevent us from thinking clearly." (ibid.)

### Factory Writings / *La condition ouvrière*

Use for factory labor, rhythm, humiliation, obedience, fatigue, separation of thought and action, and the bridge to `malheur`.

Edition caution: use direct factory writings rather than biographical summaries when building pages. The factory journal was written during Weil's year as an unskilled worker at the Alsthom factory near Paris (1934–35). *La condition ouvrière* (Gallimard, 1951) collects the journal and related essays; Camus selected it for the Gallimard imprint. No standard complete English translation exists; Bernard Harcourt and others have translated excerpts.

Key passages confirmed:
- "It's only on Saturday afternoon and Sunday that a few memories and shreds of ideas return to me, and I remember that I am also a thinking being." (Factory Journal, 1934)
- "Slavery has made me entirely lose the feeling of having any rights." (Factory Journal)
- The "do not count in the eyes of anyone" passage is present in the factory writings as a description of the worker's social non-existence, but exact English wording depends on translator; treat as Tier C and verify against chosen edition.

### *The Need for Roots* / *L'enracinement*

Use for liberty, obligation, rootedness, uprootedness, work, needs of the soul, reconstruction, truth, and anti-hagiographic caution.

Edition caution: late wartime political writing, posthumously published; do not reduce it to inspirational rootedness. Multiple English translations exist. The Arthur Wills translation (Routledge/Penguin, 1952; intro T.S. Eliot) is widely cited; a newer translation by Ros Schwartz (Penguin, 2022) updates the language significantly.

Key passages confirmed:
- "To be rooted is perhaps the most important and least recognized need of the human soul." (opening)
- "A human being has roots by virtue of his real, active, and natural participation in the life of a community which preserves in living shape certain particular treasures of the past and certain particular expectations for the future." (opening definition of rootedness)
- "A right is not effective on its own but solely in relation to the obligation to which it corresponds."
- "Physical labour is a daily death." (also used for the factory explainer)
