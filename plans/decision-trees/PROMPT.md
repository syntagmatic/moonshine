# Decision Trees

I want six chapters on one of the oldest and most useful models in machine learning, for technically literate readers who know basic probability and ML vocabulary. A single tree is a flowchart of yes-or-no questions and is easy to read. Practical tree models combine hundreds or thousands of them, which predicts better and is much harder to read. The series builds both, with figures where the reader drags thresholds and adds trees by hand.

Two moves recur throughout and hold it together. The first is the split: at each node, pick the question that separates the data best, which means trees care about rankings and ignore distances. The second is the ensemble: one tree is unstable and many are stable, whether averaged (bagging, random forests) or added so each corrects the last (boosting). The last two chapters recover what the ensemble costs in readability and then turn the same machinery toward a causal question: where does an intervention change the outcome.

## Articles

### 1. The Single Tree
How a tree scores a split, and why growing one greedily works until it doesn't. The reader drags a split line through a strip of points and watches the Gini, entropy and misclassification curves, then sees the gain at every candidate threshold at once. A family of splits that all make the same number of mistakes shows why misclassification error is the wrong score: it stays flat while Gini and entropy reward the purer split. A regression tree grows a staircase fit that cannot extrapolate, with training error falling while error on fresh data turns back up. A tree grown level by level on XOR is set against the best depth-2 tree found by exhaustive search, first on one sample and then across many, where greedy growth reliably stalls.

### 2. Geometry and Pruning
Trees cut the plane with axis-aligned lines, which makes them blind to monotone rescaling and weak on diagonals. The reader rescales a feature and sees the tree's leaves and predictions stay put while a nearest-neighbour classifier's change. Rotating the true boundary from vertical through 45 degrees shows the leaf count and error climbing, against a single oblique cut; a search over every direction shows how much gain an oblique split can find that axis splits cannot. Cost-complexity pruning is walked one weakest-link step at a time, and the pruning parameter is then chosen by cross-validation, with the lowest-error and one-standard-error rules compared across fold shuffles.

### 3. Voting Ensembles
Averaging trees cancels their jitter, down to a floor set by how correlated they are. The reader draws a bootstrap sample and checks the left-out share against 1/e, then simulates the variance of an average of correlated predictors against the textbook formula. A forest of faint individual trees and their thick average responds to the number of trees, features per split and threshold rule, and a companion figure measures the correlation, spread and bias directly for each setting, showing how random forests and Extra-Trees buy lower correlation. A depth sweep shows why bagged trees should be grown deep, and out-of-bag error is checked against a test set across many training sets.

### 4. Boosting
Boosting trains trees one at a time, each fit to the residuals of the ensemble so far. The reader adds trees by hand on a 1D regression problem, seeing the prediction close in on the true function beside the residuals and a preview of the next tree. A learning-rate sweep shows test error climbing after the first few trees when the rate is 1, level-wise and leaf-wise growth are compared on the same residuals, and bias and variance are tracked against the number of trees for boosting beside bagging. Swapping squared, absolute and Huber loss on data with outliers shows boosting as gradient descent in function space with any differentiable loss. It briefly describes the design choices of modern implementations.

### 5. Reading the Forest
Two ways to read a model of many trees. First, split-based feature importance, and the ID-column trap: the reader raises the sample size and watches a meaningless unique identifier win under raw information gain and shrink under gain ratio, to about 1/log2 N: level with the one informative feature, still ahead of the weak ones, sinking only slowly as N grows. A forest grown in the page then sets impurity importance against permutation importance and shows a pure-noise continuous feature overtaking a real one as trees deepen. Second, Shapley values, which split one prediction among its features: a waterfall of contributions that always sum exactly, the lattice of all feature subsets whose weighted marginal contributions make each bar, and what "absent" means inside a tree, which is the rule TreeSHAP computes. It ends on what SHAP does not tell you.

### 6. Causal Trees
Trees that look for where a treatment changes the outcome, as opposed to where the outcome is high. On a promo-code example the reader toggles the tree between predicting purchase and maximizing uplift and sees it find different regions, then scores every possible cut under the uplift criterion with and without its noise penalty, including on shuffled data. On data with no heterogeneity at all, the winner's curse inflates the chosen leaf's same-sample effect as more leaves are compared. 200 simulated studies show the double-dipping problem: a leaf's effect estimated on the data that chose it is badly biased with poor interval coverage, while honest estimation on a held-out half is close to unbiased. Honest trees averaged into a causal forest recover the effect map better than one tree, and a confounded-assignment figure shows a pooled comparison going wrong where a stratified one does not.

## What to get right

- Everything is simulated from seeded synthetic data, and the figures must actually compute what the captions say: the greedy-versus-best depth-2 comparison, the correlated-variance check, the honest-estimation bias and coverage. Pick a representative default sample, never a lucky one.
- The Shapley figure is exact Shapley values by subset enumeration on a small hand-written model with a stated baseline. If it is not TreeSHAP on a trained forest, the caption must say so.
- Causal trees reward heterogeneity of effects across leaves, with a penalty for noisy leaves; they do not maximize within-leaf variance. They also work beyond randomized experiments under unconfoundedness (generalized random forests), so do not claim randomization is required.
- Extra-Trees' default feature count is the same as a random forest's, not one; a single random feature is "totally randomized trees". "Boosting beats bagging on tabular data because of bias" is folk wisdom; flag it as such if used. Do not state vendor speedups or benchmark win counts without a source.
- Default seeds are checked against a spread of seeds in node and chosen near the median; a seed that makes the figure's point unusually well is a lucky seed.
- Keep a fixed color language across chapters: the two classes, each impurity measure, the split, truth versus prediction.
