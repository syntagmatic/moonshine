# The Classification of Simple Lie Algebras

Cartan–Killing's theorem, one Dynkin diagram at a time. Fifteen interactive explainers that walk from the definition of a Lie algebra to the full classification tree — four infinite classical families A, B, C, D plus the five exceptions E₆, E₇, E₈, F₄, G₂ — and explain *why* those are exactly the simple Lie algebras that exist and nothing else.

## Locked plan

**Spine.** The classification of simple Lie algebras over ℂ is one of the great theorems of 20th-century mathematics: Killing (1888–90), Cartan (1894, 1914), and finally Dynkin's clean diagrammatic reformulation (1947) give a complete list of all finite-dimensional simple Lie algebras — four infinite families and five exceptions. This series tells the classification story as a three-act pedagogical arc: first build the machinery (Lie algebra → Cartan subalgebra → root system → Weyl group), then use the four classical families as anchors before proving the classification's termination, then tour the exceptions one at a time. The `e8-lattice` series already tells the reader the punchline about E₈; this series tells them the rest of the tree and explains why E₈ sits at the top of it.

**Reader endpoint.** After completing this series, a reader should be able to: (a) state the definition of a Lie algebra and give two non-trivial examples; (b) explain why a Cartan subalgebra decomposes the algebra into root spaces; (c) read a Dynkin diagram and name the corresponding algebra; (d) sketch why the classical families A, B, C, D correspond to matrix Lie algebras; (e) name all five exceptional Lie algebras and one distinguishing fact about each; (f) explain at a high level why the classification terminates — why there is no E₉.

**Identity.** Slug `lie-algebras`, title *The Classification of Simple Lie Algebras*, tagline *"Cartan–Killing's theorem, one Dynkin diagram at a time."* Voice is first-person-plural and deliberately classifier-in-a-museum: we are walking the reader past specimens in a carefully curated collection, not building up a research program. Visual register matches `e8-lattice`: 740px article width, serif body, sans-serif headings, interactive SVG figures with KaTeX math overlay.

**Shape.** Three acts, five explainers each.

- **Act I: Machinery** (5 explainers). What is a Lie algebra? How does a Cartan subalgebra slice it into root spaces? What is a root system as an abstract object, independent of any particular Lie algebra? What is a Weyl group, and why is it the finite symmetry group acting on the root system? By the end of Act I the reader has the complete set of tools the classification uses — everything that follows is applying those tools, not inventing new ones.
- **Act II: The Classification** (5 explainers). The crystallographic restriction on root-system angles, the Cartan-matrix / Dynkin-diagram encoding, the four classical families as the first worked examples, then the graph-pruning + spherical-Diophantine argument that produces the three exceptional branching solutions. By the end of Act II, the classification tree is on the board and the reader knows it is both complete and restrictive.
- **Act III: The Exceptions** (5 explainers). Each of the five exceptional Lie algebras gets its own chapter: G₂ and the octonions; F₄ and the exceptional Jordan algebra; E₆ and the Cayley plane; E₇ and E₈ with the end of the E-series; and a capstone that uses the Freudenthal magic square to unify all five exceptions through the division-algebra ladder ℝ, ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆. The capstone also carries a short "what lies beyond" sidebar on affine Kac-Moody algebras as a pointer to the `monstrous-moonshine` series.

**Per-explainer shape.** 500–800 lines, in the same moonshine-series format used by `e8-lattice`: historical framing, formal content with KaTeX, at least one interactive figure per explainer, insight boxes, closing reflection. Figures lean heavily on live-rendered Dynkin diagrams, root-system projections (reusing `e8-math.js` where applicable for the E-type exceptions), Weyl-group orbit animations, and Cartan-matrix inspectors.

**Rendering stack.** D3 v7 + SVG + KaTeX. A shared `lib/lie-math.js` holds: root-system generators for all finite Coxeter types, Dynkin diagram layout helpers, Cartan matrix builders, Weyl group orbit enumerators, and a small table of structural constants (dimension, rank, Coxeter number, order of Weyl group) for each algebra. On E₈-specific pages (parts of #14 and #15) we additionally load `../e8-lattice/lib/e8-math.js` as a second `<script>` tag rather than duplicating code; `lie-math.js` is authoritative for everything except E₈-specific helpers, for which `e8-math.js` is the canonical source.

### The "H₀ ascent" pedagogical framing

The research pass frames the classification as the **H₀ ascent** — a combinatorial algorithm that adds one node at a time to a Dynkin diagram, with positive-definiteness of the Cartan matrix as the termination constraint. Act I builds the algebraic machinery that grounds each step of the ascent in linear algebra; Act II runs the ascent to its termination, using the classical families as worked examples before proving that only three more branching solutions (E₆, E₇, E₈) can exist; Act III visits each of the five exceptions in turn and closes with the Freudenthal magic square. This single thread is what lets the classification story be told in 15 pedagogical steps rather than a semester-long course.

## Explainer list

### Act I · Machinery

**01. What Is a Lie Algebra?** A Lie algebra 𝔤 over ℂ is a vector space equipped with a non-associative, alternating bilinear "bracket" [·,·] satisfying anti-commutativity ([x,y] = -[y,x]) and the Jacobi identity ([x,[y,z]] + [y,[z,x]] + [z,[x,y]] = 0). Canonical examples: ℝ³ with the cross product (= so(3), the infinitesimal rotations of 3-space); n×n trace-zero complex matrices under the commutator [X,Y] = XY − YX (= sl_n); smooth vector fields on a manifold under their Lie bracket. An ideal 𝔦 ⊂ 𝔤 is a subspace closed under bracketing with the whole algebra ([𝔤, 𝔦] ⊂ 𝔦); a Lie algebra is *simple* if it is non-abelian and contains no non-trivial ideals. Simple Lie algebras are the indivisible atomic units of continuous symmetry. The classification theorem relies heavily on the *adjoint representation* ad_x(y) = [x, y], which turns each element x into a linear operator on 𝔤 itself; eigenvalue analysis of these adjoint operators drives everything that follows. **Figure:** live bracket computation on sl₂ (pick two matrices, see [X,Y]); a visualisation of so(3) ≅ ℝ³ under the cross product, where the reader picks two vectors and watches their cross-product as a rotation generator.

**02. The Cartan Subalgebra.** A Cartan subalgebra 𝔥 ⊂ 𝔤 is a maximal toral subalgebra — a maximal subspace whose elements are all simultaneously diagonalizable under the adjoint representation. Over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero (such as ℂ), every semisimple Lie algebra has a Cartan subalgebra, and all Cartan subalgebras are conjugate under the inner automorphism group. The crucial property for the classification is that 𝔥 is *abelian*: all its elements commute, so their adjoint operators {ad_x : x ∈ 𝔥} form a commuting family of diagonalizable linear operators on 𝔤. A standard theorem of linear algebra says that any commuting family of diagonalizable operators can be *simultaneously* diagonalized — and this simultaneous diagonalization is what lets us decompose the whole algebra 𝔤 into eigenspaces. **Figure:** sl₃ with its 2D Cartan subalgebra highlighted inside the 8-dimensional algebra, and a rotatable 2D projection of the roots the Cartan defines.

**03. Roots and the Root Space Decomposition.** Simultaneous diagonalization of {ad_x : x ∈ 𝔥} yields the root space decomposition: 𝔤 = 𝔥 ⊕ ⊕_{α ∈ Δ} 𝔤_α, where each α is a non-zero linear functional in the dual space 𝔥* (it maps a Cartan element to its eigenvalue), and 𝔤_α = {z ∈ 𝔤 : [x,z] = α(x)·z for all x ∈ 𝔥} is the corresponding root space. The non-zero α for which 𝔤_α is non-empty are called the *roots*, and the finite set Δ of all roots is the *root system*. In a simple Lie algebra, every root space 𝔤_α is strictly 1-dimensional — so each root identifies a single vector direction in the algebra. This reduces the entire problem of classifying simple Lie algebras to the problem of classifying which sets of linear functionals (roots) are possible. **Figure:** sl₃ decomposition with live eigenvalue display — click a Cartan element and watch the 6 root spaces pop out as eigenvectors of the adjoint action.

**04. The Cartan–Killing Form and the Shift to Euclidean Geometry.** The Killing form κ(x,y) = tr(ad_x ∘ ad_y) is a bilinear form on 𝔤 that evaluates how elements interact via the trace of their composed adjoint operators. **Cartan's criterion** (which we take as a black-box theorem, since a full proof requires substantial additional machinery): a Lie algebra is semisimple if and only if its Killing form is non-degenerate. Restricted to the Cartan subalgebra 𝔥, κ remains non-degenerate, inducing a canonical isomorphism 𝔥 ≅ 𝔥* and therefore an inner product on the space of roots. Critically: on the real span of the roots, this induced inner product is *strictly positive definite*. This is the pivot of the whole classification — it translates the abstract, non-associative algebraic problem into a rigid finite spatial problem in a standard real Euclidean space 𝔼. From this point forward the classification is a problem in Euclidean geometry, not in algebra. **Figure:** live Killing-form computation on sl₂ and sl₃, showing the induced inner product on the root space and verifying positive-definiteness by displaying the Gram matrix's eigenvalues.

**05. sl₂ Subalgebras and Weyl Reflections.** For every root α ∈ Δ, the negative functional -α is also a root (the roots come in pairs). Picking non-zero elements e_α ∈ 𝔤_α and f_α ∈ 𝔤_{-α}, their bracket [e_α, f_α] = h_α lies in 𝔥. Suitably scaled, the triple (e_α, f_α, h_α) satisfies the commutation relations of sl₂(ℂ) exactly: [h_α, e_α] = 2e_α, [h_α, f_α] = -2f_α, [e_α, f_α] = h_α. So every root produces an embedded sl₂ inside 𝔤. Here we take the **representation theory of sl₂ as a black box**: the concrete fact we use is that the eigenvalues of h_α on any finite-dimensional sl₂-module are integers, spaced by 2, and symmetric about zero. This forces the Cartan integers n_{β,α} = 2⟨β,α⟩/⟨α,α⟩ to be integers, and therefore forces the reflection of β across the hyperplane orthogonal to α, s_α(β) = β - n_{β,α}·α, to be another element of Δ. The group generated by all such reflections is the *Weyl group* W, a finite isometry group of 𝔼 that permutes the roots. **Figure:** Weyl-group orbit animator on the A₂ (hexagonal) and B₂ (square + diagonals) root systems; click a root and watch its orbit under the full Weyl group.

### Act II · The Classification

**06. The Crystallographic Restriction: Quantized Angles.** The Weyl-reflection formula requires the Cartan integer n_{β,α} = 2⟨β,α⟩/⟨α,α⟩ ∈ ℤ for every pair of roots. Multiplying the two symmetric Cartan integers gives n_{α,β} · n_{β,α} = 4cos²θ, which must be a non-negative integer. Since cos²θ ∈ [0,1], the product 4cos²θ is strictly in {0, 1, 2, 3, 4}. For linearly independent pairs of *simple* roots (where the mutual inner product is required to be non-positive to ensure obtuse or right angles), the product is strictly < 4, restricting θ to exactly 90° (0 edges), 120° (1 edge, equal lengths), 135° (2 edges, length ratio √2), or 150° (3 edges, length ratio √3). These are the *only* angles allowed between simple roots. This crystallographic restriction is the reason simple Lie algebras are so tightly constrained: any other angle would break integrality and force an infinite cascade of roots. **Figure:** angle picker with a slider over θ ∈ [90°, 180°], live-displaying the Cartan integers n_{α,β}·n_{β,α} and highlighting the four legal configurations in green; everything else in red with a "non-integer" warning.

**07. Simple Roots, Cartan Matrices, and Dynkin Diagrams.** Choose an orientation: partition Δ = Δ⁺ ⊔ Δ⁻ into positive and negative roots, and find a set Π = {α₁, …, α_n} of *simple roots* such that every positive root is a non-negative integer combination of simple roots. This Π is a basis for 𝔥* and has n elements where n is the *rank* of the algebra. The full geometric structure is then encoded in the n×n Cartan matrix A_{ij} = n_{α_i, α_j}, an integer matrix whose diagonal entries are 2 and whose off-diagonal entries are in {0, -1, -2, -3}. Because a simple Lie algebra has an *irreducible* root system (not decomposable into orthogonal subsystems), the Cartan matrix cannot be block-diagonal. Eugene Dynkin's graphical encoding: one node per simple root, edges between nodes i and j weighted by A_{ij}A_{ji} ∈ {0,1,2,3} (0 meaning no edge), with an arrow from long-root node to short-root node when the multiplicity is 2 or 3. The classification problem becomes: which connected Dynkin diagrams correspond to positive-definite Cartan matrices? This is the densest chapter of Act II — the reader absorbs positive/negative splitting, simple roots, Cartan matrices, and Dynkin diagrams in one shot, so the interactive figure does most of the lifting. **Figure:** a reader-drawn root system (pick simple roots in 2D with sliders for angle and length) that live-generates the corresponding Cartan matrix and Dynkin diagram; pre-loaded examples for sl₃ → A₂, so(5) → B₂, sp(4) → C₂, and G₂.

**08. The Four Classical Families: A, B, C, D.** Before proving that the Dynkin machinery restricts which diagrams are possible, we instantiate it on the four matrix Lie algebra families we already know. Each has a natural Cartan subalgebra (the diagonal matrices), a natural root system (differences of diagonal entries), and a characteristic Dynkin-diagram shape:

- **A_n** (n ≥ 1): simply-laced linear chain of n nodes. Lie algebra sl_{n+1}(ℂ) — the special linear algebra of (n+1)×(n+1) trace-zero complex matrices. Compact real form SU(n+1), the group most intimately connected with quantum mechanics and the Standard Model.
- **B_n** (n ≥ 2): linear chain ending in a double edge with arrow from long to short. Lie algebra so_{2n+1}(ℂ) — the odd-dimensional special orthogonal algebra. Compact form SO(2n+1). Starts at n=2 because B₁ ≅ A₁ (so_3 ≅ sl_2).
- **C_n** (n ≥ 3): linear chain ending in a double edge with arrow reversed. Lie algebra sp_{2n}(ℂ) — the symplectic algebra. Compact form Sp(n). Starts at n=3 because C₁ ≅ A₁ and C₂ ≅ B₂.
- **D_n** (n ≥ 4): branched simply-laced tree with two short arms at one end. Lie algebra so_{2n}(ℂ) — the even-dimensional special orthogonal algebra. Compact form SO(2n). Starts at n=4 because D₁ is abelian, D₂ ≅ A₁ ⊕ A₁ disconnects, and D₃ ≅ A₃.

These four shapes — simple linear chains, chains with one double edge at an end, and a tree with a single short branch — are the raw material for the termination proof in #09 and #10. They also give the reader a concrete library of examples to anchor the abstract pruning argument. **Figure:** Dynkin-diagram browser with a rank slider, live-rendering the four classical diagrams and their matrix realisations; hovering a node shows the corresponding matrix generator and the root it represents.

**09. Graph Pruning: Trees, Loops, and Valency Bounds.** With the classical families in hand, we ask: can any other connected diagrams give simple Lie algebras? The classification proceeds by two structural constraints that eliminate most candidate diagrams. First, *no loops*: an admissible diagram must be a tree. Proof sketch: let v = Σ_{i=1}^n v_i be the sum of the (normalized) simple-root vectors; since v ≠ 0, (v,v) > 0. Expanding gives n + Σ_{i≠j} (v_i, v_j) > 0, and each edge contributes 2(v_i, v_j) ≤ -1. Combining: (number of edges) < n, which forces a tree structure on any connected admissible diagram. Second, *valency ≤ 3*: a single node can have at most 3 edges incident to it (counting multiplicities). Proof sketch: projecting a central node v onto k orthogonal neighbours u_1, …, u_k gives Σ(v,u_i)² < (v,v) = 1, so (total edge count at v)/4 < 1, i.e. the total is ≤ 3. These two bounds eliminate cycles, stars with four or more arms, and dense configurations — leaving only trees with linear chains, at most one branch-point of degree 3, and at most one multiple edge (double or triple). **Figure:** candidate-diagram sandbox where the reader draws arbitrary graphs by clicking to place nodes and drag-to-connect edges; the system reports in real-time which of the two pruning constraints the current diagram violates and highlights the failing element in red.

**10. The Spherical Diophantine Threshold.** After #09's pruning, what remains is a short list of shape candidates: simply-laced linear chains (any length → A_n); simply-laced chains with one branch-point of valency 3 (→ D_n and the E-series); chains with a double edge at one end (→ B_n, C_n); chains with a double edge in the middle (→ F₄ only if short enough); and one isolated triple edge (→ G₂, with the valency bound saturated on both endpoints). The final step is to determine how long each chain can be before the Cartan matrix loses positive-definiteness.

For simply-laced branched trees, the three arms emanating from the branch node have lengths (p, q, r) counting from the terminal to the branch node inclusive, ordered p ≥ q ≥ r ≥ 2. A vector-norm calculation on the node unit vectors gives the **spherical Diophantine inequality**

1/p + 1/q + 1/r > 1,

equivalent to the spherical-triangle angle sum on S² exceeding the Euclidean value. Solving:

- **q = 2**: gives 1/p > 0, holding for all p ≥ 2 — this is the infinite **D_n family** we already met in #08.
- **q = 3**: gives 1/p > 1/6, so p ∈ {3, 4, 5} — three exceptional branching solutions **(3,3,2) → E₆**, **(4,3,2) → E₇**, **(5,3,2) → E₈**.
- **q ≥ 4**: gives nothing.

The F₄ and G₂ cases reduce to short finite checks for the non-simply-laced shapes, each producing exactly one solution. Every other candidate is eliminated. **The classification is now complete**: A_n, B_n, C_n, D_n (four infinite families from #08), G₂, F₄ (the two non-simply-laced outliers), and E₆, E₇, E₈ (the three simply-laced branching solutions). The E-series terminates at E₈ because a hypothetical (6,3,2) would give 1/6 + 1/3 + 1/2 = **exactly** 1 — not strictly greater — so the Cartan matrix becomes only positive *semi*-definite and the algebra fails to be finite-dimensional. E₈ is therefore the topological ceiling of simple Lie algebras. **Figure:** live Diophantine solver with three sliders for the arm lengths (p, q, r); the screen shows 1/p + 1/q + 1/r as a bar with the threshold at 1 drawn in red, and every valid integer triple is highlighted and coloured by the Lie algebra it produces (D_n blue, E₆ purple, E₇ pink, E₈ gold, E₉ red with a "forbidden" cross).

### Act III · The Exceptions

**11. G₂ and the Octonions.** G₂ is the smallest exception — rank 2, dimension 14, with a **triple edge** between its two Dynkin nodes. The triple edge exhausts the valency bound on both nodes immediately, so no further nodes can be attached, giving a truly isolated diagram. Its Cartan matrix is [[2, -1], [-3, 2]], off-diagonal entries -1 and -3. The root system has 12 roots in the plane, six short and six long, at a length ratio √3. G₂ is realised as the **derivation algebra of the octonions** Der(𝕆) — a connection Cartan himself highlighted in his 1894 thesis, and the first rigorous appearance of the octonions inside the Cartan-Killing framework. The smallest non-trivial representation is the 7-dimensional Im 𝕆, on which G₂ acts as the group of automorphisms preserving the octonion product. G₂ also shows up in differential geometry as the holonomy group of certain 7-manifolds (the G₂-manifolds), and physically as a proposed structure group for M-theory compactifications. **Figure:** the G₂ root system in the plane with all 12 roots labelled, a Weyl-group-orbit animator (order 12 = the dihedral group of the hexagram), and a Fano-plane cross-reference panel tying each long root to a triple of imaginary octonion units — bridging to the `octonions` series.

**12. F₄ and the Exceptional Jordan Algebra.** F₄ is a rank-4 algebra of dimension 52, distinguished by a **double edge in the middle** of its linear Dynkin diagram — a chain of four nodes where the second-to-third edge is doubled. The 48 roots split 24-24 into short and long, and the 24 long roots together form the vertices of the regular 4D polytope known as the **24-cell**, the only regular 4-polytope with no analogue in any other dimension. F₄ is realised as the **derivation algebra of the exceptional Jordan algebra h₃(𝕆)** — the 27-dimensional non-associative algebra of 3×3 Hermitian octonionic matrices, one of the deepest bridges between Lie theory and non-associative algebra and the setting for the E₆, E₇, E₈ constructions to come. **Figure:** an interactive 24-cell viewer (4D polytope rendered via a 3D projection the reader can rotate); a second panel shows the F₄ Dynkin diagram with its distinguishing double-edge arrow and the Cartan matrix live-displayed.

**13. E₆ and the Cayley Plane.** The (3,3,2) simply-laced Diophantine solution is **E₆**: a simply-laced branched diagram with six nodes — a linear chain of five plus a single extra node attached at the middle. Rank 6, dimension 78, with 72 roots. E₆ is realised as the isometry group of the **octonionic projective plane 𝕆P² (the Cayley plane)** — the largest projective plane that can be constructed with octonion coordinates, and an exceptional structure in its own right. The E₆ root system has a beautiful connection to the **27 lines on a smooth cubic surface**: those 27 lines are permuted by the Weyl group of E₆, which is sometimes called the "group of the 27 lines." E₆ also appears in grand unified theories of particle physics as a candidate gauge group that properly contains the Standard Model group SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1). **Figure:** a 27-lines visualisation on a model cubic surface, with the Weyl group of E₆ acting by permutation on the lines; the reader can click a line to see its full orbit, and a side panel shows the E₆ Dynkin diagram with the corresponding branch highlighted.

**14. E₇, E₈, and the End of the E-Series.** The last two simply-laced branching solutions are E₇ and E₈. The **(4,3,2) solution gives E₇**: rank 7, dimension 133, with 126 roots. E₇ is the isometry group of a quaternionic projective plane (via a variant of the Cayley plane construction) and is the second-largest exceptional algebra. The **(5,3,2) solution gives E₈**: rank 8, dimension 248, with 240 roots — the entire subject of the `e8-lattice` series, where the 240 roots are shown to be the minimum-norm vectors of the E₈ lattice and to give the densest sphere packing of ℝ⁸ (Viazovska 2016). This explainer treats E₈ briefly and cross-references the e8-lattice series for its full story; most of the content here is E₇ in detail plus a summary of why the E-series stops at E₈. No further solution exists: a hypothetical **(6,3,2) would give 1/6 + 1/3 + 1/2 = exactly 1**, making the Cartan matrix only positive semi-definite and breaking the finite-dimensional classification. E₈ is therefore the topological ceiling of simple Lie algebras; what lies beyond is the domain of affine Kac-Moody algebras, which we touch as a sidebar in #15 and leave to the `monstrous-moonshine` series for full development. **Figure:** side-by-side E₇ and E₈ Dynkin diagrams with the arm-length slider from #10 animated from (4,3,2) through (5,3,2) to the forbidden (6,3,2), with the 1/p + 1/q + 1/r meter showing the drop below the threshold at the last step; an E₈-series cross-link box pointing to the e8-lattice explainers for the 240 roots.

**15. The Full Classification and the Freudenthal Magic Square.** The classification is now complete: A_n (n ≥ 1), B_n (n ≥ 2), C_n (n ≥ 3), D_n (n ≥ 4), G₂, F₄, E₆, E₇, E₈ — four infinite families and five exceptions. The exceptions are not a random list. The **Freudenthal magic square** arranges them in a 4×4 table whose rows and columns are indexed by the four normed division algebras ℝ, ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆:

| | ℝ | ℂ | ℍ | 𝕆 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ℝ** | 𝔰𝔬(3) | 𝔰𝔲(3) | 𝔰𝔭(3) | **F₄** |
| **ℂ** | 𝔰𝔲(3) | 𝔰𝔲(3)² | 𝔰𝔲(6) | **E₆** |
| **ℍ** | 𝔰𝔭(3) | 𝔰𝔲(6) | 𝔰𝔬(12) | **E₇** |
| **𝕆** | **F₄** | **E₆** | **E₇** | **E₈** |

F₄, E₆, E₇, and E₈ are exactly the bottom row and right column — the algebras built from octonionic input to the Vinberg / Tits construction. G₂ sits outside the square as Der(𝕆), the automorphism group of the octonions themselves. The five exceptional Lie algebras exist **because the octonions exist**: if you accept the Cartan-Killing machinery, then the octonions are not optional — they are the reason the classification has exceptions at all.

This closes the main narrative of the series. Two pointers forward:

- **Affine Kac-Moody algebras** (short sidebar). If you force a ninth node onto E₈, or any other natural extension, the Cartan matrix determinant drops to exactly zero, the matrix becomes positive *semi*-definite rather than positive definite, and the structure transitions from a finite simple Lie algebra into an **affine Kac-Moody algebra** — the extended Dynkin diagrams 𝐴̃_n, 𝐷̃_n, 𝐸̃₆, 𝐸̃₇, 𝐸̃₈. These are infinite-dimensional but form the mathematical backbone of 2D conformal field theory, integrable systems, and string theory. The `monstrous-moonshine` series picks this thread up.
- **Parallel series**. The `e8-lattice` series walks through E₈ in full (240 roots → Weyl reflections → Leech lattice → Viazovska's sphere packing theorem); the `octonions` series builds the octonion structure that underpins G₂ through E₈, with the Freudenthal magic square as its own capstone in its explainer #14. The three series (lie-algebras, e8-lattice, octonions) are designed to be readable in any order and mutually reinforcing.

**Figure:** the full magic square as a 4×4 interactive table, each cell showing its algebra's Dynkin diagram, dimension, and a one-line description; hovering the ℝ column or row lights up the classical ancestors of each exception; a final "walk the tree" animation replays the H₀ ascent from A₁ through the classical chains and into E₈, closing the series on the image the opening set out to build.

## Dependencies

1. **Linear algebra is a hard prerequisite.** Matrices, inner products, eigenvalues, orthogonal complements — taken for granted throughout.
2. **Basic group theory is a soft prerequisite.** Enough that "the symmetric group" is a familiar phrase, but we do not rely on Sylow theorems, representation theory, or any sophisticated group-theoretic machinery.
3. **`e8-lattice` series is not a hard prerequisite but is extensively cross-linked.** Act III explainer 14 specifically defers E₈-detail work to that series, and #15 cross-references its magic-square capstone. A reader who has already gone through `e8-lattice` will find explainer 14 more of a victory lap than an introduction.
4. **`octonions` series is a soft prerequisite for Act III.** G₂, F₄, E₆ all use the octonions, and while we introduce just enough to be self-contained, readers who have been through `octonions` get a clearer picture of why those exceptional algebras exist at all.

## What this series will not do

- **Give complete proofs.** The classification theorem takes a careful semester to prove rigorously. We give the combinatorial heart of the argument (restrictions on Dynkin diagrams) and sketch the rest, marking Cartan's criterion (#04) and the sl₂ representation theory used in the Cartan-integer argument (#05) as black-box inputs.
- **Representation theory beyond what is needed for the classification.** Irreducible representations, weight diagrams, characters, Kostant multiplicity — all important, all separate-series material. We mention representations only as much as needed to motivate root spaces and quote the sl₂ fact used in #05.
- **Infinite-dimensional Lie algebras.** Affine Kac-Moody, loop algebras, Virasoro, vertex algebras — all out of scope. Explainer 15 mentions Kac-Moody once as a sidebar answering "why no E₉" and pointing to the `monstrous-moonshine` series.
- **Physics applications.** Standard Model, gauge theory, grand unification — all out of scope. Physics belongs in a separate series that *uses* this series as a prerequisite.
- **Field-theoretic subtleties.** We work over ℂ throughout. Real forms, compact forms, Vogan diagrams, Chevalley's theorem about fields of definition — out of scope. The classical compact real forms (SU(n+1), SO(2n+1), Sp(n), SO(2n)) are mentioned in passing in #08 only for name recognition.
- **Computational details beyond the interactive figures.** Root-system computations are done by the D3 code; we don't teach the reader to compute by hand.
