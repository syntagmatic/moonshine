# Information Geometry

The space of probability distributions is a Riemannian manifold, and everything from maximum likelihood to natural gradient to the EM algorithm follows from that fact. Fifteen interactive explainers that treat probability distributions as geometric objects — points on a curved space with a Fisher metric, KL-divergence distances, and flat exponential-family coordinates — and explain why modern machine learning methods have the shapes they do.

## Locked plan

**Spine.** Most statistics textbooks teach probability as algebra over a flat set of distributions. Information geometry, due mainly to Amari (1985 onward) and building on Rao (1945) and Chentsov (1972), treats that set as a manifold with a natural Riemannian metric — the Fisher information metric — and a natural divergence — KL. In that geometric picture, the usual algorithms of statistics stop being disparate tricks and become applications of a single framework: maximum likelihood is information projection, natural gradient is the geodesically-natural gradient, the EM algorithm is alternating projection between two flat submanifolds, exponential families are the "flat" distributions that are easy to compute with, and mixture models are their bent cousins. This series teaches that framework from the ground up in three acts: first get the reader onto the manifold, then explore its structure, then apply the structure to real ML algorithms.

**Reader endpoint.** After this series, a reader should be able to: (a) look at a parameterised family of distributions and sketch its Fisher metric locally; (b) state and interpret KL divergence without treating it as "something like distance"; (c) recognise an exponential family on sight and write down its natural parameters; (d) explain the Pythagorean theorem for KL and when it applies; (e) implement natural gradient descent instead of vanilla SGD and justify the choice; (f) explain the EM algorithm as alternating information projection; (g) recognise when a problem benefits from information geometry and when it doesn't.

**Identity.** Slug `information-geometry`, title *Information Geometry*, tagline *"The space of probability distributions is a Riemannian manifold, and everything follows."* Voice is first-person-plural, pedagogically careful, and conscious that the typical reader arrives with ML background rather than differential geometry background. We introduce the small amount of differential geometry needed (tangent spaces, connections, parallel transport) on a just-in-time basis rather than dumping it upfront. Visual register matches the other moonshine series: 740px article width, serif body, interactive SVG figures with KaTeX math overlay.

**Shape.** Three acts, five explainers each.

KL divergence as the finite version of that same idea. Bregman divergences as the larger family. Tangent spaces and score functions as the differential-geometry scaffolding. By the end of Act I the reader has stopped thinking of "a distribution" as "a table of probabilities" and started thinking of it as "a point on a curved 2D surface" (for concrete 2-parameter examples we can actually draw).

## Explainer list

Fifteen explainers in three acts, filled in against the research pass (`research/04-15-2026-info-geom.txt`). The research took the series in an unexpectedly rich direction: Act III pivots away from classical ML applications (EM, variational inference, mixture models) and toward the **unclaimed Moonshine frontier** — how the Fisher metric is mathematically isomorphic to the Weil-Petersson metric on CFT moduli spaces, how the Kullback-Leibler divergence measures thermodynamic length between modular invariants, and how the j-function itself becomes an entropic potential function in a Koszul-Souriau dually flat geometry. ### Act I · The Manifold

**01. Parameterised Distributions as a Manifold.** A parametric family S = {p(x|θ) : θ ∈ Θ} is a differentiable manifold of dimension n (the number of parameters), where each "point" is an entire probability distribution, not a spatial location. The Gaussian family {𝒩(μ, σ) : μ ∈ ℝ, σ > 0} is the canonical example: a 2D manifold whose points are distributions. A step of size ε in parameter space means very different things at different points — shifting μ by 0.01 when σ = 0.01 is a huge statistical move; shifting μ by 0.01 when σ = 100 is imperceptible. The manifold interpretation forces us to measure moves by *distinguishability*, not by coordinate difference. Interactive: (μ, σ) slider with a live density plot on one side and a "you are here" dot on the 2D manifold on the other.

**02. The Fisher Information Metric.** The Fisher information matrix g_{ij}(θ) = 𝔼_p[(∂log p / ∂θ_i)(∂log p / ∂θ_j)] is the unique (up to a constant scalar) Riemannian metric tensor on any statistical manifold. Equivalently, under mild regularity conditions, g_{ij}(θ) = -𝔼_p[∂²log p / ∂θ_i ∂θ_j], the negative Hessian of the log-likelihood. Regions of high Fisher information are highly curved (small parameter changes cause large changes in distribution); regions of low Fisher information are "flat" (small parameter changes barely move the distribution). For the Gaussian family, g_{ij} in (μ, σ) coordinates turns out to be diagonal with entries depending on 1/σ², and the resulting geometry is *hyperbolic* — isometric to the Poincaré half-plane — with constant negative curvature. Geodesics between Gaussians are semi-circles orthogonal to the σ = 0 boundary, not straight lines. Interactive: Fisher ellipse visualiser on (μ, σ) space showing how the metric scales with σ.

**03. Chentsov's Uniqueness Theorem.** Why the Fisher metric, and not some other Riemannian metric? Chentsov's 1972 theorem proves that, for a manifold of probability distributions over a finite sample space, the Fisher information metric is the *unique* (up to a constant scalar factor) Riemannian metric that remains invariant under Markov morphisms — reversible, information-preserving transformations of the data. A sufficient statistic is the standard example: if you can transform your data through a sufficient statistic without losing information, the Riemannian distance between two distributions must be unchanged. Any metric other than Fisher would let you artificially stretch or compress distances by merely relabelling your sample space — a violation of basic information-theoretic principles. Chentsov's theorem turns the Fisher metric from "a convenient choice" into "the only possible choice".

**04. Kullback–Leibler Divergence: The Asymmetric Distance.** The KL divergence D_KL(P ∥ Q) = ∫ p(x) log(p(x)/q(x)) dx is the global companion to the Fisher metric. It is non-negative (Gibbs' inequality), zero iff P = Q, but it is famously *not* a metric: it is not symmetric (D_KL(P ∥ Q) ≠ D_KL(Q ∥ P) in general) and does not satisfy the triangle inequality. The asymmetry is physically meaningful: the information cost of approximating a complex "true" distribution P with a simpler model Q is not the same as the cost of approximating simple Q with complex P. In generative modelling, minimising forward KL D_KL(P_data ∥ P_model) gives mode-covering behaviour (blurry outputs); minimising reverse KL D_KL(P_model ∥ P_data) gives mode-seeking behaviour (sharp but mode-collapsed outputs like GANs). The asymmetry mirrors the thermodynamic arrow of time: information loss is a strictly directional phenomenon. Interactive: KL heatmap on a 2D parameter space with a forward/reverse toggle, illustrating the asymmetry.

**05. Local KL Equals Fisher.** Despite being globally non-metric, KL divergence has a beautiful infinitesimal relationship to the Fisher metric. Taylor-expanding D_KL(P(θ) ∥ P(θ + dθ)) around dθ = 0: the zeroth-order term vanishes (P equals itself), the first-order term vanishes (derivative of D_KL at the identity is zero), and the *second*-order term is exactly (1/2) g_{ij}(θ) dθ^i dθ^j, where g_{ij} is the Fisher matrix. This is the bridge: globally, KL is asymmetric and non-metric, but locally it is exactly the Riemannian distance of the Fisher metric. It acts as an *asymmetric pre-contrast function* that induces the Riemannian geometry. For symmetric global distances, one uses the Jeffreys divergence (symmetrized KL) or the Hellinger distance (a proper metric). Recent work on κ-deformed KL divergences extends this framework to non-extensive thermodynamic systems. Interactive: compare KL along a short curve to Fisher-metric arc length, seeing the agreement as the step shrinks.

### Act II · Dual Connections and Dually Flat Spaces

**06. α-Connections and Duality.** A Riemannian manifold needs more than a metric: it needs a *connection* that defines parallel transport of tangent vectors and therefore identifies geodesics. Euclidean space has the trivial Levi-Civita connection; information geometry instead comes with Amari's one-parameter family of α-connections ∇^(α), α ∈ ℝ. The α = +1 connection is the "e-connection" (exponential), the α = -1 connection is the "m-connection" (mixture), and α = 0 recovers the Levi-Civita connection of the Fisher metric. Two connections ∇ and ∇* are *dual* with respect to g if Z(g(X,Y)) = g(∇_Z X, Y) + g(X, ∇*_Z Y) for all vector fields X, Y, Z. The e- and m-connections are dual to each other in this sense. When both connections are simultaneously flat (their curvature tensors vanish), the manifold is called *dually flat* — a structure that most classical statistical models enjoy.

**07. Exponential Families and Dual Flatness.** An exponential family is a parametric family of the form p(x|θ) = exp(Σ θ^i x_i - ψ(θ)), where θ = (θ¹, …, θⁿ) are the *natural parameters*, x_i are the sufficient statistics, and ψ(θ) is the log-partition function (a strictly convex potential). The Gaussian, Bernoulli, Poisson, Dirichlet, beta, gamma, von Mises-Fisher, and most other "nice" distributions are exponential families. Information geometry's key observation: exponential families are *dually flat*. Both the e-connection and the m-connection have identically zero curvature. There are two canonical coordinate systems on the same manifold: the natural parameters θ and the expectation parameters η_i = 𝔼[x_i] = ∂ψ(θ)/∂θ^i. The two coordinate systems are linked by a Legendre transform: ψ(θ) + ϕ(η) - Σ θ^i η_i = 0, where ϕ(η) is the dual potential (typically the negative Shannon entropy in statistical physics interpretation). Interactive: live θ ↔ η conversion for a Gaussian family with the Legendre relationship visualised.

**08. The Generalized Pythagorean Theorem.** In a dually flat space, the KL divergence obeys an analogue of the Pythagorean theorem. If three distributions P, Q, R are positioned such that the e-geodesic from P to Q is Fisher-orthogonal to the m-geodesic from Q to R, then D_KL(P ∥ R) = D_KL(P ∥ Q) + D_KL(Q ∥ R). This is the engine behind every projection-based statistical algorithm: maximum likelihood estimation is an m-projection of the empirical distribution onto the model family, the EM algorithm is alternating e- and m-projection, variational inference is e-projection onto a simpler family, and belief propagation is iterated projection on a graphical model's factor graph. The Pythagorean theorem reduces complex maximum-likelihood problems to pure geometric orthogonal projections. Interactive: project a data point onto an e-flat submanifold and see the KL decomposition light up.

**09. Bregman Divergences.** A Bregman divergence D_φ(x ∥ y) = φ(x) - φ(y) - ⟨∇φ(y), x - y⟩ generalises KL to any strictly convex potential φ. Squared Euclidean distance is the Bregman divergence for φ(x) = ‖x‖²/2; KL divergence is the Bregman divergence for negative Shannon entropy; Itakura-Saito divergence (used in speech processing) is the Bregman divergence for φ(x) = -log x; logistic loss is the Bregman divergence for the softplus function. Every Bregman divergence inherits a Pythagorean theorem and supports its own Voronoi diagrams and clustering algorithms. The unifying observation is that any strictly convex potential ψ on an affine space induces a dually flat geometry whose divergence is a Bregman divergence, generalising the exponential-family story. Interactive: pick a convex generator, see the corresponding Bregman contour plot.

**10. Natural Gradient Descent.** Vanilla SGD uses the Euclidean gradient: θ_{t+1} = θ_t - η ∇L(θ_t). This implicitly assumes a flat parameter space — a catastrophic error in probability space, where shifting σ by 0.01 near σ = 0.01 causes huge KL shifts while the same numerical shift near σ = 100 is imperceptible. Natural gradient descent corrects this by pre-multiplying the gradient by the inverse Fisher matrix: θ_{t+1} = θ_t - η G⁻¹(θ_t) ∇L(θ_t). The natural gradient is *parameterisation invariant* — whether your model is parameterised by σ, σ², or log σ, the natural gradient produces the same trajectory through the intrinsic manifold. In deep networks, the Fisher matrix is equivalent to the Gauss-Newton Hessian approximation, giving second-order convergence benefits without the instability of negative eigenvalues. Recently, Quantum Natural Gradient (QNG) has extended the framework to variational quantum algorithms, using the Quantum Fisher Information Matrix to escape "barren plateaus" in parameterised quantum circuits. Interactive: side-by-side SGD vs NGD on a steeply curved loss landscape.

### Act III · Visualisation and the Moonshine Frontier

**11. Visualising Probability: Hyperbolic and Bregman Voronoi Diagrams.** Classical Voronoi diagrams partition Euclidean space by nearest neighbour; when applied to statistical manifolds they must respect the curvature of the underlying geometry. In a dually flat space the appropriate generalisation is the Bregman Voronoi diagram, constructed from equivalent Laguerre power diagrams, with cells whose boundaries are induced by Bregman divergences rather than squared Euclidean distances. For the hyperbolic Gaussian family the result is a hyperbolic Voronoi diagram, with cells warping and stretching as they approach the σ = 0 boundary. For categorical distributions the Fisher manifold is isometrically embedded on the positive orthant of a sphere in Euclidean space, so its Voronoi cells are great-circle diagrams. For multivariate autoregressive data, researchers use Hilbert simplex geometry and Siegel space embeddings to track temporal probabilistic dependencies. Interactive: hyperbolic Voronoi diagram in the Poincaré half-plane for a mixture of Gaussians.

**12. The EM Algorithm as Alternating Projection.** The Expectation-Maximization algorithm (Dempster-Laird-Rubin 1977) for latent-variable models has a clean information-geometric interpretation: it is alternating projection between two flat submanifolds. The E-step is an m-projection: given current parameters θ_t, project onto the observed-plus-latent joint distribution using the mixture connection. The M-step is an e-projection: project back to the model family using the exponential connection. The generalized Pythagorean theorem (explainer 8) then forces KL divergence to decrease strictly with each iteration, which proves EM's monotone convergence property. This explainer connects the statistical theory of Act II to real algorithms that deep learning, Bayesian inference, and computer vision rely on every day. Interactive: animated EM trajectory for a 2-component Gaussian mixture with the projection steps highlighted.

**13. The Unclaimed Frontier: Information Geometry Meets Moonshine.** The research's most important pivot. The Moonshine ecosystem — `monstrous-moonshine`, umbral moonshine, Mathieu moonshine — is populated by partition functions, moduli spaces, relative entropies, and modular invariants. These are fundamentally *statistical* objects: a conformal field theory's partition function Z(τ), once normalized, is a generating function for a probability distribution over quantum states, and the parameter space τ is a natural statistical manifold. Yet information geometry has barely touched this territory, and the research argues it *should*. This explainer introduces the frontier: Z(τ) as a partition function, the moduli space as a Riemannian manifold, the Fisher metric as a natural Riemannian structure on that moduli space, and KL divergence between conformal vacua as a measure of thermodynamic length along renormalization group flows. Pointer forward to the `monstrous-moonshine` series for the ecosystem and to explainers 14 and 15 for the deep connections.

**14. The Fisher Metric as the Weil-Petersson Metric on Moduli Spaces.** In string theory, the moduli space of a Calabi-Yau manifold or K3 surface is equipped with the Weil-Petersson metric (or the Zamolodchikov metric in 2D CFT), derived via complex algebraic geometry. *Viewed through information-geometric eyes*, these metrics are mathematically isomorphic to the Fisher information metric on the moduli space treated as a statistical manifold of normalised characters. This was verified explicitly for instantons in nonlinear sigma models like CP¹ and CP²: the Fisher metric of an instanton with topological charge k yields a three-dimensional hyperbolic metric identical to the metric of Euclidean anti-de Sitter space AdS₃, with ds² = (4k²/3)(dλ² + d**b**²)/λ². Applying this to the K3 surfaces that appear in Umbral Moonshine reveals that the Fisher metric represents a previously unrecognised constraint on quantum gravity theories: consistent theories must have moduli-space metrics bounded by an information curvature. Natural gradient descent on the statistical manifold then gives a purely geometric formulation of renormalization group flow — with the c-theorem (information loss under RG flow) appearing as the asymmetry of KL divergence.

**15. Koszul-Souriau and the j-Function as Thermodynamic Entropy.** The deepest and most esoteric integration of information geometry into Moonshine. In the specialised field of Lie-group thermodynamics, the Fisher information metric can be formulated via the Koszul-Souriau moment map, where it emerges as the opposite of the derivative of the mean moment map with respect to a geometric temperature — physically equivalent to a geometric specific heat. Defining a J-invariant positive-definite inner product on the Lie algebra associated to the Monster group, the j-function of classical number theory can be mapped directly to an *entropic potential function* in a Koszul-Souriau dually flat geometry framework. The states of the Monster VOA become thermodynamic states on a Koszul-Souriau statistical manifold, and geometric operations like the Legendre transform dictate the symmetries of modular forms. This frames the "magical" coincidences of Monstrous Moonshine not merely as topological equivalences between string theory and algebra, but as an optimization of information entropy bounded by the Fisher metric of a massive quantum state space. This is the series' final reflection and the unclaimed research frontier it points toward: cultivating information-geometric interpretations of Moonshine may yield a novel, purely geometric proof of Umbral and Monstrous phenomena and unify the most exceptional symmetries in mathematics with the most fundamental geometric constraints on probability and uncertainty.

## Dependencies

1. **Probability and statistics are hard prerequisites.** The reader knows what a Gaussian is, what an expectation is, what a likelihood function is. We do not re-teach probability.
2. **A bit of differential geometry is introduced just-in-time.** Tangent spaces (explainer 2), connections (explainer 6), parallel transport (implicit in explainer 10). None of this is done with coordinate-free rigour; we stay concrete and use coordinates throughout.
3. **Machine learning intuition is useful for Act II.** The reader has seen SGD and fit at least one exponential-family model. A reader without this background will still follow the geometry but will miss some of the "why does natural gradient matter" motivation.
4. **`monstrous-moonshine` series is a soft prerequisite for Act III.** Explainers 13, 14, and 15 lean heavily on Moonshine content. A reader who has not seen `monstrous-moonshine` will encounter unfamiliar terminology (VOA, Monster, j-function, K3 surface, RG flow) in three of the final five explainers. The series can be read before `monstrous-moonshine` — in which case Act III is a preview of that material viewed through the information-geometric lens — or after it, in which case Act III becomes a retrospective that unifies the two frameworks. Either reading order works, and the series text should make this explicit.
5. **`lie-algebras` series is a very soft prerequisite for explainer 15.** The Koszul-Souriau material uses the Lie algebra of the Monster group as the substrate for the J-invariant inner product. A reader familiar with `lie-algebras` will recognise this construction as a Lie-group thermodynamic interpretation; a reader unfamiliar with it will still see the result but may miss some of the connective tissue.

## What this series will not do

- **Full differential geometry textbook treatment.** Tensors, connections in coordinate-free form, parallel transport as a formal construction — all out of scope. We use coordinates throughout and stay concrete.
- **Quantum information geometry in full depth.** Density matrices, Bures metric, quantum Fisher information — mentioned in passing in explainer 10 (Quantum Natural Gradient) but not developed. A future sequel could cover it.
- **Survey every application of information geometry.** There are many. We pick five classical applications (Acts I–II) and five frontier/Moonshine connections (Act III) and let the rest be mentioned in passing. Classical topics that got cut: variational inference in detail, mixture models beyond a brief mention, the information bottleneck principle, belief propagation as information projection. All are worthy but none fit our pivoted narrative as cleanly as the Moonshine frontier.
- **Prove the Cramér-Rao bound rigorously.** We state it, give the geometric intuition (Fisher metric as infinitesimal distinguishability), and move on. The proof belongs in a statistics textbook.
- **Re-prove Chentsov's theorem.** Explainer 3 states the theorem and explains why it matters, but we do not attempt the full proof (which involves functorial axioms on categories of statistical models).
- **Claim the Moonshine frontier is established mathematics.** Act III's explainers 13–15 are careful to present the information-geometric reading of Moonshine as a *research frontier* rather than a settled theory. We cite the specific connections that have been verified (Weil-Petersson ↔ Fisher, AdS₃ ↔ CP¹ instantons) and are honest that the Koszul-Souriau j-function interpretation is speculative, not proven. This series opens a door; it does not claim to have walked through it.
