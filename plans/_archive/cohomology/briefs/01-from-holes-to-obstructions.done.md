# Brief 01 — From holes to obstructions: why dualize at all

**Slug.** `01-from-holes-to-obstructions`
**Target file.** `docs/cohomology/01-from-holes-to-obstructions.html`
**Series.** *Holes Have Names* — [spine](../README.md) · [AGENTS](../AGENTS.md)
**Act.** I — From holes to rings.

## One-line claim

A cohomology class is what a 1-cochain becomes when you stop caring about the parts of it that come from a function on vertices.

## Motivating frame

Homology counts holes. Cohomology gives those holes names you can pair against chains and get a number. The reader has met homology in the TDA series; this essay motivates the next step by exhibiting an *obstruction* — the hairy ball theorem, named but not proved — and pointing out that its cleanest statement is a cohomology class. We do not prove the hairy ball; we *show what it would mean* to name an obstruction with a number that depends only on topology, and then we build the smallest example of that naming move: a 1-cochain on a triangulated annulus, with an exact piece slid in and out, and the pairing on cycles staying invariant.

This is the essay's only job: convince the reader that ker δ / im δ is the right quotient. The torus computation and the cup product wait for explainers 02 and 03.

## Mathematical content

- Recall: $C_k$ is the $\mathbb{Z}/2$-vector space generated by $k$-simplices of a complex; $\partial_k: C_k \to C_{k-1}$ is the boundary; $\partial^2 = 0$; $H_k = \ker \partial_k / \mathrm{im}\,\partial_{k+1}$.
- New: $C^k = \mathrm{Hom}(C_k, \mathbb{Z}/2)$, the *cochain group*. A $k$-cochain is a function on $k$-simplices.
- The coboundary $\delta_k : C^k \to C^{k+1}$ is the *transpose* of $\partial_{k+1}$. Concretely, $(\delta a)(\sigma) = a(\partial \sigma)$.
- The Kronecker pairing $\langle a, c \rangle = \sum a(\sigma) c(\sigma) \pmod 2$ for $a \in C^k$, $c \in C_k$.
- If $a$ is closed ($\delta a = 0$) and $c = \partial b$ is a boundary, then $\langle a, c \rangle = \langle a, \partial b \rangle = \langle \delta a, b \rangle = 0$. The pairing descends to $H^k \otimes H_k \to \mathbb{Z}/2$.
- The shift to make at the end of the essay: cocycles are the things that pair well with cycles, and two cocycles that differ by a coboundary $\delta f$ give the *same* pairing on every cycle. That is what it means to be in the same cohomology class.

The hairy ball anecdote: a nonvanishing tangent vector field on $S^2$ would give a section of the tangent bundle; the obstruction to that section lives in $H^2(S^2; \mathbb{Z})$ and is the Euler class. We name this and move on; the formal proof is not in scope.

## Interactive figure

**The annulus pairing demo.** A triangulated annulus, drawn flat in the plane, $\sim 24$ vertices and $\sim 40$ edges and $\sim 24$ triangles. Use `COH.tri.annulus(8, 16)` (inner ring 8, outer ring 16). The annulus comes with two canonical 1-cycles: the inner boundary loop $\gamma_\text{in}$ and the outer boundary loop $\gamma_\text{out}$. Both lift the generator of $H_1(\text{annulus}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2$, so $\langle a, \gamma_\text{in} \rangle = \langle a, \gamma_\text{out} \rangle$ for every cocycle $a$.

Two panels, side by side:

- **Left.** The annulus, edges drawn as lines. The reader can click edges to toggle a value in a base 1-cochain $\alpha$ (edge highlighted green for "1", grey for "0"). A vertex-function picker lets the reader choose a vertex $v$; clicking the vertex picks a $0$-cochain $f = $ indicator of $v$. A slider $t \in \{0, 1\}$ (binary toggle is fine over $\mathbb{Z}/2$; for the visual feel, animate it as a smooth fade between two cocycles $\alpha$ and $\alpha + \delta f$) animates adding $\delta f$ to $\alpha$. Edges where the cochain *changed* under the toggle flash red briefly: those are the edges incident to $v$. The reader sees δ as a *physical operation* — it touches exactly the simplices that border the vertex.
- **Right.** Two live readouts: $\langle \alpha, \gamma_\text{in} \rangle$ and $\langle \alpha, \gamma_\text{out} \rangle$. Each is rendered as a big "0" or "1" in `--c-pair` gold. As the reader toggles the slider, both readouts *do not change*. That is the entire point.

A tiny third panel below shows the *unclosed* cochain check: $\delta \alpha$ rendered as a number on each triangle. If any triangle has $\delta \alpha = 1$ (i.e., $\alpha$ is not closed), the readouts on the right turn dim and a note says "α is not a cocycle." This is the only place the reader is taught the closedness condition: by seeing it fail and seeing the pairing become ill-defined.

**Distinct visual grammar.** Cells-on-a-flat-strip with two giant pairing readouts. This is the only essay where the geometry is a flat strip and the figure leans into a 2-panel evaluation comparison.

## Pitfalls

- Do not develop the Euler class formally. It is anecdote; cited, not built.
- Over $\mathbb{Z}/2$, the cup product reduces nicely but the *signs* in $\delta = \pm \partial^T$ disappear. Mention this; do not develop signs.
- The flat embedding of the annulus is purely a drawing convention; the algebra is intrinsic. Make sure no language suggests the embedding is part of the cohomology.
- Resist over-explaining "what cohomology is for." The next two essays do the work; this essay just has to make ker δ / im δ feel like the right object.

## Closing — what we take with us

- A cocycle is a cochain that vanishes on every boundary.
- An exact cochain is one that comes from a vertex function via δ.
- $H^k = \ker \delta_k / \mathrm{im}\,\delta_{k-1}$ is the right quotient because the Kronecker pairing on $H_k$ is well-defined exactly on this quotient.
- Cohomology is the algebra of obstructions: a class is a number you can pair with a chain to get a topological invariant.
