# Foam: Dividing Space with the Least Surface

I want seven articles on one question: what is the least-wall way to divide space into cells of equal size? It is for curious readers comfortable with some math and new to crystallography. The plane has a proven answer, two bubbles have a proven answer, and filling three-dimensional space is still open: Kelvin's elegant guess stood for 106 years, then lost by about 0.3% to a two-cell foam found in 1993. The first four articles climb to that foam. The last three follow it outward: the same arrangement in metal, ice and soft matter, the lattice recipe that proposes foams in any dimension (stopping at four), and what a real foam does over time. A reader should finish knowing Plateau's laws by feel, why the hexagon wins in the plane, why Kelvin's flat cell cannot be the answer as drawn, what the Weaire-Phelan foam is and why it keeps turning up, why packing and partition are different contests, and why a bubble's fate in a flat foam depends on its number of sides.

The series index opens with a real Voronoi strip whose seed points slide from random to a triangular lattice, so the cells go from froth to honeycomb across the page.

## Articles

### 1. The Honeycomb
In the plane three films always meet at 120 degrees, and the regular hexagonal grid spends the least wall per unit area; Hales' honeycomb theorem proves it beats every equal-area partition. The article opens with four pins at the corners of a square joined by film: the reader slides the bridge between two forks, and the length bottoms out where the forks reach 120 degrees, below the four-way X. A random froth on a wrap-around box then relaxes step by step (Lloyd relaxation of Voronoi cells) and shows the forks opening toward 120 degrees, the side counts bunching around six (the mean is six from the start, by Euler), and the wall cost falling, though it stalls short of a honeycomb and the caption says so. A smaller figure ranks the three regular tilings by wall cost. The proof gets its own figure: the reader trades hexagons for mixes of other side counts with the mean pinned at six and watches the regular-polygon floor rise, which is the old argument for convex cells, drawn beside the straight line Hales's proof uses in its place. Two figures then test the theorem with real films: equal-area bubbles with curved walls relaxed in the page beside a precomputed survey of scrambled honeycombs and random starts, none of them below the hexagon; and every cell of one such foam against both floors, where few-sided cells with bulging walls beat their own regular polygon and none beats Hales's bound. Say that an experiment is not a proof. It closes on three pentagons around a vertex leaving a gap, a frustration that returns in 3D, where foam cells are mostly pentagons.

### 2. The Double Bubble
One volume gives the sphere: a figure compares plane shapes enclosing the same area, and a chart ranks the five regular solids, and a cylinder whose proportions the reader sets, by how much more surface they need than a sphere of the same volume. Two volumes give the familiar double bubble. The signature figure is a double bubble in cross-section with a slider for the share of gas in one chamber, built from the 120-degree condition with volumes in closed form, whose middle wall is flat only when the volumes are equal; a companion chart shows how much film sharing a wall saves over two separate spheres. The history: the equal-volume case by a computer-assisted proof in 1995 (Hass, Hutchings and Schlafly), the general case in 2000, published 2002 (Hutchings, Morgan, Ritoré and Ros) with no computer. To show what ruling out other clusters means, one rival is computed exactly in the plane, where every film is a circular arc: one region in two pieces on either side of the other, balanced and with 120-degree corners, yet dearer than two separate circles, with sliders for the two moves the proofs use against it. It closes on the standard triple bubble in the plane, solved from three chosen areas, and on the state of proofs for three and more bubbles.

### 3. Kelvin's Bubble
Kelvin's 1887 cell is the truncated octahedron, the territory of one point of the body-centered cubic lattice. The reader cuts the points off an octahedron to any depth and sees that only one depth fills space, and what the cell costs there, then checks Plateau's two rules in 3D (120 degrees along an edge, the tetrahedral angle where four edges meet) and sees the flat cell break both: its faces meet at 109.47 and 125.26 degrees, so they must curve. Following Thomson's own paper, a soap film on a cubic wire frame, relaxed in the page, shows why more than four edges never meet at a point: twelve flat films through the center cost more than the film that opens a small square there. Height maps of one hexagon and one square of the relaxed cell show how the faces bend (the hexagon up at three edges and down at three, the square flat with bowed edges), with true amplitudes and Thomson's formula beside the mesh. The cost figure computes the relaxed value in the page on several meshes, sets it beside the published one, and marks the 1993 Weaire-Phelan foam that beats it, on a zoomed axis. A last chart sets the face shapes counted in a real foam (Matzke, 1946) between Kelvin's cell and Weaire and Phelan's: mostly pentagons, of which Kelvin's cell has none.

### 4. The Counterexample
Weaire and Phelan's foam has two cell shapes of equal volume, a dodecahedron and a fourteen-faced cell, eight to a repeat. The article builds it from eight points in a cube (each point takes the region nearest to it), shows that those raw cells are unequal and that the walls must shift slightly to equalize them, and argues from Plateau's corner angle why cells of pentagons with a few hexagons are the thing to aim for. The signature figure relaxes Kelvin's foam and this one side by side in the page with a small soap-film relaxer (triangulated walls moved downhill in area at fixed cell volumes), plotting wall cost per step against the published values and drawing the cells as they bend. Companion figures show every junction angle closing on Plateau's values; the same relaxation on finer and finer meshes, extrapolated to the published values; the cost of each kind of cell, flat and relaxed, which shows the dodecahedra carrying most of the saving; and cost against the dodecahedron's share of the volume, flat and relaxed, which gives the pressure difference between the two kinds of cell and sets the flat equal-volume foam against the proved floor for Kelvin's arrangement. Other figures turn the two cells over, pull one repeat apart (and show the three perpendicular families of columns), and cut a flat slice through the foam at any tilt, which is the story of the Beijing Water Cube. It says plainly what is proved (it beats Kelvin's arrangement) and what is not (optimality).

### 5. Why It Recurs
The eight points were known before 1993 as a metal structure and as the cage framework of gas hydrates, and later as a liquid crystal of soft molecular balls. The common cause is that regular tetrahedra almost fill space: five around an edge leave a small gap. The signature figure closes five tetrahedra around an edge and shows the gap and what it takes to close it. Another shows one site with its cell and its shell of neighbors as one object read two ways, for 12, 14, 15 and 16 neighbors, with the tetrahedra around each bond equal to the sides of the wall it crosses. The bonds ringed by six tetrahedra, the ones that take up the misfit, are then drawn alone for three crystals of the family (straight lines, sheets with lines, one network), with their share of all bonds beside the share the ideal count requires. A chart compares the mix of cell types in several structures against the ideal average, and a companion relaxes a rival of the family in the page and plots wall cost against how unequal the cells are allowed to be: nearer the ideal count, cheaper with its natural unequal cells, dearer than Kelvin's once the volumes are equal. A figure reads one cube as atoms at cell centers or water molecules at cell corners, and slides the corners to the measured positions in a real hydrate. The soft-matter section gives the proposed reason (soft coats that pay for shared wall area) as its authors' free energy, computed from the series' own cells into a diagram of which arrangement wins where, and gives its limit.

### 6. The Lattice Recipe
Any lattice becomes a foam when each point takes its nearest region, and the same cell also scores the lattice as a packing of balls. In the plane one lattice wins both contests: a figure slides through a family of plane lattices and both curves turn at the triangular one. In space they split: a one-parameter family passing through face-centered cubic, simple cubic and body-centered cubic shows the densest packing at one end and the cheapest foam at the other, with the cell and its largest inscribed ball drawn so the reader can see which walls the ball touches. The checkerboard description of the face-centered lattice then carries the recipe to four dimensions, where the cell turns out regular: the 24-cell. The signature figure sets the 3D cell beside the projected 4D one and lets the reader turn the 4D cell until pyramid tips land where cube corners were. A chart scores lattices in two, three and four dimensions on both contests at once, and a rank chart adds two more scores read from the same cell (covering and rounding error), computed in the page beside the published tables, to show which contest disagrees with the others in each dimension. A junction test measures where walls meet in four flat lattice foams: the hexagons pass, the body-centered and face-centered foams in space fail, and the four-dimensional foam of 24-cells passes at every kind of junction, which is a known theorem and is cited as one. A last figure deforms five lattices along seeded random directions and plots the change in wall cost, as evidence, not proof, of which are local optima among lattices. The series stops at four dimensions.

### 7. Foams in Motion
A foam left alone coarsens: gas leaks through the films from high pressure to low, small bubbles vanish, and the rest grow. In a flat foam the rate of change of a bubble's area depends only on its number of sides, with six as the balance point. The article derives that by walking once around a bubble and counting the turning at its corners, with a figure of the symmetric n-sided bubble and its turning budget. The signature figure is a live simulated foam, pre-run to a telling state, with neighbor swaps and vanishing bubbles marked as they happen; a second figure measures each bubble's rate of area change in that same simulation against the predicted line; a third follows one bubble, chosen by the reader, through its life as a line that is straight until its side count changes; and another tracks mean bubble area and total film length over the run. A honeycomb is then run with one change: with one film removed the merged bubble keeps growing while almost every other bubble stays a hexagon, and with one bubble doubled in size nothing moves, because it still has six sides. It closes on grain growth in metals and the exact three-dimensional law, evaluated in the page on relaxed cells: Kelvin's sit at zero and the Weaire-Phelan dodecahedra shrink, so the best known foam would itself coarsen, and coarsening is not a search for the optimal foams of the earlier articles.

## What to get right

- Wall cost counts each shared wall once, split between its two cells; state that convention beside the hexagon's number, and keep "least perimeter" distinct from "most circular cell".
- Check every isoperimetric ratio by computing it (the cube matching a unit sphere's volume has about 24% more surface, the regular tetrahedron about 49%).
- The smaller bubble is at higher pressure, so the middle wall bulges into the larger one. Prose and figure must agree on this.
- Weaire-Phelan is the best known foam and nobody has proved it optimal. Its two cell types have equal volume, and the nearest-point cells of its eight points do not; the equalizing step has to be shown, not skipped.
- Surface-area values (Kelvin relaxed about 5.306, Weaire-Phelan about 5.288) should be computed in the page and set beside the published values, each labelled as which. A coarse mesh sits slightly above the true value; say so, and check the relaxer by refining the mesh and extrapolating. Plot on an honestly labelled zoomed axis.
- Bending of relaxed cells is small. If a drawing exaggerates it, say by how much and offer the true shape.
- Published accounts of the Water Cube say the foam was rotated and sliced but do not give the orientation. Let the reader tilt the cut; do not claim to reproduce the facade.
- The foam and the crystal share a network, not measurements. The foam bends walls to meet Plateau's angles; ice moves corners until bonds are equal; neither is a scaled copy of the other.
- Frank and Kasper singled out shells of 12, 14, 15 and 16 neighbors. Draw a shell only from a real structure's coordinates. Date the soft-matter results from the papers themselves, and keep the superconductivity of some of these alloys out of the argument.
- Packing and partition agree in the plane and split in three dimensions; do not state the split as a general law. Among the four-dimensional lattices a reader is likely to try, one wins both.
- The lattice recipe gives candidates, not optima: the best known 3D foam is not a lattice foam. Report the state of proofs about lattice foams and about four dimensions from the current literature, with preprints named as preprints.
- The coarsening simulation must not have the n minus 6 law or the 120-degree corners written into its rules, or measuring them shows nothing. Test it against lone bubbles of each side count, conservation of area, and a mean of exactly six sides before building figures on it.
- The n minus 6 form is for flat foams only. State the three-dimensional law as its authors do, and keep neighbor swaps and vanishing bubbles distinct.
- Relaxed wall costs depend on the mesh. Wherever a page quotes one, name the mesh, and keep the fine-mesh limits the same across articles 3, 4 and 5.
- Kusner and Sullivan score a foam by area cubed over volume squared, with each wall's area split between its two cells; convert before setting their numbers beside the series' wall cost. Their proof that the foam beats Kelvin's concerns the flat-faced equal-volume foam and a lower bound for foams arranged like Kelvin's, not the relaxed foams.
- Once relaxed, the two Weaire-Phelan cell types sit at different pressures. Articles 4 and 7 both compute the difference, by different routes, and must state the same value in the same units.
- In the film model a single neighbor swap in an unstrained honeycomb undoes itself. A lasting defect needs a film removed or many swaps; do not promise the reader a lone five-seven pair.
- Matzke's counts of a real foam are reached through later papers that reproduce his tables. Say which, and do not draw a distribution that was not found in a source.
- One color language throughout: film in a warm color, equilibrium angles in another, lattice points in a neutral, and the two Weaire-Phelan cell types each in their own.
