# Foam: Dividing Space with the Least Surface

I want seven short articles on one question: what is the least-wall way to divide space into cells of equal size? It is for curious readers comfortable with some math and new to crystallography. The plane has a proven answer, two bubbles have a proven answer, and filling three-dimensional space is still open: Kelvin's elegant guess stood for 106 years, then lost by about 0.3% to a two-cell foam found in 1993. The first four articles climb to that foam. The last three follow it outward: the same arrangement in metal, ice and soft matter, the lattice recipe that proposes foams in any dimension (stopping at four), and what a real foam does over time. A reader should finish knowing Plateau's laws by feel, why the hexagon wins in the plane, why Kelvin's flat cell cannot be the answer as drawn, what the Weaire-Phelan foam is and why it keeps turning up, why packing and partition are different contests, and why a bubble's fate in a flat foam depends on its number of sides.

The series index opens with a real Voronoi strip whose seed points slide from random to a triangular lattice, so the cells go from froth to honeycomb across the page.

## Articles

### 1. The Honeycomb
In the plane three films always meet at 120 degrees, and the regular hexagonal grid spends the least wall per unit area; Hales' honeycomb theorem proves it beats every equal-area partition. The signature figure relaxes a random froth step by step (Lloyd relaxation of Voronoi cells) and shows the three-way forks opening toward 120 degrees, the side counts bunching around six (the mean is six from the start, by Euler), and the wall cost falling toward the hexagon's value. Smaller figures rank the three regular tilings by wall cost and show three pentagons around a vertex leaving a gap, a frustration that returns in 3D, where foam cells are mostly pentagons.

### 2. The Double Bubble
One volume gives the sphere: a figure compares shapes enclosing the same area and their perimeters, and the prose gives the cube's and tetrahedron's surface excess over the sphere at equal volume. Two volumes give the familiar double bubble. The signature figure is a double bubble in cross-section with a volume-ratio slider, built from the 120-degree condition, whose middle wall is flat only when the volumes are equal. The history: the equal-volume case by a computer-assisted proof in 1995 (Hass, Hutchings and Schlafly), the general case in 2000, published 2002 (Hutchings, Morgan, Ritoré and Ros) with no computer. It closes on a non-standard competitor, one region wrapped as a torus around the other, of the kind the proof had to rule out.

### 3. Kelvin's Bubble
Kelvin's 1887 cell is the truncated octahedron, the territory of one point of the body-centered cubic lattice. The reader turns the cell over, then checks Plateau's two rules in 3D (120 degrees along an edge, the tetrahedral angle where four edges meet) and sees the flat cell break both: its faces meet at 109.47 and 125.26 degrees, so they must curve. A last figure compares dimensionless surface area for the flat cell, the relaxed cell and the 1993 Weaire-Phelan foam that beats it, on a zoomed axis.

### 4. The Counterexample
Weaire and Phelan's foam has two cell shapes of equal volume, a dodecahedron and a fourteen-faced cell, eight to a repeat. The article builds it from eight points in a cube (each point takes the region nearest to it), shows that those raw cells are unequal and that the walls must shift slightly to equalize them, and argues from Plateau's corner angle why cells of pentagons with a few hexagons are the thing to aim for. The signature figure relaxes Kelvin's foam and this one side by side in the page with a small soap-film relaxer (triangulated walls moved downhill in area at fixed cell volumes), plotting wall cost per step against the published values and drawing the cells as they bend. A companion figure shows every junction angle closing on Plateau's values. Other figures turn the two cells over, pull one repeat apart (and show the three perpendicular families of columns), and cut a flat slice through the foam at any tilt, which is the story of the Beijing Water Cube. It says plainly what is proved (it beats Kelvin's arrangement) and what is not (optimality).

### 5. Why It Recurs
The eight points were known before 1993 as a metal structure and as the cage framework of gas hydrates, and later as a liquid crystal of soft molecular balls. The common cause is that regular tetrahedra almost fill space: five around an edge leave a small gap. The signature figure closes five tetrahedra around an edge and shows the gap and what it takes to close it. Another shows one site with its cell and its shell of neighbors as one object read two ways, for 12, 14 and 16 neighbors, with the tetrahedra around each bond equal to the sides of the wall it crosses. A chart compares the mix of cell types in several structures against the ideal average, and shows that being near the ideal count does not make the cheapest foam. A last figure reads one cube as atoms at cell centers or water molecules at cell corners, and slides the corners to the measured positions in a real hydrate. The soft-matter section gives the proposed reason (soft coats that pay for shared wall area) and its limit.

### 6. The Lattice Recipe
Any lattice becomes a foam when each point takes its nearest region, and the same cell also scores the lattice as a packing of balls. In the plane one lattice wins both contests: a figure slides through a family of plane lattices and both curves turn at the triangular one. In space they split: a one-parameter family passing through face-centered cubic, simple cubic and body-centered cubic shows the densest packing at one end and the cheapest foam at the other, with the cell and its largest inscribed ball drawn so the reader can see which walls the ball touches. The checkerboard description of the face-centered lattice then carries the recipe to four dimensions, where the cell turns out regular: the 24-cell. The signature figure sets the 3D cell beside the projected 4D one and lets the reader turn the 4D cell until pyramid tips land where cube corners were. A final chart scores lattices in two, three and four dimensions on both contests at once. The series stops at four dimensions.

### 7. Foams in Motion
A foam left alone coarsens: gas leaks through the films from high pressure to low, small bubbles vanish, and the rest grow. In a flat foam the rate of change of a bubble's area depends only on its number of sides, with six as the balance point. The article derives that by walking once around a bubble and counting the turning at its corners, with a figure of the symmetric n-sided bubble and its turning budget. The signature figure is a live simulated foam, pre-run to a telling state, with neighbor swaps and vanishing bubbles marked as they happen; a second figure measures each bubble's rate of area change in that same simulation against the predicted line, and a third tracks mean bubble area and total film length over the run. It closes on grain growth in metals, the exact three-dimensional law, and why coarsening is not a search for the optimal foams of the earlier articles.

## What to get right

- Wall cost counts each shared wall once, split between its two cells; state that convention beside the hexagon's number, and keep "least perimeter" distinct from "most circular cell".
- Check every isoperimetric ratio by computing it (the cube matching a unit sphere's volume has about 24% more surface, the regular tetrahedron about 49%).
- The smaller bubble is at higher pressure, so the middle wall bulges into the larger one. Prose and figure must agree on this.
- Weaire-Phelan is the best known foam and nobody has proved it optimal. Its two cell types have equal volume, and the nearest-point cells of its eight points do not; the equalizing step has to be shown, not skipped.
- Surface-area values (Kelvin relaxed about 5.306, Weaire-Phelan about 5.288) should be computed in the page and set beside the published values, each labelled as which. A coarse mesh sits slightly above the true value; say so, and check the relaxer by refining the mesh and extrapolating. Plot on an honestly labelled zoomed axis.
- Bending of relaxed cells is small. If a drawing exaggerates it, say by how much and offer the true shape.
- Published accounts of the Water Cube say the foam was rotated and sliced but do not give the orientation. Let the reader tilt the cut; do not claim to reproduce the facade.
- The foam and the crystal share a network, not measurements. The foam bends walls to meet Plateau's angles; ice moves corners until bonds are equal; neither is a scaled copy of the other.
- Frank and Kasper singled out shells of 12, 14, 15 and 16 neighbors. Draw a shell only from a real structure's coordinates. Date the soft-matter results from the papers themselves, and keep the superconductivity of some of these alloys out of the argument.
- Packing and partition agree in the plane and split in three dimensions; do not state the split as a general law. Among the four-dimensional lattices a reader is likely to try, one wins both.
- The lattice recipe gives candidates, not optima: the best known 3D foam is not a lattice foam. Report the state of proofs about lattice foams and about four dimensions from the current literature, with preprints named as preprints.
- The coarsening simulation must not have the n minus 6 law or the 120-degree corners written into its rules, or measuring them shows nothing. Test it against lone bubbles of each side count, conservation of area, and a mean of exactly six sides before building figures on it.
- The n minus 6 form is for flat foams only. State the three-dimensional law as its authors do, and keep neighbor swaps and vanishing bubbles distinct.
- One color language throughout: film in a warm color, equilibrium angles in another, lattice points in a neutral, and the two Weaire-Phelan cell types each in their own.
