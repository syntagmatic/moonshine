# Foam: Dividing Space with the Least Surface

I want a seven-article series on one question asked in dimension after dimension: what is the least-area way to divide space into cells of equal size? It is for curious readers comfortable with some math and new to crystallography. The plane has a proven answer, two bubbles have a proven answer, and filling three-dimensional space is still open: Kelvin's elegant guess stood for a century, then lost by about 0.3% to a two-cell structure that also turns up in alloys, gas-trapping ice and self-assembling polymers. The refrain is that packing and partition are different problems with different winners. A reader should finish knowing Plateau's laws by feel, what the Weaire-Phelan structure is and why it recurs, and how a real foam evolves by an exact law.

Two acts, four and three, stopping at four dimensions on purpose. Articles 1 to 3 exist; 4 to 7 are still to be built.

## Articles

**Act I: The least-area problem**

### 1. The Honeycomb
In the plane three films always meet at 120 degrees, and the regular hexagonal grid spends the least wall per unit area; Hales' honeycomb theorem proves it beats every equal-area partition. The reader relaxes a random froth step by step and watches junctions settle toward 120-degree forks, the mean side count go to six, and the wall cost fall toward the hexagon's value. Smaller figures rank the regular tilings and show pentagons failing to tile, a frustration that returns in 3D.

### 2. The Double Bubble
One volume gives the sphere; two give the familiar double bubble, proved optimal only in 2002. The signature figure is a double bubble in cross-section with a volume-ratio slider, built from the 120-degree condition, whose middle wall is flat only when the volumes are equal. It closes on a non-standard competitor (one region wrapped as a torus around the other) of the kind the proof had to rule out.

### 3. Kelvin's Bubble
Kelvin's 1887 cell is the truncated octahedron, the territory of one point of the body-centered cubic lattice, with faces relaxed to obey the 3D Plateau laws. The reader turns the cell over, then checks Plateau's two rules (120 degrees along an edge, the tetrahedral angle where four edges meet) and sees the flat cell break both, so its faces must curve. A last figure compares surface area for the flat cell, the relaxed cell and the 1993 structure that beats it.

### 4. The Counterexample
In 1993 Weaire and Phelan, using Brakke's Surface Evolver, found a foam of two equal-volume cell types, pentagonal dodecahedra and fourteen-faced cells in a fixed ratio, that beats Kelvin by about 0.3%. This is the series' peak: Kelvin and Weaire-Phelan side by side with their surface areas and the gap, an exploded view of the repeating unit, and a slice at the angle that became the facade of Beijing's Water Cube.

**Act II: Recurrence, reach and motion**

### 5. Why It Recurs
The same geometry is the cage set of type-I clathrate hydrate (methane in ice), the A15 structure of beta-tungsten and Cr3Si, and a soft-matter phase of block copolymers. All of them pack tetrahedral neighborhoods as evenly as possible, which can almost but never quite be done. The reader flips one point set between packed spheres and its Voronoi foam, and browses Frank-Kasper coordination shells.

### 6. The Lattice Recipe and the 24-Cell
Any lattice gives a foam: take each point's Voronoi cell. The reader picks a lattice, watches the foam rebuild, and sees it scored twice, as a sphere packing and as a foam, with face-centered cubic winning one and body-centered cubic the other. One rung up, the D4 lattice gives the self-dual 24-cell, shown rotating, a shape with no 3D counterpart.

### 7. Foams in Motion
Real foam coarsens as gas diffuses from small, high-pressure bubbles into large ones, and in 2D von Neumann's law says a bubble's area changes at a rate set only by its number of sides: fewer than six shrink, more grow. The reader watches a live 2D foam colored by side count lose its small bubbles and swap neighbors, beside a plot of growth rate against sides that is a straight line through six.

## What to get right

- Wall cost counts each shared edge once; state that convention beside the hexagon's number, and keep "least perimeter" distinct from "most circular cell".
- The smaller bubble is at higher pressure, so the middle wall bulges into the larger one. Prose and figure must agree on this.
- Weaire-Phelan is the best known foam and nobody has proved it optimal; the 24-cell is likewise only a candidate. Both Weaire-Phelan cell types have equal volume. Its link to A15 is shared topology, so don't call the two metrically identical.
- Surface-area values (Kelvin about 5.306, Weaire-Phelan about 5.288) should be published Surface Evolver results or computed from a formula, labelled as which. Plot them as points on an honestly labelled zoomed axis; bars on a truncated axis exaggerate the gap.
- The n minus 6 law holds for 2D dry foams only; 3D needs MacPherson and Srolovitz. Keep neighbor swaps and bubble disappearances distinct.
- One color language throughout: film in a warm color, equilibrium angles in another, and the two Weaire-Phelan cell types in two fixed colors from article 4 on. Kelvin's relaxation has so far only been described; showing it, even approximately, would help.
