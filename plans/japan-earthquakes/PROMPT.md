# Japan Earthquakes

Made with Daniel Overstreet. Keep that credit on the series index and wherever the series is listed.

I want a seven-article series on Japan's earthquakes for a science-literate reader who lives in Japan and has felt these quakes, written in English with a full Japanese translation the reader can switch to. Japan sits where four tectonic plates meet, and the series works from real data: mostly the complete USGS catalog of magnitude 4.5 and larger earthquakes around Japan from 2000 through 2025, about seventeen thousand events. The through-line runs from where the earthquakes strike, to why, to how they cluster in time and how often the big ones come, and then to what follows from them for people: history, tsunamis and early warning.

By the end the reader should be able to look at a map of dots and read the subducting slabs in it, know what the Gutenberg-Richter law does and doesn't predict, and understand what an early warning can and can't buy them.

## Articles

### 1. Where the Earth Cracks Open
Twenty-six years of earthquakes on a map colored by depth, linked to a timeline. The reader brushes a region on the map to filter the timeline and brushes a time span to filter the map, and sees the clusters line up along trenches, with the deep events concentrated under Izu-Bonin. The map covers the whole catalog box and keeps every quake, draws plate boundaries from Bird (2003) and, on a checkbox, Slab2 depth contours in the dots' depth colors, and brushing it recounts the timeline. The timeline and a heatmap of depth band by month (0-70, 70-150, 150-300, 300+ km, shaded against each band's typical month) count M5 and up, because the catalog records M4.5-4.9 more completely after about 2010; a button restores M4.5, and one paragraph says why. The heatmap shows shallow quakes bursting, with March 2011 alone holding 13.5% of them, while deep ones arrive at a steady rate, tested against Poisson scatter at both cutoffs. Bands split at 70 km, not 30, because the catalog fixes uncertain depths at 33 or 10 km. A second figure asks how close each quake is to a plate: the distribution of 3D distance to the nearest Slab2 surface by depth band against a random-position control drawn over twenty seeds (72% within 30 km against 13%; default-depth events separate; a 2018-on holdout after Slab2's data), with a draggable rule, and independent evidence from GCMT mechanisms that follows the brushes (on the plate mostly thrust; seaward, the subducting side of the nearest trench, mostly normal), checked against scipy and numpy references. A button lights on the map the free-depth quakes beyond the rule. The text names where this misses (the 2015 Bonin M7.8 far from any slab, crustal Noto and Kumamoto, default depths, Slab2's own use of hypocentres), says a quiet plate boundary can mean it is locked, and computes Nankai recurrence from the historical list.

### 2. Tectonic Plate Geometry of Japan
Why the four-plate junction produces these clusters, tested against the USGS Slab2 model rather than asserted. The reader clicks transects across a map of plate boundaries, Slab2 depth contours (Pacific solid, Philippine Sea dashed, in the shared depth ramp) and Holocene volcanoes, each on a checkbox, and sees each cross-section in depth with the Slab2 top surface and its published uncertainty drawn under the catalog dots, plate names anchored on the line, computed dip labels and a computed readout. Sections follow the Japan Trench, the Nankai Trough, the Kanto triple junction, Izu-Bonin (a fourth transect along 28.5N, where the slab reaches 683 km), and the difference between shallow and deep danger. Cross-sections are drawn at true scale so their dips compare. The text says that most events at 70 km or deeper (85%) lie inside the slab, below its top, and farther inside with depth; that dip is read from the model, not from the dots; that plate age does not set the angle (the same plate dips 28 and 62 degrees at similar ages) although the young Philippine Sea slab goes quiet at 140 km where Slab2 follows it to 352 km; that volcano depths, about 100 km over the slab, are an independent check; and where the model misses (the 2015 Bonin events outside the slab, transect C's events above it, circularity). A whole-catalog depth histogram (log counts, marks at 410 and 660 km), brushable onto the map, shows the lull at 250-300 km, the second peak at 350-450 km and the cutoff at the base of the transition zone, and the text explains why deep earthquakes stop there. Published values (ages, plate rate, HERP's two Nankai estimates given side by side, Tohoku slip) come from a data file with citations.

### 3. When the Ground Won't Stop Shaking
The whole catalog animated in time, with speed and minimum-magnitude controls and a running sparkline of cumulative seismic energy. The reader watches aftershock sequences burst and fade and sees Tohoku dominate the energy total, which motivates why the magnitude scale is logarithmic. Map dots are coloured by depth like every other map. A log-log figure of the Tohoku aftershock box, linked both ways to the map's clock, counts M5 and up, because the catalog's M4.5-4.9 counts per M5+ event rise from about 2.5 before 2010 to about 4.4 from 2012 while M5+ stays near 110 a year (the map keeps every quake). A temporal ETAS model, fitted by maximum likelihood and checked against Ogata's own SAPP software and simulated catalogs, shows about a quarter of the box's M5+ events are background; the ETAS curve and background sit over the binned rates with the catalog's incomplete first hours shaded and not scored, and cutoff buttons show the plain Omori c shrinking as that gap is excluded. How long the sequence lasts is stated as the share of the box's M5+ events descended from the mainshock by year, not as a crossing time. The text names the misses: a branching ratio of 1.00, a modest goodness-of-fit failure, the M4.5 fit reading the catalog's rise as triggering, and a model with time but no space. Bath's law and the far-field M6 events after Tohoku are computed from the catalog.

### 4. How Often the Big Ones Come
The Gutenberg-Richter law fitted live to the catalog. The reader drags the cutoff magnitude, which starts at M5.0 where the two halves of the catalog count alike, and watches the maximum-likelihood b-value and the predicted count of M7+ events change against the observed count, with a b-stability cutoff marked and a strip showing where magnitude types switch. Small multiples then compare fitted b-values across nine regions that cover 99% of the catalog (Izu-Bonin included, Noto 2024 inside Chubu), with their uncertainty. A column chart of M6+ earthquakes per year against a Poisson band at the fitted rate, with a Gardner-Knopoff mainshocks-only series and a brushable training window whose forecast of the held-out M7+ count carries an exact Poisson 95% range, shows what the law does and doesn't predict: the long-run rate fits, single years don't (2011, whose window miss the text names), and a ten-year fit can forecast anywhere from about 0.6 to 2.2 M7+ a year. The text says declustering lowers b partly as an artifact. A last figure tests the 26-year fit against the USGS catalog for 1920-1999 in three eras (low by about a quarter at M7, high at M8.5+), adds a tapered law with a corner-magnitude profile (95% lower bound about M8.7, open above) and the Earthquake Research Committee's per-source mean intervals as floors, and ends by saying 26 years cannot bound the largest earthquake. The fits are checked against seismostats, OpenQuake and scipy.

### 5. Thirteen Centuries of Earthquakes
Japan's written record from 684 to 2024, all from NOAA's Japan list. A running-count chart first shows how much of the past the record misses: magnitude 7+ and 8+ earthquakes against the 20th-century rate projected back to 684, with a damaging-only view. Then every earthquake NOAA credits with more than 100 deaths (a count, or its 101-1,000 or over-1,000 range; same-day regional splits merged), about ninety, on a timeline whose year axis is split into differently scaled blocks, a magnitude-versus-deaths scatter over every record with at least 10 deaths (the page says the chart cannot separate location, depth, buildings, time of day and tsunami), and a map with a Ryukyu inset. A script applies the rule and writes the data; the page states the rule. A fifth figure shows how the official 30-year probability of the next Nankai Trough earthquake is built and how sensitive it is, as a method and not a forecast: renewal models (Brownian passage time, a time-predictable fit, a seeded Monte Carlo over the uplift errors and a flat-prior grid posterior, checked against scipy and HERP's published numbers) are run on HERP's nine Nankai earthquakes, whose dates come from NOAA records, and drawn as probability against evaluation year beside HERP's published values. The history chosen (HERP's cases I-V) and the model each move the 30-year probability across a wide range, and the page says its Monte Carlo is a stand-in for HERP's Bayesian model. A Japan Trench toggle runs the same method on the tsunami-deposit sequence for the day before 2011, so the Jogan precedent is quantified, and the text does not say 2011 was due. The reader follows the Jogan precedent and what each disaster changed.

### 6. Tsunami Propagation
How the 2011 Tohoku earthquake sent waves across the Pacific. It opens on a model of how the earthquake made the tsunami, along one line through the epicentre across the Japan Trench on the real seafloor (ETOPO1): Okada dislocation for the seafloor movement, Kajiura smoothing for the initial sea surface, and the linear shallow-water equations for the first hour, shown as linked panels on one distance axis (sea surface, seafloor movement, the fault in section with catalog quakes near the line, and a distance-by-time plot with two gauge traces). The reader picks slip, depth, width and dip or one of three same-magnitude presets (slip peaking at the trench, spread evenly, moved down the fault under the shelf) and can add the horizontal push of the sloping seafloor. The 2011 preset is the model's best fit to the measured 2011 ground and seafloor movement, which is marked on the figure, together with the Miyagi-Central GPS buoy's peak; the text says where the model misses. The reader sets ocean depth and sees wave speed and predicted arrival times at Hilo and Crescent City beside the observed ones, watches the wavefront cross the ocean, and sees shoaling turn an invisible deep-water wave into a destructive coastal one. On the Sanriku coast the same model runs on a map (ETOPO1 at 1 arc-minute, the first 45 minutes, sea surface or highest sea surface) beside the six GPS buoys moored off the coast, each with its modeled trace against its digitized record and published peak. Two sources: one fitted to the six buoys and the measured seafloor movement (a longer fault with less slip at the trench), and the cross-section's, which overshoots at Miyagi-Central even on the map, so the text says the line's miss came from the source, not from the wave being unable to spread along the coast; it also says what the fitted source still misses offshore and at a deep-ocean DART buoy. Wavefront rings are great-circle distances, so they agree with their labels. A scatter of the real 2011 first arrivals at NOAA's DART buoys and tide gauges against distance, on the same depth slider, lets the gap show: open-ocean buoys ride one depth (about 5,300 m), while coastal gauges arrive over an hour late after the slow crossing of shelf water. It ends with why only some fault types make tsunamis.

### 7. Earthquake Early Warning
Warning comes from the gap between the fast P wave and the damaging S wave. The reader moves a distance slider and watches both wavefronts expand past cities, timed with JMA's own travel-time table (checked against an independent ray tracer through JMA's velocity model), with the remaining warning time at each, then follows the timeline of the 2011 alert with JMA's recorded detection, reports and warning drawn on it. Warning has one definition everywhere: S arrival minus an alert issued at the second-detecting station plus a processing delay, on a 20 km station grid over land; the delay is fitted to JMA's published warning records (the median and quartiles become the three choices) rather than set by hand, and the S-P gap and the warning are shown as different numbers. A range plot of modeled warning across the strongly shaken land of every damaging M6.5+ quake since public warnings began, one row per quake and clickable onto the map, carries a second column with the model's alert beside JMA's first report and first warning, and shows how much land gets no warning. The text says where the model misses (Noto 2024's warning before the mainshock, the offshore cable detection off Aomori, the wait for a magnitude off Fukushima, quakes with no warning on record), corrects the common claim that the first 2011 estimate was M7.2 (it was M4.3, with M7.2 at the warning), names its shaking model, and says which way its point-source simplification biases the result. One sentence, not a chart, says what protective actions fit in a few seconds.

## What to get right

- The catalog is real USGS data, fetched from the USGS earthquake catalog for a fixed box and date range, and every count, fit and prediction in the prose is computed from it in the page. Nothing is hand-typed. The historical list comes from the NOAA NCEI significant earthquake database, one source for every value, and events NOAA lacks are left out. Plate boundaries come from a published model such as Bird (2003).
- Use USGS magnitudes for modern events (Tohoku M9.1, Kumamoto M7.0, Noto M7.5), noting the JMA value once where it differs. Fit b-values by maximum likelihood; a least-squares fit to cumulative counts is biased.
- Facts drafts get wrong: Tokyo received no public warning in 2011 (the alert went to five Tohoku prefectures); the S-P gap grows about 12 seconds per 100 km; the deepest slab events sit near 680 km, at the base of the transition zone; Tohoku's run-up reached about 40 m; the Nankai 30-year probability was revised in 2025, so check the current official figure. Check tsunami arrival times against observations and let the gap show.
- Depth color runs from red for shallow through magenta and purple to blue for deep on every map, one shared ramp with no pale middle. The index introduces the shindo intensity scale and its palette, which is how Japan actually rates shaking.
- The Japanese translation covers all prose, captions and labels, and a native speaker should review it.
- Catalog completeness: the USGS catalog records M4.5-4.9 more completely after about 2010 (M4.5-4.9 per M5+ event rises while M5+ per year stays flat). Fits, rates and counts use M5 and up wherever the step matters, computed in the page; each page says why once, and maps keep every quake.
- Every model is checked against an independent reference (a published table, another implementation such as scipy, seismostats or Ogata's software, or held-out observations). The model lives in a shared library with its checks, runnable outside the page, and the data it needs is a file the page loads and passes in.
