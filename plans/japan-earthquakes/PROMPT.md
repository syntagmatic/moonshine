# Japan Earthquakes

Made with Daniel Overstreet. Keep that credit on the series index and wherever the series is listed.

I want a seven-article series on Japan's earthquakes for a science-literate reader who lives in Japan and has felt these quakes, written in English with a full Japanese translation the reader can switch to. Japan sits where four tectonic plates meet, and the series works from real data: mostly the complete USGS catalog of magnitude 4.5 and larger earthquakes around Japan from 2000 through 2025, about seventeen thousand events. The through-line runs from where the earthquakes strike, to why, to how they cluster in time and how often the big ones come, and then to what follows from them for people: history, tsunamis and early warning.

By the end the reader should be able to look at a map of dots and read the subducting slabs in it, know what the Gutenberg-Richter law does and doesn't predict, and understand what an early warning can and can't buy them.

## Articles

### 1. Where the Earth Cracks Open
Twenty-six years of earthquakes on a map colored by depth, linked to a timeline. The reader brushes a region on the map to filter the timeline and brushes a time span to filter the map, and sees the clusters line up along trenches, with the deep events concentrated under Izu-Bonin. The map covers the whole catalog box, and brushing it recounts the timeline. Under the timeline, a heatmap of depth band by month (0-70, 70-150, 150-300, 300+ km, shaded against each band's typical month) shows shallow quakes bursting, with March 2011 alone holding 13.5% of them, while deep ones arrive at a steady rate. Bands split at 70 km, not 30, because the catalog fixes uncertain depths at 33 or 10 km.

### 2. Tectonic Plate Geometry of Japan
Why the four-plate junction produces these clusters. The reader clicks transects across a map of plate boundaries and catalog events and sees each cross-section in depth, where the dots themselves trace the sinking slab down to the base of the transition zone. Sections follow the Japan Trench, the Nankai Trough, the Kanto triple junction, Izu-Bonin (a fourth transect along 28.5N, where the slab reaches 683 km), and the difference between shallow and deep danger. Cross-sections are drawn at true scale so their dips compare. A whole-catalog depth histogram (log counts, marks at 410 and 660 km), brushable onto the map, shows the lull at 250-300 km, the second peak at 350-450 km and the cutoff at the base of the transition zone, and the text explains why deep earthquakes stop there.

### 3. When the Ground Won't Stop Shaking
The whole catalog animated in time, with speed and minimum-magnitude controls and a running sparkline of cumulative seismic energy. The reader watches aftershock sequences burst and fade and sees Tohoku dominate the energy total, which motivates why the magnitude scale is logarithmic. Map dots are coloured by depth like every other map. A log-log Omori-Utsu figure, fitted by maximum likelihood to the Tohoku aftershock box and linked both ways to the map's clock, shows the rate decaying with p near 1 and staying above the 2001-2010 background for about seven years, so aftershock sequences last years, not weeks.

### 4. How Often the Big Ones Come
The Gutenberg-Richter law fitted live to the catalog. The reader drags the cutoff magnitude and watches the maximum-likelihood b-value and the predicted count of M7+ events change against the observed count, with a strip showing where magnitude types switch. Small multiples then compare fitted b-values across nine regions that cover 99% of the catalog (Izu-Bonin included, Noto 2024 inside Chubu), with their uncertainty. A column chart of M6+ earthquakes per year against a Poisson band at the fitted rate, with a Gardner-Knopoff mainshocks-only series and a brushable training window, shows what the law does and doesn't predict: the long-run rate fits, single years don't (2011), and a ten-year fit can forecast anywhere from about 0.6 to 2.2 M7+ a year.

### 5. Thirteen Centuries of Earthquakes
Japan's written record from 684 to 2024, all from NOAA's Japan list. A running-count chart first shows how much of the past the record misses: magnitude 7+ and 8+ earthquakes against the 20th-century rate projected back to 684, with a damaging-only view. Then every earthquake NOAA credits with more than 100 deaths (a count, or its 101-1,000 or over-1,000 range; same-day regional splits merged), about ninety, on a timeline whose year axis is split into differently scaled blocks, a magnitude-versus-deaths scatter over every record with at least 10 deaths, and a map with a Ryukyu inset. A script applies the rule and writes the data; the page states the rule. The reader hovers each event for its story, sees how weakly magnitude predicts deaths, and follows the Jogan precedent and what each disaster changed.

### 6. Tsunami Propagation
How the 2011 Tohoku earthquake sent waves across the Pacific. It opens on a model of how the earthquake made the tsunami, along one line through the epicentre across the Japan Trench on the real seafloor (ETOPO1): Okada dislocation for the seafloor movement, Kajiura smoothing for the initial sea surface, and the linear shallow-water equations for the first hour, shown as linked panels on one distance axis (sea surface, seafloor movement, the fault in section with catalog quakes near the line, and a distance-by-time plot with two gauge traces). The reader picks slip, depth, width and dip or one of three same-magnitude presets (slip peaking at the trench, spread evenly, moved down the fault under the shelf) and can add the horizontal push of the sloping seafloor. The 2011 preset is the model's best fit to the measured 2011 ground and seafloor movement, which is marked on the figure, together with the Miyagi-Central GPS buoy's peak; the text says where the model misses. The reader sets ocean depth and sees wave speed and predicted arrival times at Hilo and Crescent City beside the observed ones, watches the wavefront cross the ocean, and sees shoaling turn an invisible deep-water wave into a destructive coastal one. Wavefront rings are great-circle distances, so they agree with their labels. A scatter of the real 2011 first arrivals at NOAA's DART buoys and tide gauges against distance, on the same depth slider, lets the gap show: open-ocean buoys ride one depth (about 5,300 m), while coastal gauges arrive over an hour late after the slow crossing of shelf water. It ends with why only some fault types make tsunamis.

### 7. Earthquake Early Warning
Warning comes from the gap between the fast P wave and the damaging S wave. The reader moves a distance slider and watches both wavefronts expand past cities, with the remaining warning time at each, then follows the timeline of the 2011 alert and sees what protective actions fit into a few seconds. Warning has one definition everywhere: S arrival minus an alert issued at the second-detecting station plus a processing delay the reader can set, on a 20 km station grid over land, so the S-P gap and the warning are shown as different numbers. A range plot of modeled warning across the strongly shaken land of every damaging M6.5+ quake in the catalog, one row per quake and clickable onto the map, shows how much land gets no warning and how much that depends on the processing delay. The page names its shaking model and says which way its point-source simplification biases the result.

## What to get right

- The catalog is real USGS data, fetched from the USGS earthquake catalog for a fixed box and date range, and every count, fit and prediction in the prose is computed from it in the page. Nothing is hand-typed. The historical list comes from the NOAA NCEI significant earthquake database, one source for every value, and events NOAA lacks are left out. Plate boundaries come from a published model such as Bird (2003).
- Use USGS magnitudes for modern events (Tohoku M9.1, Kumamoto M7.0, Noto M7.5), noting the JMA value once where it differs. Fit b-values by maximum likelihood; a least-squares fit to cumulative counts is biased.
- Facts drafts get wrong: Tokyo received no public warning in 2011 (the alert went to five Tohoku prefectures); the S-P gap grows about 12 seconds per 100 km; the deepest slab events sit near 680 km, at the base of the transition zone; Tohoku's run-up reached about 40 m; the Nankai 30-year probability was revised in 2025, so check the current official figure. Check tsunami arrival times against observations and let the gap show.
- Depth color runs from red for shallow through magenta and purple to blue for deep on every map, one shared ramp with no pale middle. The index introduces the shindo intensity scale and its palette, which is how Japan actually rates shaking.
- The Japanese translation covers all prose, captions and labels, and a native speaker should review it.
