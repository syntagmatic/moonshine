# Sheaves

I want seven essays that explain sheaves to a working mathematician (someone who knows topology, linear algebra and a little category theory) by computing with them. The reader should come away seeing a sheaf as local data plus rules for how neighbours must agree, global sections as the answers that agree everywhere, and cohomology as the name for why local answers sometimes can't be glued. Then they should see that picture doing real work: sensor fusion, consensus and opinion dynamics, recovering orientations from noisy pairwise measurements, and graph neural networks that don't wash out their features.

The series starts with cellular sheaves on graphs and small cell complexes, where a sheaf is a pile of matrices and every figure can compute its answer live by linear algebra in the page. It ends with the classical sheaf of holomorphic germs, so the reader sees that the finite version was copying the classical one all along. The series does not re-derive simplicial cohomology; the cohomology series in this gallery does that, and these essays link to it where they lean on it.

## Articles

**Act I: Local to global**

### 1. Stalks and restriction maps
A cellular sheaf on a graph is a vector space on every vertex and edge and a linear map from each vertex to each edge it touches. Global sections are the kernel of one matrix. The reader edits a sheaf on a small graph (a triangle with a pendant edge: stalk dimensions of 1 or 2, restriction maps stepped through a palette of identities, signs, rotations, reflections, projections, inclusions and zero) and watches the coboundary matrix rebuild next to the graph, with dim H^0 from its rank and a basis section drawn at the vertices. The anchor example is a twisted sheaf of lines around a hexagon, drawn cut open at a chosen vertex: it looks like the constant sheaf at every vertex and edge, every proper arc has a section, and the whole loop has none. The disagreement lands on whichever edge closes the loop, so it has no location; rescaling a vertex's coordinate moves the twist without changing the holonomy, which is a complete isomorphism invariant for sheaves of lines with invertible maps. The poset view (a cell complex as a poset, a sheaf as a functor out of it, the Alexandrov topology and why "restriction" points from vertex to edge) is given in one paragraph for the category-minded reader.

### 2. When local sections don't glue
H^1 as the cokernel of the coboundary, and what a nonzero class means: a set of edge discrepancies no vertex assignment can explain. The reader drags prescribed edge values on a hexagon, a hexagon with a chord, or a tree, with or without a sign twist, and least squares shows the best vertex assignment and the residual, the harmonic representative of the class. The twisted hexagon has H^1 = 0 as well as H^0 = 0 (rank and nullity tie the two), while the chord keeps one loop's worth. H^1 of a tree vanishes when the restriction maps are surjective, as they are for every sheaf in the essay, but not in general: an edge stalk between zero vertex stalks is a tree with H^1 nonzero. Then the reader picks local sections on the pieces of a cover of the hexagon by open sets of the Alexandrov topology (three arcs, two arcs overlapping in two places, four arcs, three wide arcs with a triple overlap, or the whole hexagon as one piece), sees exactly which overlaps fail, projects onto the families that glue, and reads the Cech complex of the cover next to the cellular one. They agree for every cover except the one-piece cover of the constant sheaf, whose piece has H^1 of its own. Links to cohomology essays 1 and 5 for the constant-coefficient case.

**Act II: Sheaves at work**

### 3. Sensor fusion and the consistency radius
Overlapping sensors report local readings that don't quite agree. A sheaf encodes what each sensor measures and how overlapping measurements must relate. Robinson's assignments put values on edges as well as vertices, his consistency radius is the largest critical threshold over incidences, and with edge values left free it is half the largest coboundary gap in the sup norm. His distortion floor D(a, s) >= c(a)/(1 + K) holds for every global section; the sup-distance nearest section can fail to be unique, so the essay fuses by least squares, the orthogonal projection onto H^0. A made-up scene of four heterogeneous sensors locating a point (a radar in world coordinates, a camera in a frame turned by 35 degrees, two lines of position) shows every gap as a segment in the plane, the fused point against the true one, and the floor; knocking one sensor lights exactly the edges it shares. Then real data: the Intel Berkeley Research Lab motes, 7 to 9 March 2004, half-hourly median temperatures on the constant sheaf over Delaunay neighbours up to 8 m, gaps filled by harmonic extension. Mote 18 drifts and sticks near 122 C; the radius with it withheld stays at the room's gradient, and the reader can corrupt any mote and see its star light up, or fail to by day. The essay notes that vertex readings carry no H^1 class.

### 4. The sheaf Laplacian
The Laplacian L = delta^T delta turns a sheaf into a quadratic energy, the total squared disagreement across edges, and heat flow on it settles onto the orthogonal projection into H^0. The graph Laplacian is the constant sheaf. Figures, in order: a signed graph as a scalar sheaf, where heat flow splits the vertices into two factions exactly when the graph is balanced (Harary) and decays to zero otherwise; a rotation sheaf on a cycle with arrows at the vertices, where a holonomy dial decides whether the arrows settle into agreement or shrink to nothing, with a live log-energy trace whose slope is the smallest eigenvalue; the spectrum of the rotation cycle as a function of holonomy, computed by eigensolver and checked against the closed form 2 - 2cos((2 pi k +/- theta)/n); and angular synchronization, recovering forty unknown orientations from noisy relative angles on the edges of a random graph using the bottom eigenvectors of L, with sliders for noise and the fraction of corrupted edges that show both success and breakdown.

### 5. Opinion dynamics on discourse sheaves
Hansen and Ghrist's discourse sheaf: a vertex stalk is a person's private opinions, an edge stalk is what a pair discusses, and a restriction map is how that person expresses themselves in that conversation (exaggerate, understate, mix principles into a policy, stay silent, lie, differently per neighbour). The page builds three of the paper's dynamics. There is no heat flow on the expressed 1-cochains in the paper; "expression" there means the restriction maps. First, opinion diffusion (eq. 4.1, Thm 4.1): five people with R^2 opinion stalks and R edge stalks, each map drawn as an arrow in the person's square, with presets for one policy each (dim H^0 = 6, each person slides along their own arrow), everyone discussing topic 1 (the constant sheaf on that topic, topic 2 frozen), polite company (Example 4.2: C and D flip their arrows toward the other camp, balanced, stable polarized sections) and one lie on a loop (every stance goes to 0); the point is that a person only moves in the span of their own maps' transposes. Second, persuasion by stubborn agents (eq. 5.1, Thm 5.1, relative cohomology H^0(G, U; F), Thm 6.1, Example 6.3, reluctance in Sec. 7) on Zachary's karate club (unweighted 78 edges and the post-split clubs as distributed with NetworkX): pinning members 1 and 34 at +1 and -1 puts 33 of 34 on the side they joined, member 9 being the exception; the reader pins or releases members and can switch on polite lies across the 11 cross-faction edges, which makes the sheaf balanced and the endpoint an exact, fully polarized global section. Third, learning to lie on one edge: map diffusion (eq. 8.1, Thm 8.1, Frobenius-nearest sheaf, Example 8.2 with maps going to -3/17 and 12/17) and the joint flow (eq. 9.1, Example 9.5), drawn in the (opinion, map) plane, where the proof of Thm 9.3 gives a conserved alpha F_v^2 - beta x_v^2 per agent: an agent with beta x^2 > alpha F^2 can never change the sign of their opinion but may flip their map (lie), and the opposite agent can never lie but may change their mind; the conserved quantity says who can lie, not who will. Nonlinear, bounded-confidence and antagonistic dynamics (Secs. 10 to 12) are named, not built.

### 6. Neural sheaf diffusion
Graph neural networks average neighbours, which washes out features on heterophilic graphs, where linked nodes tend to differ; GCN is one Euler step of heat diffusion with the normalized graph Laplacian plus weights (Bodnar et al., eqs. 1 to 3). The normalized sheaf Laplacian D^{-1/2} L D^{-1/2} replaces it, and the paper's Section 3 asks which classes of sheaves have diffusion limits that separate the classes by a hyperplane (Def. 7). First figure: a two-class bipartite graph with equal sides (20 nodes, 28 edges, seeded), one scalar feature per node, traces against time on a symlog axis with the limit column and the best threshold; the graph Laplacian's limit is c sqrt(deg) and the threshold falls from 16 of 20 to 12 (Prop. 9, whose proof is the equal degree sums), while the sign flip (Prop. 10, -1 at the class-A end) ends at 20 of 20. On a bipartite graph the sign-flip sheaf is isomorphic to the constant sheaf (negate class B), so the spectra agree; the sheaf needs the classes to help. Second figure: plane stalks, three classes, every map out of a class-c node the rotation by c phi; path-independent, dim H^0 = 2, each class on its own ray (Lemma 6, Prop. 11, Prop. 13); a phi slider and an exact separability readout (outer classes free at any nonzero angle, the middle one from 65 deg on this seed, two classes collide at 180). Then the model: maps Phi(x_v, x_u) = sigma(V[x_v || x_u]), layer eq. 6 with eq. 55's (1 + eps), augmented normalization. Third figure trains four models in the page on the WebKB graphs (Texas, Cornell, Wisconsin; Geom-GCN files and ten fixed 48/32/20 splits): features only, plain diffusion (identity maps), scalar maps (tanh, d = 1, started at 0.9), rotations (d = 2), with diffusion-only layers by default (the paper's synthetic setting) and a toggle for the full layer. Diffusion-only on Texas: 83.0, 60.3, 81.6, 70.0 (largest class 58.9); full layer: all within noise. Fourth figure: the scalar maps collapse to zero (a disconnected sheaf, H^0 = C^0) on all three graphs, with or without weight decay; rotations turn more across class boundaries (median 50 vs 13 deg, 103 vs 54 deg on the first Texas split) but still lose to ignoring the graph. The paper's benchmark claim is named and not tested; its numbers are not reproduced.

**Act III: Where the idea came from**

### 7. Germs and analytic continuation
The sheaf of holomorphic functions on the plane, with the identity and gluing axioms stated as an exact equalizer sequence, and two presheaves that fail gluing (bounded holomorphic functions; constant functions, whose sheafification is the constant sheaf of essay 1). Stalks as colimits: a holomorphic germ is a convergent power series and the identity theorem makes it rigid. First figure: the flat function exp(-1/x), whose germ at 0 is not its zero Taylor series, with a draggable point, the Taylor coefficients from the exact derivative recursion, and the two sections of the space of germs drawn as strands that are one to the left of 0 and fork at 0 (a non-Hausdorff etale space). The etale space of O is Hausdorff, which makes continuation along a path unique. Second figure: drag the centre of a disk of convergence (radius |c|, always touching the branch point) round 0 for sqrt z, cbrt z and log z; each step sums the old 48-term series at a new centre at most 0.3 of the radius away and takes the other coefficients from the function's ODE (the library checks this against re-expanding the old series; re-expanding everything each step is unstable). The germ is shown as its first four coefficients, and a rotatable surface (height Re f for roots, arg z for log) carries the germ as a patch summed from its series and the path lifted by the germs' values; returning near the start draws a red segment between the germ you left with and the one you came back with (sqrt negated, cbrt times e^{2 pi i/3}, log plus 2 pi i per loop). Third figure: a ring of 3 to 8 disks round 0 with the local system of solutions of 2zf' = f, 3zf' = f or (zf')' = 0; clicking a disk switches its branch, overlaps are coloured by whether branches agree (the maps measured by continuing both branches to the overlap), and the nerve is drawn as a cellular sheaf whose H^0 and H^1 come from sheaf-math.js: sqrt on six disks is the twisted hexagon of essay 1, with the principal branch cut as the closing edge; log has a shear and H^0 = H^1 = C. Convex pieces make it a Leray cover. The match with essay 1: in the Alexandrov topology each vertex has a smallest open set, its star, so the stalk is F(v), and the gluing sequence for the star cover is exactly x -> delta x. The essay closes on history read in the primary sources: Leray's 1945 captivity course (Theoreme 12, constant coefficients, closed "simple" covers), the 1946 CRAS note that introduces "faisceau" on closed sets and announces the sheaf version, the 1950 memoir's Theoreme 49.2 with proof, and Cartan's seminar expose of 9 April 1951 that defines a sheaf as a local homeomorphism (credited to Lazard) and identifies a connected component of the holomorphic germ sheaf with an unramified Riemann surface. Weierstrass and Weyl are named as the standard lineage that these sources do not state. Cosheaves (Curry), persistence (Kashiwara-Schapira) and contextuality (Abramsky-Brandenburger) are named as directions not taken.

## What to get right

- Sign and orientation conventions: fix (delta x)_e = F_{v,e} x_v - F_{u,e} x_u for e = (u, v) once and use it everywhere. L does not depend on the orientation choice; delta does.
- H^0 = ker delta = ker L. Heat flow dx/dt = -Lx converges to the orthogonal projection of x(0) onto H^0, and the rate is set by the smallest nonzero eigenvalue. Figures that claim convergence compute it from an eigendecomposition, not from a canned animation.
- A rotation sheaf on a cycle has dim H^0 = 2 when the holonomy is 0 mod 2 pi and 0 otherwise. The holonomy is the product of the rotations around the loop, not any single edge.
- H^1 of a tree is zero only when the restriction maps are surjective (enough to solve outward from a root). Say so; do not claim it for every sheaf on a tree.
- Cech cohomology of a cover computes the sheaf's cohomology when every piece and every intersection is acyclic (Leray; stated with proof for closed covers in his 1950 memoir, Thm 49.2). Show a cover that breaks the hypothesis and gets the wrong answer, not only covers that work.
- Signed graph balance: H^0 is nonzero iff every cycle has an even number of negative edges. Show both outcomes.
- Angular synchronization breaks down gradually with noise and sharply with outliers. Report the error after the optimal global rotation, since a global rotation of the answer is still a section.
- Cite the applied papers for what they actually show (Hansen and Ghrist on spectral sheaf theory and discourse sheaves, Robinson on consistency radius and sensor integration, Bodnar et al. on neural sheaf diffusion, Singer on angular synchronization). Benchmark numbers come from the page's own runs, not from the papers' tables.
- Discourse sheaves: cite Hansen and Ghrist by equation and theorem, and say which statements are the page's own derivations (the per-agent conserved quantity and its lie/change-of-mind dichotomy come from the proof of their Thm 9.3, not its statement). The joint opinion-map flow converges, but can do so slowly, so figures integrate it rather than assume an exponential rate.
- Neural sheaf diffusion: benchmark numbers come from the page's own runs with one hand-fixed configuration shared by all models, never from the paper's Table 1, and the page says its small model does not test the paper's claim. Check what learned maps actually are before narrating them: here they went to zero, not negative. Edge homophily depends on the edge convention (Texas 0.061 undirected without self-loops, 0.108 over the raw directed lines with loops, 0.11 in the paper).
- Series colors: vertex stalk data in teal, restriction maps and edges in ochre, global sections and agreement in olive green, disagreement (delta x) in brick red, holonomy and twist in plum, fixed or observed values in ink slate. No default blue-purple pair.
- Germs: continuation figures compute by power series (only the starting value and the branch on each cover disk use a closed form); history claims come from Leray and Cartan as printed (sheaves on closed sets until Cartan's seminar; the etale space in Cartan's expose of 9 April 1951, credited to Lazard), and the Weierstrass/Weyl lineage is labelled as standard framing, not sourced.
