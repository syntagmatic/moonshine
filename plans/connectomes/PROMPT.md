# Connectomes

I want an eight-article series on connectomes, the wiring diagrams of nervous systems, for a technically literate reader: comfortable with linear algebra, graphs, probability and a little dynamical systems, but not a neuroscientist. Each article makes one argument and carries two figures (three in the last), and every figure computes what it shows at runtime from real, cited data.

The through-line is that a connectome is a measurement, not a blueprint. What a wiring diagram tells you depends on what was counted and how reliably, what you compare it against, and how you draw it; and even a perfect one leaves out much of what the animal does. The first two articles are about the measurement itself, the middle four about reading the graph (null models, wire cost, ordering, flow), and the last two about what the graph can and cannot settle.

C. elegans anchors the series: 302 neurons, both sexes, eight animals across development, small enough that every graph computation (null ensembles, eigenvectors, rankings, layouts) runs in the page. The larval fly, the adult fly mushroom body, the mouse visual cortex, human temporal cortex and macaque cortex come in where they are the best example. The adult male fly CNS appears only in article 2 (completeness) and article 8 (sex differences).

By the end the reader should be able to look at a published connectome figure or headline and ask the right three questions: what is an edge here, compared to what, and drawn how.

## Articles

### 1. What Gets Counted
The same word, connectome, names graphs whose edges mean different physical things, and their weights cannot be compared across methods.
- **One pathway, three rulers.** Mouse primary visual cortex (VISp) to its nine neighbouring higher visual areas, measured by diffusion MRI (Trinkle et al. 2021, five brains, undirected) and by Allen anterograde tracer projection density (every wild-type VISp injection, with the Knox et al. 2019 model beside them). Each modality on its own axis (units differ), joined by lines per target area so the reader sees where the rankings agree and cross; the reader toggles normalisation (raw, fraction of total, rank). Then the third ruler, which can't join the comparison: the MICrONS cubic millimetre contains only VISp, RL and AL, and its proofread VISp axons put a few percent of their synapses into those areas, mostly local wiring across the border. EM counts synapses exactly but sees only what fits in the block. Computes: each modality's values from its released data, the rank correlation between dMRI and tracer, and the per-axon share of synapses landing outside VISp.
- **What an edge is made of.** The H01 human cortex histogram of synapses per connection (log count axis): almost all connections are a single synapse, and a few axons make dozens onto one target. The reader slides the "edge exists if at least k synapses" threshold and watches the share of edges and the share of synapses kept diverge. Computes: both shares from the released connection counts.
The article ends on scale: H01's cubic millimetre took most of a year of imaging, and what that rate implies for a whole mouse brain.

### 2. How Far to Trust an Edge
A connectome is an estimate. Strong edges are reproducible and weak ones are often noise, and the errors are not symmetric between a neuron's inputs and outputs.
- **Does this edge exist in the next animal?** For every edge, its synapse count against the chance it reappears: across the eight Witvliet worms, and across the left and right hemispheres of FlyWire (and the hemibrain, as Schlegel et al. 2024 did). The reader picks a weight threshold and sees what fraction of the kept edges replicate and what fraction of replicating edges were thrown away. Computes: replication rates by weight bin from the edge tables.
- **Inputs nearly complete, outputs not.** In the male fly CNS about 94% of presynaptic sites and 42% of postsynaptic sites sit on traced bodies, because the lost fragments are thin postsynaptic twigs. So a traced neuron's input partners are mostly known while its output weights are undercounted by about 2.4 times. A model figure: take a real weight distribution, thin each synapse with the reader's completeness probability, and watch which edges fall below a threshold, and how an output-side ranking reshuffles. Computes: the binomial thinning, labelled as a model on real weights.
Tractography's version of the problem (Maier-Hein et al. 2017's phantom challenge: most true bundles found, alongside many more invalid ones) closes the article in prose.

### 3. Surprising Compared to What?
Every "brains are small-world / reciprocal / rich-club" claim is a comparison with a null model, and the answer depends on which null.
- **A ladder of nulls.** The C. elegans chemical graph (Varshney 2011, 279 neurons, undirected) and its clustering, path length and small-world index against four ensembles generated in the page: Erdős–Rényi, a distance-binned spatial null (cell-body distances), degree-preserving rewiring, and degree plus distance bins preserved. Dot strips per null with the observed value marked. The index falls from about 6 to 5 with distance and to about 2.3 with degree: degree, not geometry, takes most of the excess, and Newman's configuration-model formula gets the degree null's clustering from the degree sequence alone. The reader changes the number of distance bins: distance alone stays near 5 at any resolution, while the degree-and-distance null falls toward 1 as bins get finer, because it then keeps a large share of the real edges (a readout shows the share). Toggle for gap junctions. Computes: every ensemble in a web worker, the index for each, the share of real edges kept.
- **Motifs and the reciprocity trap.** The 13 connected triad classes of the directed worm graph as z-scores against a degree-preserving null and a degree-and-reciprocal-pairs null (Milo's). The worm has about 4 times the reciprocal pairs its degrees predict (Song et al. 2005 found about 4 times in rat cortex). Holding reciprocal pairs fixed flips 111D, 111U and 201 from enriched to depleted, and turns the feedforward loop from unremarkable (z about 2) to strongly enriched (z above 15), because the degree-only null breaks reciprocal pairs into spare one-way edges that form feedforward loops by chance. A synapse-count threshold slider and a detail panel with the selected class's counts under both nulls. Computes: the census and both ensembles.
The worm rich club (Towlson et al. 2013) is stated in prose with its degree-preserving null.

### 4. The Cost of Wire
Brains are cheap to wire but not as cheap as possible. Geometry predicts much of a connectome, and the residual is where the interest lies.
- **Where the neurons would sit.** Chen, Hall and Chklovskii's 2006 model on the 2011 wiring: fix sensory endings and muscles (WormAtlas's fixed-point table), charge every connection a power of its length, synapses and muscles divided by alpha = 29.3 synapses per wire, and solve for one position per neuron along the body (one linear solve at exponent 2; reweighted least squares otherwise). A scatter of model position against cell-body position with the anchors as strips above it; sliders for the exponent and alpha, a gap-junction toggle; drag a neuron to pin it anywhere and watch the rest re-settle. A side panel shows the selected neuron's partners at their model positions, its anchors, and its cost curve with the others held. Results to reproduce: mean error 9.7%, median 5.2% (paper 9.71%, 5.10%), random about 35%; best near exponent 2; the real layout costs 4.2 times the optimum, almost all of it in neuron-to-neuron wiring (5.8 times, against 1.4 for anchors), random about 16. AVG (a ventral-cord pioneer, cell body 0.22, model 0.71), PVP and PVQ are the clearest misses. The cost of pinning one neuron at its cell body is (a_i - x_i)^2 / (Q^-1)_ii, and the costliest are the command interneurons AVA, PVC and DVA; pinning the ten costliest takes the cost to 2.5 times the optimum and makes the other 269 predictions worse (8.4% to 9.3%), because the model puts synapses at cell bodies. Chen's shared-wire model (9.4%) is the fix, named in prose. Computes: the placement at every setting, pinned re-solves, the closed-form prices, actual and random costs.
- **The exponential distance rule.** Macaque FLNe (Markov et al. 2014, Cereb Cortex) against white-matter distance (Markov et al. 2014, J Comp Neurol, Table 2) for the 628 pathways into the 11 targets with published distances, log FLNe on the vertical axis; repeat injections averaged with zeros. A least-squares line and a reader's line whose slope (decay constant) is dragged or set by slider, starting at the published 0.188 per mm (Horvát et al. 2016 Table 1, from Ercsey-Ravasz et al. 2013's neuron-count fit); beside it a forest plot of the best decay constant per target with 95% intervals, rows selecting that target's pathways. Pooled: 0.16 per mm, e-fold 6.3 mm, R² 26%, residual 1.07 decades; a height per target 32%, a line per target 37%. Visual targets MT, V2, TEO fall fastest (0.25 to 0.30); frontal 8l and parietal 7A slowest (about 0.08). Don't read the residual against SLN: weak pathways have extreme SLN by chance. Computes: every fit, R², intervals.

### 5. Reading the Matrix
A connectivity matrix looks like whatever order you put its rows in, and some famous summary statistics are degree in disguise.
- **Ordering changes the picture.** The worm's directed chemical adjacency matrix under alphabetical, degree, cell-type, body-position, random and spectral (Fiedler vector of the unweighted undirected Laplacian) orders, animated between them; the order list doubles as a bar chart of mean edge span, beside a strip of each row's cell-body position and a cell-type strip. A random order averages (n + 1)/3 = 93 rows; labels get 62 to 80; spectral gets 33, and it is the head-to-tail axis (rank correlation about 0.78 with body position), not a sensory-to-motor one: the head's motor neurons sit at the head end. Where it disagrees with cell-body position it follows the wiring (ALN and PLN, tail cell bodies whose synapses are all with head neurons; AVA, toward the cord motor neurons it drives). The weighted unnormalised Laplacian localises on weakly attached neurons and loses the axis; normalising restores it. Computes: every ordering, its span, the eigendecomposition.
- **Controllability is degree.** Average controllability (Gu et al. 2015) against strength for every worm neuron (symmetrised chemical plus gap junctions), with the horizon, the scaled largest eigenvalue, weights and gap junctions as controls, beside a horizon-by-eigenvalue grid of the rank correlation. One step gives every node 1; two steps give 1 plus squared weights (degree exactly, unweighted); across the grid the ordinary correlation stays 0.8 to 0.93 and the rank correlation falls to about 0.6 only at long horizons with the eigenvalue near 1, where the score becomes eigenvector centrality. Computes: average controllability from the eigendecomposition of the scaled matrix, and both correlations.
The modularity resolution limit is shown on a ring of five-node cliques (pairs merge past 22 cliques, from the formula) and stated for the worm (sqrt(2L) about 63 edges).

### 6. Which Way Signals Flow
Rankings and cascades turn a recurrent graph into layers from sense to action; the layers are real but their boundaries depend on the model's settings.
- **SpringRank on the worm.** Every neuron placed at its SpringRank height (De Bacco, Larremore and Moore 2018, Eq. 3 and 5, solved by conjugate gradient) in three beeswarm columns, sensory, inter and motor; pick a neuron (click, list or arrow keys) to draw its inputs and outputs, ink when the synapses run down and blue when they run up. Beside it, the synapse-weighted histogram of sender minus receiver height, with the mean of 20 direction-shuffled networks (their significance null, which keeps A_ij + A_ji) as an outline. Controls: regularisation alpha (0 to 1,000) and gap junctions, which enter as a spring in each direction and so pull two neurons level. Results: 89% of synapses run down against 58% shuffled (energy per synapse 0.20 against 0.48); interneurons outrank motor neurons 94% of the time but sensory outrank inter only 71% (AIN and AIM, with almost no chemical input, near the top); the command interneurons AVA, AVB, AVE rank 171st to 187th, among the motor neurons; the head motor neurons RMD, SIA, SIB go with the cord motor neurons, the opposite of part 5's spectral order; VD and DD at the bottom (84% of their input from cord motor neurons, output to muscle not in the graph); 11% of synapses run up, heaviest AVA onto PVC and SMD and RMD back onto RIA. PLML (one synapse, no input) ranks first at alpha 0, 21st at 1, 85th at 3; at large alpha the ranking becomes each neuron's out-minus-in surplus, which sends 77% down and correlates about 0.6 with the full solve. Gap junctions squeeze the range from 4.2 to 3.1 and leave 88% down; DVB and PLML move most. Computes: the sparse solve at every setting, the shares, energies and shuffled networks.
- **Hops from each sense.** Winding et al. 2023's signal cascade through the axon-to-dendrite graph of the first-instar larval brain (Data S1, 2,952 of the 3,016 neurons, 63,545 connections), seeded from one of 12 modalities at a time (seed and output sets from the authors' CATMAID annotations), each synapse transmitting with p, each neuron active once, outputs (DN-VNC, DN-SEZ, RGN) stopping the signal, 1,000 runs and 8 hops, run in a worker. A modality-by-hop heatmap of brain neurons first reached at each hop (a neuron's hop is the first at which the share of runs reaching it passes the threshold), with never-reached counts and mean hop; clicking a row shows stacked bars of outputs and other neurons per hop. Sliders for p (reruns the worker) and the threshold (instant). At the published settings (p 0.05, half the runs): smell and taste reach brain neurons at a mean of 3.3 to 3.4 hops, proprioceptive, mechano II/III and respiratory at about 6; median hop to DN-VNC 4 against 6; 11.7% of reached brain neurons get one modality only (paper 12%). Across p 0.025 to 0.1 and thresholds 0.25 to 0.75 that share runs from 3% to 24%, while olfactory and both gustatory stay the three shallowest and respiratory, proprioceptive and mechano II/III among the four deepest. The paper's own lowest-order layering (21% second order, 56% third, 15% fourth, under 1% fifth) and its note that no pathway longer than five hops has been tested functionally close the article. Computes: the cascades, the layer assignments, the unimodal share.

### 7. Random by Design?
The fly mushroom body was the textbook case of random wiring. Testing that claim properly takes the right null, and the answer is "almost, with structure that geometry largely explains", as long as the reader sees how much of the real wiring a local null keeps.
- **Which inputs share a Kenyon cell.** Zheng et al. 2022's FAFB reconstruction (bocklab/pn_kc: 6,468 uniglomerular inputs onto 1,354 Kenyon cells, 54 glomeruli; claw positions from their synapse records; FAFB's old names VC3l, VC3m, VC5 given as VC3, VC5, VM6). Their conditional input (claws from glomerulus j on the cells with input from i) as a 54 x 54 heatmap of z-scores in their Fig. 3B order, against 1,000 label shuffles run in a worker; one slider runs from the whole calyx (every cell keeps its claw count and every glomerulus its claws: Caron et al. 2013's shuffle, Zheng's random claw model) to 2 to 400 k-means cells of claw positions within which labels may move; a Kenyon cell class select (γ, α′β′, αβ). Beside it a frontal map of all claws with the core community marked, the selected pair's two glomeruli, and a circle for the typical cell size. Results: the ten core-community glomeruli (all food) have mean z 2.95 against the whole-calyx shuffle, z spread 1.46 (Zheng 1.47), 1,916 core claws as in the paper; a bouton-proportional null gives above 6. Within one KC class the core z is 1.2 to 1.6 (αβ and α′β′ take 36 to 40% of claws from core glomeruli, γ 19%). Spatial cells: about 1.8 at 10 cells (median 8 µm from the centre), 1.0 at 50, 0.8 at 100, but the share of claws left on their own glomerulus rises from 2.4% to 16%, 25% and 72% at 400; Zheng's five-nearest-bouton null works at that scale (rebuilt: core z about 1, a quarter kept) and Li et al. 2020 note data and model coincide at radius zero. The structure is in territories (food PN axons clustered, αβ and α′β′ dendrites in them, four neuroblast clusters), not in which nearby bouton a claw grabs. Computes: the conditional input, every shuffled ensemble, k-means cells and kept shares.
- **What random wiring is good for.** Hallem and Carlson 2006 (110 odours, 23 receptors after dropping Or33b, change from spontaneous firing; via drosolf, checked against DoOR) summed through Zheng's claws, the top 5% (68 cells) as each odour's tag, ties broken at random, the sparse-hashing view of Dasgupta et al. 2017. A scatter of all 5,995 odour pairs (response correlation against tag overlap) with binned means for real and 20 shuffled wirings and the 5% chance line; buttons swap in shuffled claws or uniformly random glomeruli; picking a pair shows their glomerular responses and the tags on a grid of Kenyon cells by class. Results: rank correlation 0.88 real, 0.88 shuffled, 0.87 uniform; pairs above r 0.8 share about two thirds of their tags under every wiring; below 0.2 they share 12% real, 10% shuffled, 9% uniform (chance 5%), so the real wiring discriminates slightly worse, as Zheng's simulation found; pairs that drive mostly core glomeruli gain 4.5 points, others about 2 (ethyl acetate and acetic acid 40% against 26%). Li et al.'s modest effect on dimension and the model's limits (no antennal lobe transform, no synapse counts, 23 glomeruli, 62% of claws; 154 cells never fire) close it. Computes: the projection, the tags and their overlap for every wiring.

### 8. Same Genes, Different Wiring
A connectome is one animal at one time, in one sex, and it omits the signals that don't travel by synapse.
- **Growing up.** Witvliet et al. 2021's eight worms from birth to adulthood: synapse counts per connection against age, with stable, variable and developmentally changing connections separated, and the reader brushing a neuron to follow its connections. Computes: growth per connection and the class shares.
- **Two sexes.** For the neurons shared by the hermaphrodite and the male (Cook et al. 2019), each connection's weight in one sex against the other on log axes, with sex-specific connections on the axes; then the fly, where most cell types match between sexes and the dimorphic ones are a small, widely connected set (Berg et al.). Computes: the joined edge table and its shares.
- **The wireless layer.** The worm's neuropeptide network (Ripoll-Sanchez et al. 2023) against its synaptic network on one layout: far denser, with different hubs. Computes: densities and degrees from the released matrices.
It ends where the series began: the graph is a measurement of one thing, and behaviour needs more.

## What to get right

- Every dataset is fetched from its primary source by a script in `scripts/connectomes/`, trimmed, saved under `docs/connectomes/shared/data/`, and cited on the page with its release version. Counts differ between releases (Varshney 2011, Cook 2019, Witvliet; hemibrain v1.0 vs v1.2.1; FlyWire versions); always name the version.
- Licences: Cook 2019 and Witvliet 2021 data state none; Markov 2014 is CC BY-NC. Say so in the data notes and cite the papers.
- The worm's 302 neurons include 20 pharyngeal neurons with their own network; analyses that use 279 dropped those and three that make no synapses. Chemical synapses are directed, gap junctions are not; never mix them silently.
- Tractography streamlines are not axons, FLNe is a fraction of labelled neurons in one injection, and EM weights are synapse counts. Never put two modalities on one numeric axis.
- Small-world claims must name their null. In the worm a distance-only null leaves the index near 5 and degree takes it to about 2.3; a degree-and-distance null falls below 2 only when its distance bins are so fine that it keeps much of the real graph. Don't repeat the "it's just geometry" story.
- The male fly CNS completeness gap: 94% presynaptic, 42% postsynaptic sites on traced bodies (Berg et al., Cell); the lost fragments are postsynaptic twigs, so outputs are undercounted.
- Facts drafts get wrong: Takemura 2013 did not identify Mi4 or Mi9 as T4 inputs; Witvliet synapses rise about six-fold (about 1,300 to about 8,000) and the reconstructions cover the brain (nerve ring), not ventral cord motor neurons; Caron et al. 2013 found about 7 inputs per Kenyon cell on average; Pang et al. 2023's main critique is a bioRxiv commentary by Faskowitz et al., not a journal paper.
- Series colors: observed data in the ink color; null models and expected values in one muted color; sensory, inter and motor neurons in three fixed hues used across both worm and fly; gap junctions and neuropeptides each get their own color, distinct from chemical synapses.
