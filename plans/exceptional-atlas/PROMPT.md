# Exceptional Atlas

I want a nine-part series on the classification of simple Lie algebras, the five exceptions it leaves (G₂, F₄, E₆, E₇ and E₈), and E₈ itself. It is for a reader with linear algebra who has heard of E₈ and wants to know why a list of four infinite families and five strays is the complete answer, and where the strays come from. The through-line is compression: an algebra becomes a set of root vectors, integrality leaves four possible angles, the vectors collapse into a small graph, and a positivity test on graphs leaves exactly nine shapes. Then the series follows the exceptions outward and ends with E₈'s 240 roots as a polytope, a lattice and the densest packing in eight dimensions.

By the end a reader should be able to read a Dynkin diagram, explain why there is no E₉, recognise the same A-D-E pattern in the finite subgroups of SU(2), and see how the octonions and the 240 roots are one object.

## Articles

**The classification**

### 1. Lie Algebras, Cartan and Roots
A Lie algebra is a vector space with a bracket; diagonalising a maximal commuting subalgebra splits it into root spaces, and the Killing form turns roots into vectors in Euclidean space. The reader computes brackets in sl₂, sees the root spaces of sl₃, and watches the Killing form built from traces for sl₂, sl₃ and sl₄.

### 2. sl₂ Ladders and Weyl Reflections
Every root carries a copy of sl₂, whose representation theory forces the Cartan integers to be integers, and reflections in roots generate the Weyl group. The reader applies the eight simple reflections of E₈ and watches one root's orbit fill all 240, arriving at the order of the Weyl group.

### 3. Simple Roots and Dynkin Diagrams
Integrality leaves four possible angles; simple roots, the Cartan matrix and the Dynkin diagram compress a root system to a graph. The reader picks an angle and length ratio and sees which rank-2 systems exist, then deletes nodes of the E₈ diagram and sees which subsystem of the 240 survives.

### 4. Pruning and the Diophantine Threshold
Which graphs can be Dynkin diagrams? A positivity test rules out cycles and crowded nodes, and the inequality 1/p + 1/q + 1/r > 1 settles the branched trees, leaving E₆, E₇, E₈. The signature interactive is a sandbox where the reader draws any graph and gets a finite, affine or indefinite verdict with the reason; affine diagrams sit on the boundary.

**The exceptions**

### 5. The McKay Correspondence
The finite subgroups of SU(2) are classified by the same A, D, E diagrams. The reader views each group as points on the 3-sphere, sees its characters computed, tensors with the 2-dimensional representation, and watches the resulting graph come out as the affine Dynkin diagram, with irrep dimensions equal to the node marks.

### 6. The Octonions
Cayley–Dickson doubling, Fano-plane multiplication, the failure of associativity and the Moufang laws that survive it, Hurwitz's theorem, and the 240 integral octonion units as the roots of E₈. The reader multiplies units on the Fano plane, tests Moufang identities on random octonions, and sees the Freudenthal–Tits magic square build F₄ through E₈ from pairs of division algebras.

### 7. F₄, E₆ and E₇
F₄ preserves the 27-dimensional Albert algebra and its long roots form the 24-cell; E₆ preserves its cubic form and its Weyl group acts on the 27 lines of a cubic surface; E₇ comes from Freudenthal's 56-dimensional triple system. The reader rotates the 24-cell in four dimensions and explores the 27 lines.

**E₈**

### 8. 240 Roots: Gosset Polytope and Coxeter Plane
The 240 roots as two coordinate families, as the vertices of Gosset's 4₂₁ polytope, and projected to the Coxeter plane as 8 rings of 30. The signature interactive is that projection, then a four-dimensional fold onto two 600-cells in golden ratio.

### 9. Sphere Packing and the Leech Lattice
The E₈ lattice, its theta series, kissing number 240, and the linear-programming bounds behind Viazovska's proof; Construction A turns the Hamming code into E₈, and in 24 dimensions the Golay code leads to Leech. The reader explores shells of lattice vectors, compares kissing bounds across dimensions, and corrects errors with the Hamming code.

## What to get right

- Every figure should compute from the math: generate root systems from simple roots, compute characters from group elements, derive the integral octonions by checking closure. Nothing hand-typed or decorative.
- Claims that are easy to get wrong: the 1/p + 1/q + 1/r derivation (use the standard textbook argument), which node deletions from affine Ẽ₈ give maximal subalgebras (only prime marks, per Borel–de Siebenthal), the 24-cell is not the only self-dual regular 4-polytope, and E₆ is not the smallest grand-unification group.
- The LP bound on kissing numbers is tight only in dimensions 1, 2, 8 and 24. Construction A on the Golay code gives a lattice with 48 minimal vectors; Leech needs more. Any Cohn–Elkies illustration must be a genuine eight-dimensional check.
- The McKay correspondence dates from 1980, after McKay's 1978 moonshine observation. Don't call it a step toward moonshine.
- Colour the integer and half-integer families of E₈ roots consistently wherever they appear, and keep long and short roots visually distinct in the non-simply-laced diagrams.
- A companion series on modular forms carries the story from the Leech lattice into moonshine; link to it.
