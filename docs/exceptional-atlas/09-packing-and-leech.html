Sphere Packing & the Leech Lattice

Part 9 of 9. The 240 roots are the shortest vectors of an infinite lattice. That lattice has the largest possible kissing number in 8 dimensions (240, proved 1979) and the densest packing (π⁴/384, proved by Viazovska in 2016). Construction A ties it to the extended Hamming code, and the same story in 24 dimensions leads to the Leech lattice, whose packing was proved optimal a week after E₈'s.

A lattice is an infinite set

The 240 roots are the shortest nonzero vectors of the E₈ lattice, a discrete subgroup of ℝ8 that extends outward in shells of increasing squared length.

The D₈⁺ construction

A vector is in the E₈ lattice if its 8 coordinates are either all integers or all half-integers (n + ½ with n ∈ ℤ), and their sum is even. The shortest integer vectors are the 112 roots (±1, ±1, 0, …, 0) of D8, and the shortest half-integer vectors are the 128 vectors (±½, …, ±½) with an even number of minus signs.

E₈ is unimodular, so its fundamental domain has volume exactly 1. Up to isometry it is the only even unimodular lattice in 8 dimensions; there are none in dimensions 1–7 and 24 in dimension 24 (the Niemeier lattices, one of which is the Leech lattice). Being even and unimodular is what makes the theta series a modular form of weight n/2.

Packing density is not kissing number

Kissing is a local question. Packing asks how densely equal spheres can fill all of ℝn; for a lattice, the density is the volume of one ball divided by the volume of a fundamental domain. E8 settled kissing in 1979, and packing took 37 more years.

Packing and error correction as the same question

A binary block code of length n is a set C ⊆ {0, 1}n of valid codewords. A channel flips some bits of a transmitted codeword, and the receiver guesses the nearest codeword to what arrived. A good code spreads its codewords apart in Hamming distance, so a few flipped bits can always be undone.

A sphere packing is the same idea in ℝn: centres at least some minimum distance apart, and noise decoded by rounding to the nearest centre. Construction A turns one problem into the other.

Why the parameters line up

The minimum squared length of Λ(C) is min(4, d), where d is the minimum distance of the code, so here it is 4. The vectors of squared length 4 come in two kinds. Each of the 14 weight-4 codewords gives vectors with ±1 in its four positions, 24 = 16 sign choices each, for 224. The zero codeword gives the all-even vectors ±2ei, 16 more. That is 224 + 16 = 240, and scaling by 1/√2 brings the squared length down to 2, E₈'s minimum shell.

So E₈ has now been described three ways: by its roots, as D8+, and as Construction A on the extended Hamming code.

Error correction in action

A code of minimum distance d corrects ⌊(d−1)/2⌋ errors, because Hamming balls of that radius around distinct codewords do not overlap. With one flip the decoder always recovers the original. Two flips put the received word at distance 2 from the original and at least 2 from every other codeword, so it can tie, and three flips can land it nearer a wrong codeword.

The lattice behaves the same way. The packing radius of E₈ (minimum norm 2) is 1/√2, so a point perturbed by less than that still rounds back to the original. A larger perturbation can carry it out of the original's Voronoi cell and into a neighbour's.

From 8 dimensions to 24

In 24 dimensions the natural code is the binary Golay code, a self-dual [24, 12, 8] code with 4,096 codewords, and the target is the Leech lattice. Construction A alone does not reach it.

Construction A on the Golay code, scaled by 1/√2 so that it is unimodular, is an even unimodular lattice, but its shortest vectors are the 48 vectors ±√2 ei of squared length 2, coming from the zero codeword. Every nonzero codeword has weight at least 8 and contributes vectors of squared length at least 4. So the result is the Niemeier lattice whose roots form 24 copies of A1, not the Leech lattice. The fix is Construction B: among integer vectors congruent mod 2 to a Golay codeword, keep those whose coordinate sum is divisible by 4, which removes ±2ei; then add the coset of an odd vector such as (−3, 1, 1, …, 1), and scale by 1/√8. The result is the Leech lattice Λ24, with minimum squared length 4 and 196,560 minimal vectors. Leech found it in the mid-1960s; its automorphism group turns out to contain three of the 26 sporadic simple groups.

No roots, and why that matters

Of the 24 even unimodular lattices in dimension 24 (the Niemeier lattices), twenty-three contain vectors of squared length 2. The Leech lattice is the one that does not, so its minimum squared length jumps to 4, carried by 196,560 vectors.

Roots would force the automorphism group to contain their Weyl group. The Leech lattice has none, and its symmetry group comes from somewhere else; it contains the symmetries of the Golay code but is far larger.

Leech and three copies of E₈

The Leech lattice can still be built from E₈, but from copies of E₈ scaled by √2, so that their minimum squared length is already 4. The Leech lattice contains three mutually orthogonal copies of √2E₈, and a Turyn-type construction (Lepowsky and Meurman, 1982, modelled on Turyn's 1967 construction of the Golay code from two versions of the Hamming code) glues them together without creating any vectors of squared length 2. Unscaled E83 cannot be enlarged at all, since it is already unimodular.

The symmetry cascade

The automorphism group of the Leech lattice, Co0, has order ≈ 8.3 × 1018. In 1968 John Conway computed this group and discovered that its quotient by a centre of order 2 is a simple group, Co1, and that Co1 contains two further sporadic simple groups: Co2, the stabiliser of a vector of squared length 4 (type 2), and Co3, the stabiliser of a vector of squared length 6 (type 3). Co1, Co2 and Co3 are three of the 26 sporadic finite simple groups. The Mathieu group M24, the symmetry group of the Golay code, sits inside Co0 as its coordinate permutations, and the Leech lattice is also the starting point for constructing the Monster, which the Modular Forms series takes up.
