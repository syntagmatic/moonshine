Triality, G₂ & the Magic Square

Part 10 of 15. The octonions give 7D the only cross product outside 3D. Their symmetry group is the exceptional Lie algebra G₂ = Der(𝕆). In 8 dimensions, vectors and spinors become interchangeable through triality. And Freudenthal's magic square arranges the rest of the exceptional family — F₄, E₆, E₇, E₈ — from the same octonionic data via the exceptional Jordan algebra h₃(𝕆) and the Cayley plane 𝕆P².

In your first physics class, you learned that $x \times y$ gives you a vector perpendicular to both $x$ and $y$. This property is so useful that we assume it is a universal law. But if you try to define a cross product in 2D or 4D, the math breaks. It turns out that a true, bilinear cross product can only exist in dimensions 3 and 7.

Why these two? Because those are the dimensions of the "purely imaginary" parts of the quaternions ($4-1=3$) and the octonions ($8-1=7$). The formula is simple: $x \times y = \text{Im}(xy)$. The Fano plane you met in Part 4 is nothing other than the multiplication table for this 7D cross product.

The G₂ Symmetry Group

A symmetry of an algebra is a rotation that respects its "logic." If you rotate the space so that $x$ becomes $x'$ and $y$ becomes $y'$, the symmetry requires that their product also rotates correctly: $(xy)' = x'y'$.

For the complex numbers, the symmetry is simple. For the quaternions, it is the group of all 3D rotations ($SO(3)$). But for the octonions, the symmetry is something special: G₂, the smallest of the exceptional Lie groups.

The Exceptional Group: G₂ is called "exceptional" because it does not fit into the standard families of rotations. It exists only because of the unique non-associative geometry of 8D space. It is 14-dimensional, meaning there are 14 independent ways to "twist" octonion space without breaking the math.

G₂ as Der(𝕆): the Lie algebra side

Of the five exceptional simple Lie algebras, G2 is the smallest, smaller than any of the classical families at the same rank. Its Dynkin diagram has only two nodes, connected by a triple edge with an arrow from the long root to the short root. Everything that matters about G2 fits on a napkin: 2 simple roots, 12 total roots, rank 2, dimension 14, Weyl group order 12 (the dihedral group of the regular hexagram). The Cartan matrix is

with product A12 · A21 = (−1)(−3) = 3, confirming the triple edge, and with the asymmetry telling us the two roots have squared lengths in ratio 1 : 3 (α2 is long, α1 is short, arrow points from α2 to α1).

G2 has 12 roots in a 2-dimensional Euclidean space, split into 6 short and 6 long. The short roots form a regular hexagon; the long roots form a larger hexagon rotated by 30°. Overlaying them gives a star of David, the signature image of G2.

Where does this Lie algebra come from? It has no matrix construction analogous to the classical families. Instead, the cleanest way to build G2 is as the derivation algebra of the octonions. A derivation of 𝕆 is a linear map D : 𝕆 → 𝕆 satisfying the Leibniz rule

Derivations form a Lie algebra under the commutator bracket [D1, D2] = D1D2 − D2D1. For ℝ there are no non-zero derivations; for ℂ and ℍ, the derivation algebras are 0 and 3-dimensional respectively. For the octonions, the derivation algebra is 14-dimensional, and it is G2:

Cartan himself identified G2 with Der(𝕆) in his 1894 thesis, making the octonions the first non-classical structure to enter the Cartan–Killing framework and the reason the classification has any exceptions at all. Without the octonions, the classification list would stop at the classical A, B, C, D families.

The smallest non-trivial representation of G2 is 7-dimensional. It acts on the imaginary octonions Im(𝕆) ≅ ℝ7 by derivations: every derivation satisfies D(1) = 0, so every derivation preserves the 7-dimensional subspace Im(𝕆) and acts there by a linear map preserving the octonion product. This is why the 7-dimensional cross product you explored in Figure 1 has symmetry group exactly G2, and why G2-manifolds, 7-dimensional Riemannian manifolds whose holonomy group is G2, show up as exceptional cases in Berger's classification and as candidate compactification spaces in 11-dimensional M-theory.

Triality and Spinors

In 3D physics, vectors and spinors are fundamentally different. If you rotate a vector 360 degrees, it returns to normal. But a spinor (like an electron) must be rotated 720 degrees to return to its original state. They inhabit different worlds.

But 8 dimensions is a geometric miracle. Here, there is a perfect three-way symmetry between vectors ($V$), left-handed spinors ($S_L$), and right-handed spinors ($S_R$). This is Triality, and it is the reason octonions are the bridge to superstring theory.

The Cayley plane 𝕆P²

In standard geometry, you can build a projective space of any dimension over the reals or complexes. A 3D space, a 100D space, or anything in between. Octonions break that freedom. Because they are non-associative, the concept of dimension for a projective space stops at two. You can build an octonionic line () and an octonionic plane, the Cayley plane , but you cannot build .

Why not? To define a 3D projective space you need matrices that act on 3-vectors, and matrix multiplication requires associativity. Since the octonions lack it, the 16-dimensional Cayley plane is the absolute end of the projective ladder. Its isometry group is the 78-dimensional exceptional Lie group E6, with the point stabiliser of the plane being the 52-dimensional . We meet both groups in full in the next article; for now it is enough to note that the Cayley plane is the geometric object that makes those two Lie groups exceptional.

Jordan algebras and h₃(𝕆)

In quantum physics, observable quantities are represented by Hermitian matrices. But if you multiply two Hermitian matrices and , the result is usually not Hermitian. Pascual Jordan invented a new multiplication rule, the Jordan product, that fixes this by averaging the two possible orders: . This makes multiplication commutative but non-associative.

Applied to octonionic Hermitian matrices, the Jordan product produces the exceptional Jordan algebra , also called the Albert algebra. It has exactly 27 dimensions: three real diagonal entries plus three off-diagonal octonionic entries (each contributing 8 dimensions). The automorphism group of this algebra is . Jordan, von Neumann, and Wigner classified finite-dimensional Jordan algebras in 1934 and found four infinite families plus exactly one exception: h3(𝕆). Every other Jordan algebra fits a classical family. This one stands alone.

Freudenthal's Magic Square

There are five exceptional Lie groups (). For a long time they seemed like random accidents of mathematics, monolithic peaks of symmetry that did not fit into the standard infinite families. Then Hans Freudenthal and Jacques Tits, working independently in the 1950s and 60s, discovered a construction that builds them all from pairs of normed division algebras. It works by combining two algebras and via the formula , where denotes the traceless part and Der the derivation algebra.

The resulting table is symmetric — M(𝔸, 𝔹) = M(𝔹, 𝔸) — and strikingly clean. The inner 3×3 classical block uses only ℝ, ℂ, ℍ and produces algebras you already know: 𝔰𝔬(3), 𝔰𝔲(3), 𝔰𝔭(3), 𝔰𝔲(6), 𝔰𝔬(12). The 4th row and 4th column, whenever the octonions enter, produce exactly the four exceptional Lie algebras F4, E6, E7, E8. G2 does not appear in the 4×4 table: it sits outside the square as Der(𝕆), the automorphism algebra of one copy of 𝕆. The magic square is the "doubled" construction; G2 is the un-doubled one.

The exceptions exist because the octonions exist. The classification of simple Lie algebras tells you there are four infinite families plus five exceptions. The exceptions are not parachute drops from nowhere: they exist precisely because one of the four normed division algebras (𝕆) is non-associative, and that non-associativity is rigid enough to produce exactly the algebras F4, E6, E7, E8 through the magic-square construction, plus G2 through its automorphism Lie algebra. Remove the octonions, and the list becomes ABCD, full stop. Keep them, and you get five more entries plus one (G2) on the side.

Takeaways

  • The cross product exists only in 3D and 7D, because Im(ℍ) and Im(𝕆) are the only imaginary spaces of normed division algebras. $x \times y = \text{Im}(xy)$; the Fano plane IS the multiplication table for the 7D cross product.
  • G2 = Der(𝕆), the 14-dimensional derivation algebra of the octonions. It is the unique simple Lie algebra with a triple-edge Dynkin diagram, with Cartan matrix [[2, −1], [−3, 2]] and 12 roots arranged as a star of David. Cartan identified this in 1894, making the octonions the first non-classical structure in the classification.
  • Triality: in 8D, vectors ($V_8$), left-spinors ($S_+$), right-spinors ($S_-$) are interchangeable. This three-way symmetry exists only in dimension 8 and is why octonions connect to string theory.
  • The Cayley plane 𝕆P² is the 16-dimensional projective plane over the octonions. It exists because 𝕆 is alternative; 𝕆P³ does not exist because 𝕆 is not associative.
  • The exceptional Jordan algebra h3(𝕆) is the 27-dimensional space of 3 × 3 octonionic Hermitian matrices with the Jordan product A ∘ B = ½(AB + BA). F4 = Aut(h3(𝕆)); E6 = Isom(𝕆P²).
  • Freudenthal's magic square M(𝔸, 𝔹) fills in all five exceptional groups by combining division algebras pairwise: the 𝕆 row gives F4, E6, E7, E8. G2 sits outside as Der(𝕆). Remove 𝕆 and the classification reduces to the four classical families.
