Normed Division Algebras

Part 8 of 15. Multiplication in low dimensions is the language of rotation. Only four dimensions allow a multiplication that preserves lengths — 1, 2, 4, and 8 — and this explainer walks the three that come before eight.

Most of us learn multiplication as a way to grow or shrink a value. On the 1D number line, multiplying by 2 stretches a line; multiplying by ½ shrinks it. But even in 1D, there is a hint of something deeper: multiplying by −1 doesn't just change the size, it flips the direction. It is a 180° rotation.

This is the first lesson of the higher-dimensional numbers we are about to meet: multiplication is geometry. If we want to understand the 8th dimension, we cannot think of numbers as mere quantities. We must think of them as transformations.

2D: The birth of division algebras

In two dimensions, we use the complex numbers (). Here, every number $z = a + bi$ has two distinct physical traits: a magnitude (how far it is from zero) and an angle (which way it points).

The geometric miracle: When you multiply two complex numbers, their magnitudes multiply, but their angles add. This means multiplication in 2D is a perfect "rotate-and-scale" engine. This property is what makes the complex numbers a normed division algebra — the length of the product is the product of the lengths ($|zw| = |z||w|$).

In the 19th century, mathematicians asked a daring question: Can we do this in any dimension? Can we find a way to multiply vectors in 3D, 4D, or 100D such that lengths always multiply correctly? It turns out the answer is almost always "no." Nature only allows this miracle in four specific dimensions: 1, 2, 4, and 8.

Hamilton's bridge: 4D

In 1843, William Rowan Hamilton was obsessed with extending this navigator to 3D. He spent years trying to make $|(a,b,c) \times (d,e,f)| = |(a,b,c)||(d,e,f)|$. He failed. Every rule he tried broke the law $|ab| = |a||b|$.

The solution came to him in a flash while walking across Brougham Bridge: to multiply in 3D, he needed a 4th dimension. He needed three imaginary units ($i, j, k$) and one real unit. He needed the quaternions.

The bridge formula: $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$

This single line implies that $ij = k$, but $ji = -k$. For the first time in history, mathematicians accepted a number system where order matters. Multiplication was no longer commutative.

Quaternions are still the industry standard for 3D graphics and aerospace because they avoid the "gimbal lock" problems of simpler systems. They proved that losing commutativity was a small price to pay for the power to navigate 3D space perfectly.
