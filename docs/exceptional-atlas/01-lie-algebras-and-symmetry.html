What Is a Lie Algebra?

Part 1 of 15. A vector space with a bilinear, anti-symmetric, Jacobi-respecting "bracket" operation. That four-line definition captures the infinitesimal shadow of every continuous symmetry in mathematics, and everything in this series unfolds from it.

We are going to spend fifteen explainers walking past the specimens in a particular museum: the complete list of finite-dimensional simple Lie algebras over ℂ. There are, up to isomorphism, nine species. Four come in infinite families, the classical types A, B, C, D. Five are singular exceptions, E6, E7, E8, F4, and G2. That every finite-dimensional simple Lie algebra belongs to exactly one of these is the Cartan–Killing classification theorem, one of the sharpest closure results in mathematics. To earn the right to read that list we first need to agree on what a Lie algebra is, and on what "simple" means for one.

This explainer does both. It gives the definition, grounds it in three canonical examples you have almost certainly already met under other names, and introduces the two ideas, ideals and the adjoint representation, that the classification will use as levers. By the end of it you should be able to write down a Lie algebra, bracket two elements, recognise an ideal when you see one, and understand why "no non-trivial ideals" is the right criterion for indivisibility.

From continuous symmetry to linear algebra

The canonical source of Lie algebras is a Lie group, a group whose underlying set is also a smooth manifold, with multiplication and inversion that are smooth maps. The rotation group SO(3) is the standard example: its elements are the 3×3 orthogonal matrices with determinant 1, which form a 3-dimensional smooth surface sitting inside the 9-dimensional space of all 3×3 matrices. The group operation is matrix multiplication. SO(3) is continuous in a way finite groups like S5 are not, you can slide along a smooth path of rotations, rotating a little more or a little less.

That smoothness is the whole point. Near the identity, a Lie group looks like a flat vector space, its tangent space at the identity element. For SO(3), this tangent space is 3-dimensional, because SO(3) itself is a 3-dimensional manifold. That 3-dimensional tangent space is already familiar: it is the space of infinitesimal rotations, and each of its vectors can be identified with an axis-and-angular-velocity pair. Euler's rotation theorem tells us every finite rotation has an axis; taking the limit of very small rotations produces a vector along that axis with magnitude equal to the angular speed.

But a tangent space is just a vector space, and a vector space alone throws away most of the information about the group. What rescues the story is a second operation, the Lie bracket, which remembers on the tangent space exactly the information needed to reconstruct the group locally. Two infinitesimal rotations compose into a finite rotation whose leading-order correction, to second order in the rotation angles, is captured by their bracket. The Lie algebra is the tangent space at the identity plus this bracket operation, and it holds all the infinitesimal information about the group packaged into a finite-dimensional linear structure.

The linearisation trick. A curved group gets flattened into a flat vector space near the identity, and all of the group's curvature gets pushed into a single extra operation (the bracket) on that vector space. The vector space is easy, it is just ℝn. The bracket is where the structure lives.

The three laws of a Lie bracket

Before meeting any examples, let us write down the definition. A Lie algebra is a vector space 𝔤 over a field (we work over ℂ throughout the series) equipped with a binary operation [·, ·] : 𝔤 × 𝔤 → 𝔤 that obeys three laws:

There is no fourth law. In particular, the Lie bracket is not required to be associative, and in every example we care about it is non-associative. What the Jacobi identity does is serve as a weakened, asymmetric replacement for associativity that is exactly compatible with anti-symmetry.

One immediate consequence of bilinearity plus anti-symmetry: every element brackets with itself to give zero. Expanding [x + y, x + y] using bilinearity and using anti-symmetry to cancel terms yields [x, x] = 0 for every x ∈ 𝔤. The bracket is alternating, a slightly stronger condition than anti-symmetric (over fields of characteristic 2 the two are not equivalent, but ℂ has characteristic 0 so there is no distinction here).

Here is the prototypical example to fix ideas: take 𝔤 to be sl2(ℂ), the space of 2×2 complex matrices with trace zero, and define the bracket as the matrix commutator [X, Y] = XY − YX. The commutator of any two matrices is trace zero (because tr(XY) = tr(YX)), so the operation stays inside sl2. Bilinearity is immediate from the distributive law. Anti-symmetry is immediate from the definition. The Jacobi identity you can check by grinding through the definition and watching all six terms cancel, which is a good thing to do exactly once.

Three canonical examples

Three constructions of Lie algebras turn up so often that they deserve names.

The general linear family: gln and sln

gln(ℂ), the space of all n×n complex matrices with the commutator bracket [X, Y] = XY − YX, is a Lie algebra of complex dimension n2. Its special linear sibling sln(ℂ) is the trace-zero subspace, of dimension n2 − 1. The commutator automatically lands in sln whenever the operands do, because tr(XY − YX) = 0 identically. sl2 we have already met; sln for n ≥ 2 turns out to be the entire A-type family An−1 in the classification.

Skew-symmetric matrices: so(n) and so(3) in particular

so(n) is the space of n×n skew-symmetric real matrices, i.e. matrices A with AT = −A. The diagonal of a skew-symmetric matrix is zero, so so(n) is (n2 − n)/2-dimensional. Under the commutator bracket, so(n) is a Lie algebra. It is the tangent space at the identity of the orthogonal group SO(n), and its elements generate the rotations of ℝn.

Vector fields under the Lie bracket

The third source of Lie algebras, which we will reference but not use, is smooth vector fields on a manifold. If X and Y are smooth vector fields on a manifold M, each can be interpreted as a first-order differential operator acting on smooth functions. Their commutator [X, Y] = XY − YX as differential operators turns out to again be a first-order differential operator, a new vector field. The space of all smooth vector fields on M, under this bracket, is a Lie algebra, although generally an infinite-dimensional one. Restricting to left-invariant vector fields on a Lie group G recovers the finite-dimensional Lie algebra of G. This construction is the cleanest bridge from Lie groups to Lie algebras: the Lie algebra of G is literally the left-invariant vector fields on G under their commutator.

Commutators and composition order. In all three examples the bracket has the flavour of "does the order of two operations matter?" [X, Y] = XY − YX measures the failure of two matrices (or two rotations, or two vector fields) to commute. If they did commute, their bracket would vanish, and Lie algebras where every bracket vanishes are called abelian. Abelian Lie algebras carry no structure beyond being vector spaces, which is why the classification focuses on the non-abelian case.

Subalgebras, ideals, and what "simple" means

Now that we have Lie algebras, we can ask the usual structural question: what are their substructures? There are two kinds, and the difference between them is the entire story of the classification.

A subalgebra 𝔥 ⊂ 𝔤 is a vector subspace that is closed under the bracket: [x, y] ∈ 𝔥 whenever x, y ∈ 𝔥. The subalgebra is then a Lie algebra in its own right, with the inherited bracket. The diagonal matrices inside sln form a subalgebra (an abelian one, because diagonal matrices commute). The upper-triangular matrices form another. sl2 embedded inside sl3 as the top-left 2×2 block is a third.

An ideal 𝔦 ⊂ 𝔤 is a subalgebra with a stronger closure property: it is closed under bracketing with anything in the ambient algebra, not just with itself. That is, [x, y] ∈ 𝔦 for every x ∈ 𝔤 and every y ∈ 𝔦. Every ideal is a subalgebra, but most subalgebras are not ideals. Ideals are the Lie-algebra analogue of normal subgroups or of two-sided ideals in an associative ring: they are subobjects that quotient well, meaning 𝔤/𝔦 inherits a Lie algebra structure if and only if 𝔦 is an ideal.

Every Lie algebra 𝔤 has two trivial ideals: the zero subspace {0} and the whole algebra 𝔤 itself. A Lie algebra is called simple if these are the only ideals and, additionally, 𝔤 is non-abelian. The non-abelian condition excludes the 1-dimensional case, the line ℂ with its unique (zero) bracket has no non-trivial ideals for dimension reasons, but it would be a degenerate entry on the classification list, so we throw it out by convention.

Simple Lie algebras are the indivisible atoms of continuous symmetry. The analogy to prime numbers in ℤ or to finite simple groups in the theory of finite groups is more than an analogy, it is the same sort of classification problem, and the simple Lie algebras play exactly the same role of being the irreducible building blocks from which more complicated semisimple Lie algebras are assembled by taking direct sums. Every finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebra over ℂ is isomorphic to a direct sum of simple Lie algebras, and the summands are unique up to permutation (this is the Weyl theorem on complete reducibility). So classifying semisimple Lie algebras reduces completely to classifying simple ones, which is our target.

The adjoint representation

All of this matters because the adjoint representation lets us study 𝔤 using the full machinery of linear algebra on 𝔤 itself. In particular we can ask: what are the eigenvalues of adx, and how does 𝔤 decompose into eigenspaces? The answer, when x is chosen well, is the root space decomposition, and that decomposition is the central tool for classifying simple Lie algebras. Takeaways

  • A Lie algebra is a vector space with a bilinear, anti-symmetric, Jacobi-respecting bracket. The bracket linearises the information content of a Lie group near its identity element.
  • sln(ℂ), the trace-zero n×n complex matrices under the commutator, is our workhorse example. sl2 is 3-dimensional with basis (H, E, F) and relations [H, E] = 2E, [H, F] = −2F, [E, F] = H. All of sln is determined by its bracket on any basis.
  • so(3) ≅ (ℝ3, ×). Skew-symmetric 3×3 matrices under matrix commutation are the same Lie algebra as ℝ3 under the cross product. This is the first surprise of the theory and a reminder that the bracket can take many disguises.
  • Ideals are subspaces that absorb brackets with everything in 𝔤, not just with themselves. A Lie algebra is simple when its only ideals are {0} and itself, and it is not abelian. Simple Lie algebras are the atomic units of continuous symmetry.
  • The adjoint representation adx(y) = [x, y] turns each element into a linear operator on 𝔤. The Jacobi identity makes each adx a derivation, and the map x ↦ adx is itself a Lie algebra homomorphism from 𝔤 to End(𝔤). Everything from here on is eigenvalue analysis of these operators.

The whole classification machine is now set up to start turning. In the next explainer we pick a maximal set of elements of 𝔤 whose adjoint operators commute, the Cartan subalgebra, and diagonalise them simultaneously. The eigenspaces of that simultaneous diagonalisation are the root spaces, and the eigenvalues are the roots. From there on, the classification is geometry.
