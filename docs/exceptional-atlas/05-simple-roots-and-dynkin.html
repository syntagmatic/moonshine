Simple Roots, Cartan Matrices, and Dynkin Diagrams

Part 5 of 15. From a root system Δ, pick a half-space, extract a minimal generating set of "simple" roots, record their pairwise Cartan integers in a matrix, and draw that matrix as a graph. Every simple Lie algebra compresses to a handful of nodes and edges, and the diagram for E8 already foreshadows the end of the story.

The last explainer reduced every pairwise interaction between roots to one of four discrete types: the 90°/120°/135°/150° angle ladder with associated length ratios 1, 1, √2, √3. That is the full set of moves a root system can make at the pair level. What we have not yet done is compress an entire root system into a finite combinatorial signature.

This explainer does that compression in three steps. First, we split Δ into positive and negative halves by picking a generic hyperplane. Second, we extract a distinguished subset Π ⊂ Δ⁺ of simple roots, a minimal linearly independent generating set whose non-negative integer combinations are exactly the positive roots. Third, we record the pairwise Cartan integers among the simple roots in a Cartan matrix, and draw that matrix as a graph called a Dynkin diagram. By the end, every simple Lie algebra is identified with a short list of numbers (the Cartan matrix) or a small graph (the Dynkin diagram), and the classification reduces to asking which graphs are possible.

Positive and negative roots

The first move is to give the root system Δ an orientation. Pick any linear functional h : 𝔼 → ℝ that is non-zero on every root (a generic choice, any h outside a finite union of hyperplanes works). Declare a root α ∈ Δ to be positive if h(α) > 0, and negative otherwise. This partitions Δ into two disjoint halves

of equal size |Δ|/2, with Δ⁻ = −Δ⁺ (negating every positive gives every negative). Different choices of h give different positive/negative splits, but any two splits differ only by an action of the Weyl group, so everything we derive from any single split will be invariant under the Weyl group, and hence intrinsic to 𝔤.

A positive root α ∈ Δ⁺ is called simple (or indecomposable) if it cannot be written as a sum α = β + γ of two other positive roots. Equivalently, α is simple if the α-component of α in any positive-root expansion is the only non-zero component. The set of simple roots is denoted Π:

Three non-obvious facts about Π hold for any semisimple Lie algebra. First, |Π| equals the rank n of 𝔤, so exactly n simple roots. Second, Π is linearly independent, so it is a basis for 𝔥*. Third, and most important for the classification:

Every positive root is a non-negative integer combination of simple roots. More precisely, for every α ∈ Δ⁺ there are non-negative integers c1, …, cn (not all zero) with α = c1α1 + ⋯ + cnαn. The ci are uniquely determined by α. This is the generation property, and it is what makes Π a genuinely minimal generating set.

Simple roots are also always at obtuse or right angles to each other: (α, β) ≤ 0 for any pair of distinct simple roots α, β ∈ Π. This is because if (α, β) > 0, then α − β would be a root of smaller height, contradicting indecomposability of α. Combined with explainer 4's classification of legal angles, this cuts the angle between two simple roots down to exactly one of 90°, 120°, 135°, 150°, precisely the four allowed by the crystallographic restriction.

The Cartan matrix

Given the simple roots Π = {α1, …, αn}, the Cartan matrix A is the n × n integer matrix whose entries are the pairwise Cartan integers:

The diagonal entries Aii are all 2, by definition. The off-diagonal entries Aij lie in {0, −1, −2, −3} (the non-positive half of the allowed Cartan integer values, because simple roots are at obtuse or right angles). And the matrix is "almost symmetric": the off-diagonal pairs Aij, Aji are related by

which says that if the two simple roots have the same length, then Aij = Aji; if they have different lengths, then Aij / Aji = (αi, αi) / (αj, αj), the ratio of their squared lengths. The "more negative" Cartan entry in each off-diagonal pair corresponds to the shorter root (appearing in the denominator of the larger of the two).

A useful way to check whether a proposed integer matrix is a Cartan matrix of a simple Lie algebra: the matrix must be positive definite (all eigenvalues strictly positive), and its off-diagonal pattern must satisfy the constraints above. Positive definiteness is the heart of the classification, explainer 7 will show that positive definiteness of the Cartan matrix forces the Dynkin diagram (which we are about to define) to be a connected tree of valency ≤ 3 with at most one multiple edge, and those constraints reduce to a short list of possibilities.

Dynkin diagrams

The Cartan matrix encodes everything, but it is an n × n integer matrix, which is hard to eyeball. Eugene Dynkin's 1947 observation was that all of the information in the Cartan matrix can be recorded in a small graph, one node per simple root, with an edge between nodes i and j weighted by the product Aij · Aji ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3} from the crystallographic restriction. That graph is the Dynkin diagram.

The weight interpretation is exactly what you would expect:

  • No edge between i and j ⇔ Aij = Aji = 0 ⇔ the two simple roots are orthogonal.
  • Single edge ⇔ Aij = Aji = −1 ⇔ 120° angle, equal lengths.
  • Double edge ⇔ {Aij, Aji} = {−1, −2} ⇔ 135° angle, length ratio √2.
  • Triple edge ⇔ {Aij, Aji} = {−1, −3} ⇔ 150° angle, length ratio √3.

For double and triple edges, the diagram additionally records an arrow pointing from the long root to the short root, which breaks the symmetry between (αi, αj) and (αj, αi) that length-difference introduced. With the arrow, the Dynkin diagram encodes the full Cartan matrix with zero loss of information: you can reconstruct every Aij from the graph, and vice versa.

Because a simple Lie algebra has an irreducible root system (one that cannot be decomposed as a direct sum of two orthogonal subsystems), its Dynkin diagram is connected. A disconnected Dynkin diagram corresponds to a semisimple but non-simple Lie algebra, a direct sum of simple factors, one per connected component. So the classification problem becomes: which connected Dynkin diagrams correspond to positive-definite Cartan matrices?, and we will answer it in the next two explainers.

The four rank-2 examples

The smallest non-trivial case is rank 2, where every root system has exactly 2 simple roots and the Cartan matrix is 2 × 2. Up to isomorphism there are four connected rank-2 root systems, one for each of the four allowed angles, A2, B2, C2, and G2. (B2 and C2 are isomorphic as abstract root systems, but their Dynkin diagrams differ in which direction the arrow points, which reflects whether we call the longer root "α1" or "α2".)

A high-rank preview: E8

Before tabulating the classical families, it helps to see the compression machinery at full scale. The root system E8 has 240 roots in 8 dimensions, a lot to track directly. Under the Dynkin procedure, all 240 collapse to 8 simple roots, which collapse further to a graph of 8 nodes and 7 edges. Every property of E8 (its Weyl group, its subsystems, its embeddings) can be reconstructed from that one diagram.

The Bourbaki simple roots of E8 are eight specific vectors in ℝ⁸. All have squared length 2; α1 is the only half-integer vector, the other seven are integer vectors of D8 type.

Why legs of length 1, 2, 4? Because that is the maximum leg length whose associated Cartan matrix stays positive definite. Extend the long leg to length 5 and a zero eigenvalue appears, you have walked off the classification's spherical Diophantine cliff into the affine Kac-Moody regime. E8 sits exactly on the last finite rung. The next two explainers (6 and 7) lay out the combinatorial argument that pins this down.

With the diagram in hand, every one of the 240 roots of E8 decomposes as a unique non-negative or non-positive integer combination of the eight αi. The sum of the coefficients is the height of the root; the highest root θ sits at height 29 with coefficients (2, 3, 4, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2).

Dynkin diagrams of the classical families

The same construction applied to the rank-n classical families An, Bn, Cn, Dn produces a distinctive signature shape for each family:

Once the classification argument is complete (explainer 7), we will see that the only additional Dynkin diagrams that correspond to simple Lie algebras are the five exceptional ones, G2, F4, E6, E7, and E8. Each of those gets a chapter of its own in Act III, and the full catalogue of all nine families forms the complete Cartan–Killing list.

What the compression buys us

The upshot of this explainer is that every simple Lie algebra 𝔤 over ℂ is determined, up to isomorphism, by its Dynkin diagram alone. Two different simple Lie algebras cannot have the same Dynkin diagram, and any valid Dynkin diagram corresponds to exactly one isomorphism class of simple Lie algebra. This is a remarkable compression: an infinite-dimensional-looking object (a Lie algebra, plus its representation theory, plus its Weyl group, plus everything else) reduces to a small graph you can sketch on a napkin.

The remaining work is to characterise which graphs can be valid Dynkin diagrams. That is a combinatorial question, it is asking which n × n integer matrices of the right form can be positive definite, and the next two explainers answer it with a short graph-theoretic argument that pens everything inside a handful of shapes.

The endgame. The classification of simple Lie algebras over ℂ is equivalent to classifying connected Dynkin diagrams whose associated Cartan matrices are positive definite. We have the translation now; what remains is to walk through the possibilities.

Takeaways

  • Positive/negative split. Pick a generic linear functional on 𝔼, declare the roots on its positive side to be "positive." The resulting Δ⁺ has |Δ|/2 elements and generates everything.
  • Simple roots Π. The minimal indecomposable generators of Δ⁺, equivalently a linearly independent basis such that every positive root has non-negative integer coefficients. |Π| = rank.
  • Cartan matrix. Aij = 2(αi, αj)/(αj, αj), an n × n integer matrix with diagonal 2 and off-diagonal in {0, −1, −2, −3}. It is positive definite if and only if the root system is finite (not affine or hyperbolic).
  • Dynkin diagram. The graph with n nodes (one per simple root) and edges weighted by Aij · Aji ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3}. Arrows on multi-edges point from long roots to short roots. Encodes the Cartan matrix with zero loss.
  • Classification restated. Simple Lie algebras over ℂ ↔ connected Dynkin diagrams with positive-definite Cartan matrix. We classify the algebras by classifying the diagrams, which is a finite combinatorial problem starting with explainer 7.
