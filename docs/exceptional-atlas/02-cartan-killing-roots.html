Cartan, Killing & Root Decomposition

Part 2 of 15. Pick a maximal commuting set of diagonalisable elements, the Cartan subalgebra. Its adjoint action cuts 𝔤 into a finite list of one-dimensional eigenspaces, and a canonical bilinear form (Killing's) turns the eigenvalues into honest vectors in a Euclidean space. This is the pivot where algebra becomes geometry.

The adjoint representation from the last explainer gave us a linear operator adx : 𝔤 → 𝔤 for every element x ∈ 𝔤. To understand 𝔤, we would like to diagonalise these operators and read off their eigenspaces. That is the standard linear-algebra manoeuvre: any interesting linear operator is best understood through its spectrum.

But 𝔤 has many elements, and each one gives a different operator adx, usually with a different eigenspace decomposition. Rather than picking one x and living with a single decomposition, we pick as large a subset as possible where all the adjoint operators can be diagonalised simultaneously. That subset is called a Cartan subalgebra, and it is the organising scaffold for every classification argument that follows.

Why we want to diagonalise

An operator T on a vector space V is diagonalisable if V decomposes as a direct sum of eigenspaces of T. That decomposition is the cleanest possible description of T: it tells you which subspaces are invariant, what T does on each one, and it lets you take functions of T (powers, exponentials, logarithms) by working eigenspace-by-eigenspace. Given a Lie algebra 𝔤 whose adjoint operators are all simultaneously diagonalisable on some subspace 𝔥 ⊂ 𝔤, we can decompose 𝔤 into joint eigenspaces, one eigenspace for each linear functional 𝔥 → ℂ, and ask what structure on those functionals the bracket forces. The forced structure is the root system, and we build it out explicitly below.

The constraint is that simultaneous diagonalisation of a family of operators requires two conditions. First, each operator in the family must itself be diagonalisable. Second, any two operators in the family must commute. Both conditions are facts about linear algebra on a fixed vector space:

Linear-algebra fact. A family of linear operators {Ti} on a finite-dimensional vector space V can be simultaneously diagonalised, meaning there is a single basis of V that is an eigenbasis for every Ti at once, if and only if (a) each Ti is diagonalisable on its own, and (b) Ti Tj = Tj Ti for all i, j. These two conditions are necessary and sufficient.

So to get a simultaneous eigenspace decomposition of 𝔤 via the adjoint representation, we need to find a subspace 𝔥 ⊂ 𝔤 of elements x for which adx is diagonalisable and for which any two of them give commuting adjoint operators. The second condition has a beautifully simple translation at the level of 𝔤 itself: adx and ady commute as operators on 𝔤 if and only if [x, y] lies in the centre of 𝔤. For simple Lie algebras the centre is zero, so adx and ady commute precisely when [x, y] = 0, that is, when x and y commute in 𝔤. So we are looking for a subspace 𝔥 of commuting elements, each of which has a diagonalisable adjoint.

Toral subalgebras and the Cartan

An element x ∈ 𝔤 whose adjoint operator adx is diagonalisable is called semisimple (or ad-diagonalisable, if we want to be explicit that we mean the adjoint and not some other representation). A subspace 𝔥 ⊂ 𝔤 is toral if every element of 𝔥 is semisimple and 𝔥 is abelian, all its elements bracket to zero with each other. A Cartan subalgebra is a maximal toral subalgebra: a toral subalgebra that is not strictly contained in any larger toral subalgebra.

"Maximal" has a concrete meaning: you cannot extend 𝔥 by adding any new semisimple element y ∉ 𝔥 while keeping the space commutative. Try to add such a y, and either y fails to be semisimple, or the extended space fails to be abelian. The upshot is that a Cartan subalgebra captures the largest possible commuting diagonalisable family, the largest batch of operators whose joint eigenspaces we can compute.

Three theorems about Cartan subalgebras hold for every finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebra over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero (such as ℂ, the field we use throughout). We use each one as a black box:

  1. Existence. A Cartan subalgebra exists. One classical construction picks any element whose adjoint is as close to being "generic" as possible and takes the centraliser of its semisimple part.
  2. Abelianness in the simple case. For a simple (or more generally semisimple) 𝔤, the Cartan subalgebra 𝔥 is honestly abelian as a subspace of 𝔤, there is no hidden nilpotent part. This is what lets us treat adh for h ∈ 𝔥 as a family of genuinely commuting diagonalisable operators.
  3. Conjugacy. Any two Cartan subalgebras are conjugate to each other under an inner automorphism of 𝔤. Equivalently, the abstract data we extract from 𝔥, its dimension, its eigenspace structure on 𝔤, the geometry of the resulting root system, do not depend on which Cartan subalgebra we picked.

The third theorem is the one we lean on most. It means that the dimension of a Cartan subalgebra is an invariant of 𝔤 itself: it does not depend on the choice of 𝔥. That invariant has a name.

Rank. The rank of a semisimple Lie algebra 𝔤 is the dimension of any (hence every) Cartan subalgebra. Rank is one of the two fundamental invariants of a simple Lie algebra, alongside its dimension. We will see later that the nine families in the classification are indexed naturally by rank: A1, A2, A3, … for increasing sln; E6, E7, E8 for the rank-6, 7, 8 exceptionals; and so on.

Diagonal matrices in sln

The construction is easiest to see in sln(ℂ), the trace-zero n × n complex matrices. Inside sln, the diagonal trace-zero matrices form an (n − 1)-dimensional subspace. They form an abelian subalgebra because any two diagonal matrices commute. Each one is diagonal, hence diagonalisable in the most trivial possible sense, which means its adjoint operator is also diagonalisable (on the space of all matrices, acting by commutation). And this subspace is maximal: you cannot enlarge it while keeping it abelian and semisimple. So it is a Cartan subalgebra of sln, and the rank of sln is n − 1.

The joint eigenspace decomposition

Now we can state the result we want, which justifies the whole apparatus. Pick any Cartan subalgebra 𝔥 ⊂ 𝔤. Because 𝔥 is abelian and consists of semisimple elements, the family of adjoint operators {adh : h ∈ 𝔥} is a commuting family of diagonalisable operators on 𝔤. of all non-zero functionals whose eigenspaces are non-empty. Taking the direct sum over all α (including α = 0, where 𝔤0 will turn out to equal 𝔥 itself), we get the root space decomposition:

Every element of 𝔤 can be written uniquely as a Cartan element plus one contribution from each root space. The decomposition has four structural facts that matter for the classification.

The four facts.

(1) 𝔤0 = 𝔥. The zero eigenspace equals the Cartan subalgebra itself. Nothing else has joint eigenvalue zero.

(2) Root spaces come in pairs. If α ∈ Δ, then −α ∈ Δ. The roots of a semisimple Lie algebra are symmetric about the origin in 𝔥*.

(3) Each root space is one-dimensional. For every α ∈ Δ, dim 𝔤α = 1. In a simple Lie algebra, each root α identifies exactly one direction in 𝔤, up to scalar.

(4) Brackets are additive on roots. If x ∈ 𝔤α and y ∈ 𝔤β, then [x, y] ∈ 𝔤α+β. In particular, [𝔤α, 𝔤β] is zero unless α + β is zero or a root.

Facts (1), (2), and (4) follow from general arguments about joint eigenspaces, the Jacobi identity plus a careful accounting of how adh interacts with brackets. Fact (3) is the deep one: it holds for semisimple Lie algebras specifically, and its proof uses the embedded sl2 triples we will meet in the next explainer. For now accept it as given. It is the single most restrictive piece of structure we will have to work with, and it is what makes the classification possible at all.

Why one-dimensionality matters

Of the four facts above, the one worth pausing on is fact (3): every non-zero root space in a semisimple Lie algebra is one-dimensional. This might look like a small technical claim, but it is the assertion that turns an infinite-dimensional classification problem into a finite combinatorial one.

A semisimple Lie algebra 𝔤 of rank r has some number of dimensions, call it d. The Cartan subalgebra takes up r of those dimensions. The remaining d − r dimensions are distributed over the root spaces 𝔤α. If each 𝔤α had arbitrary dimension, we would need to record the dimension of each root space as part of the data of 𝔤, and the classification would be much more complicated. But one-dimensionality says: there is exactly one root per missing dimension, so |Δ| = d − r. Once we know the root system Δ as an abstract finite set in 𝔥*, we know everything about 𝔤 beyond the Cartan itself, up to isomorphism.

This is the moment where "classify semisimple Lie algebras" becomes "classify finite configurations of vectors". The rest of Act I sets up what structure a root system must have, and Act II proves the sharp classification of those structures. But the move from algebra to geometry happens right here, at fact (3).

Rank counting. For sln+1: dimension n(n+2), rank n, so |Δ| = n(n+2) − n = n(n+1). That's the root count of An. For so(2n+1): dimension n(2n+1), rank n, so |Δ| = 2n². For E8: dimension 248, rank 8, so |Δ| = 240. Every one of the nine families in the classification satisfies this arithmetic, and the arithmetic works because root spaces are one-dimensional.

The Killing form

We now have the root system Δ as a finite set of linear functionals on 𝔥. That is the structure we want to classify. But classifying sets of linear functionals is still an abstract linear-algebra problem; we do not yet have a geometry on 𝔥* that would tell us when two roots are "close," or what angle they form, or whether they are "long" or "short."

This section supplies that geometry. We build, from nothing more than the bracket, a canonical bilinear form on 𝔤 itself, the Killing form; show that it descends to a well-defined inner product on the Cartan subalgebra; transport that inner product to 𝔥* via the canonical isomorphism 𝔥 ≅ 𝔥*; and verify that the resulting form is strictly positive definite on the real span of the roots. At that point the roots stop being abstract functionals and become genuine vectors in a real Euclidean space 𝔼.

Given a Lie algebra 𝔤, every element x ∈ 𝔤 produces a linear operator adx : 𝔤 → 𝔤. Composing two such operators gives another linear operator, adx ∘ ady, whose trace is a number, a coordinate-independent scalar extracted from the composition. That trace, as a function of the two inputs x and y, is the Killing form:

Cartan's criterion

Non-degeneracy of the Killing form is not just a nice property; it is the defining property of semisimple Lie algebras. The theorem is due to Cartan, and we take it as a black box.

Cartan's criterion. A finite-dimensional Lie algebra 𝔤 over a field of characteristic zero is semisimple if and only if its Killing form is non-degenerate: for every non-zero x ∈ 𝔤 there is some y ∈ 𝔤 with κ(x, y) ≠ 0. Equivalently, the Gram matrix of κ in any basis has non-zero determinant.

For sln, we have verified non-degeneracy by computation (for sl2 directly, for sln via κ(X, Y) = 2n tr(XY)). For an abstractly specified semisimple algebra, non-degeneracy is how one knows it is semisimple. The criterion is the bridge between "the bracket has no proper non-trivial ideals" and "we have a non-degenerate bilinear form to work with."

Cartan's criterion has a remarkable consequence: because the Killing form is non-degenerate on all of 𝔤, it remains non-degenerate when restricted to certain subspaces, in particular, to the Cartan subalgebra 𝔥. The restriction κ|𝔥 × 𝔥 is a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form on 𝔥, which lets us build an inner-product structure on 𝔥 that we can push forward to 𝔥*. That push-forward is where the roots become vectors.

Positive-definiteness on the real span

Here is the fact that anchors the whole classification.

Theorem. The induced inner product (·, ·) on the real linear span of the roots is strictly positive definite: for any non-zero ξ = Σ cα α in the real span of Δ, we have (ξ, ξ) > 0.

The proof, which we sketch rather than give, uses three ingredients. First, the Killing form of a simple Lie algebra, restricted to its Cartan, takes integer values on a specific integer lattice spanned by the co-roots. Second, for any root α, the value κ(tα, tα) equals a positive integer (essentially the number of roots in the "α-string" weighted by their α-components, a non-negative count). Third, the Gram matrix of any set of linearly independent roots is therefore symmetric with positive diagonal and interlaced integer off-diagonal entries, and a linear-algebra lemma shows that such a matrix is positive definite.

What this means, operationally, is: the root system Δ lives inside a real vector space

of real dimension equal to the rank of 𝔤, and that real vector space carries an honest Euclidean inner product. Distances between roots, angles between roots, lengths of roots, all of these are well-defined and obey the usual axioms of Euclidean geometry. From this moment on, every classification argument becomes a statement about the geometry of a finite configuration of vectors in 𝔼.

What the shift to geometry buys us

Once we are doing Euclidean geometry on Δ ⊂ 𝔼, a lot of machinery becomes available that would have been painful to derive directly from the Lie bracket. Here are three consequences we will cash in on from the next explainer onward:

  • Angles and lengths. Every root has a squared length (α, α) > 0, and every pair of roots has a well-defined angle via the standard cosine formula. In the classification, only a handful of angles are allowed (90°, 120°, 135°, 150°) and the discreteness of the allowed set is what makes the classification tractable.
  • Reflections. Each root α defines a reflection of 𝔼 across the hyperplane perpendicular to α. Because these reflections send roots to roots (this is the sl2 fact from the next explainer), they generate a finite reflection group, the Weyl group, and that group is the second main actor of the classification story.
  • Finite configurations of vectors. The root system Δ is now a finite, symmetric-about-the-origin configuration of vectors in 𝔼, and we can ask the combinatorial question: which such configurations are allowed? The answer, it turns out, is spectacularly restrictive.

The pivot. Up to this explainer we have been doing algebra: Lie brackets, ideals, semisimplicity, adjoint representations. From the next explainer forward we are doing Euclidean geometry: angles, hyperplanes, reflections, finite configurations of vectors. The bridge is κ, and the positive-definiteness theorem above is the reason the bridge holds up.

Takeaways

  • Cartan subalgebra. A maximal abelian subspace of 𝔤 all of whose elements are semisimple. Rank = dim 𝔥 is an invariant of 𝔤 itself.
  • Root space decomposition. 𝔤 = 𝔥 ⊕ ⊕α ∈ Δ 𝔤α, with each root space one-dimensional, roots paired α ↔ −α, and brackets additive on roots.
  • One-dimensionality. Turns "classify Lie algebras" into "classify finite vector configurations." |Δ| = dim 𝔤 − rank 𝔤, cleanly explaining why E8 (dim 248, rank 8) has exactly 240 roots.
  • Killing form. κ(x, y) = tr(adx ady), a canonical ad-invariant symmetric bilinear form. Non-degenerate iff 𝔤 is semisimple (Cartan's criterion).
  • Geometric root system. κ restricts non-degenerately to 𝔥, pushes forward to 𝔥* via 𝔥 ≅ 𝔥*, and is strictly positive definite on 𝔼 = spanℝ Δ. The roots are now honest vectors in a Euclidean space, with well-defined lengths and angles.

Next we use the sl2 triples embedded in 𝔤 to extract the first genuinely restrictive constraint on Δ: that Weyl reflections send roots to roots, with integer coefficients that force the pairing ⟨α, β⟩ := 2(α, β)/(β, β) to be an integer for every pair of roots. The Weyl group, which will drive the rest of the classification, emerges from that integrality.
