The McKay Correspondence

Why ADE Dynkin diagrams classify both simply-laced Lie algebras and finite subgroups of SU(2). A direct bridge from platonic rotations to the exceptional series.

The previous explainer (Part 7) closed the classification of simply-laced simple Lie algebras: An, Dn, E6, E7, E8, determined by Dynkin-diagram pruning and a single Diophantine inequality. This looks like a statement purely about Euclidean root systems. It is not.

The same list (A, D, E) classifies the finite subgroups of SU(2). Platonic rotations, which have nothing obvious to do with Lie algebras, produce the same Dynkin diagrams from an entirely different starting point: their irreducible representations. This is the McKay correspondence, discovered by John McKay in 1980. It is the first hint that the ADE Dynkin diagrams are a much deeper combinatorial object than the classification of Lie algebras suggests, and it is the first step on the road to monstrous moonshine.

This explainer builds the correspondence from the ground up. We pick a finite subgroup G of SU(2), visualise it on the 3-sphere S3, list its irreducible representations, tensor each with the fundamental 2-dimensional rep, and draw the resulting McKay quiver. What comes out is always an affine ADE Dynkin diagram, the same diagrams as the Lie-algebra classification, with one extra node added for the trivial representation.

The two ADE classifications

There are two finite lists in this story. Both have the same letters, both are indexed the same way, and they were proved a century apart from very different premises.

The Lie-algebra classification (Killing 1888, Cartan 1894, Dynkin 1947) says: every simply-laced simple complex Lie algebra has a Dynkin diagram equal to An, Dn (n ≥ 4), E6, E7, or E8. The diagrams are trees with all single edges, and their shapes are fixed by positive-definiteness of the Cartan matrix.

The finite-subgroup classification of SU(2) (Klein 1884, lifted to SU(2) by combining with the ±1 kernel) says: every finite subgroup G ⊂ SU(2) is one of five types, cyclic ℤ/n, binary dihedral BD4n, binary tetrahedral 2T, binary octahedral 2O, binary icosahedral 2I. They are the double covers of the rotational symmetry groups of the cyclic, dihedral, tetrahedral, octahedral, and icosahedral configurations on S2.

McKay's 1980 bridge says these two lists are the same list, with a precise recipe: if G ⊂ SU(2) is a finite subgroup, the McKay quiver of G equals the affine Dynkin diagram Ã, D̃, Ẽ6, Ẽ7, or Ẽ8 of the corresponding simply-laced type. The two classifications are not merely in bijection, they share an explicit combinatorial object.

A note on "affine" versus "finite." The Lie-algebra classification produces finite Dynkin diagrams An, Dn, E6–8, each with rank + 1 nodes less than the affine version. The McKay quiver always produces the affine version, with one extra node (the trivial representation) that, if deleted, recovers the finite Dynkin diagram. The extra node is the affine node, and its position is determined by the correspondence.

Finite subgroups of SU(2) live on S3

SU(2) consists of 2×2 unitary matrices of determinant 1. Writing an element as [[a, b], [-b̄, ā]] with |a|² + |b|² = 1 shows that SU(2) is the unit 3-sphere S3 ⊂ ℂ2 ≅ ℝ4. Every finite subgroup of SU(2) is therefore a finite configuration of points on S3, closed under the group product (which on S3 is quaternion multiplication, the isomorphism SU(2) ≅ S3 ≅ unit quaternions is the key viewpoint).

The five subgroup types occupy increasingly symmetric configurations:

  • ℤ/n: n points equispaced on a great circle of S3.
  • BD4n: 4n points forming a prism pattern on two great circles.
  • 2T: 24 points, the icosian ±1, ±i, ±j, ±k together with the 16 half-integer quaternions ½(±1 ± i ± j ± k).
  • 2O: 48 points, the 24 of 2T plus 24 more obtained by multiplying by ω = (1+i)/√2.
  • 2I: 120 points, the icosians, the 24 of 2T plus 96 more from even permutations of (0, ±1, ±φ, ±1/φ)/2 where φ is the golden ratio.

The McKay construction

Given a finite subgroup G ⊂ SU(2), the construction has four steps.

Step 1. List the irreducible complex representations of G. Call them ρ0 (the trivial representation, always present), ρ1, …, ρn. There are finitely many; in fact the number of irreps equals the number of conjugacy classes of G.

Step 2. Take V = ℂ2, the standard 2-dimensional representation of SU(2) restricted to G. This is the fundamental representation.

Step 3. For each irrep ρi, form the tensor product V ⊗ ρi. This is another representation of G, generally reducible. Decompose it:

where the integers aij ≥ 0 count how many copies of ρj appear in V ⊗ ρi. A remarkable fact: for a finite subgroup G ⊂ SU(2), every aij ∈ {0, 1}.

Step 4. Build a graph, the McKay quiver: one vertex per irrep ρi, and an edge between ρi and ρj whenever aij = 1. Because V is self-dual (SU(2) has V ≅ V*), the matrix aij is symmetric and we get an undirected graph.

This graph is the affine Dynkin diagram for G. The affine node is always ρ0, the trivial representation.

Building the McKay quiver

Each irrep ρi becomes a node. The edges come from decomposing V ⊗ ρi. The figure below animates this one step at a time: pick a group, then step through each irrep ρ0, ρ1, … and watch the edges appear as its tensor decomposition is computed.

For the cyclic group ℤ/n, all irreps are 1-dimensional and indexed by a character χk: g ↦ ζk where ζ = e2πi/n. The fundamental V is χ1 ⊕ χ-1, so V ⊗ χk = χk+1 ⊕ χk-1. Each node connects to its two neighbours mod n, a cycle. That's the affine Ãn-1 Dynkin diagram.

For the binary polyhedral groups, the construction produces exactly the advertised affine Dynkin diagrams. We hardcode the known decomposition matrices (they are listed in standard references such as Humphreys or Slodowy's Simple Singularities).

Why it matters

The McKay correspondence is one of the first concrete instances of what later became a guiding principle: ADE Dynkin diagrams appear in every corner of mathematics where a finite classification meets a positively-curved combinatorial structure. They show up in: simple singularities of surfaces (the Du Val classification), cluster algebras, quivers with potentials, del Pezzo surfaces, the Cartan matrices of finite reflection groups, and the simple sporadic groups that feed monstrous moonshine. In every case, the same five types (A, D, E6, E7, E8) arise, and in every case there is a reason, usually a hidden octonionic or modular connection.

John McKay's 1978 observation that the coefficients of the j-function's q-expansion are sums of dimensions of Monster irreps, which launched monstrous moonshine and led to the discovery of vertex operator algebras and generalised Kac–Moody algebras, used exactly the same mental move as the correspondence we built here: look at the irreducible representations of a mysterious group and see what combinatorial object they assemble into. The McKay correspondence for SU(2) is the warm-up; the Moonshine story, picked up in the Modular Forms series, is the full-scale payoff.

The bridge is real. A physicist who meets E8 through the 248-dimensional adjoint rep of a Lie algebra, and a geometer who meets E8 through the 120-element binary icosahedral group, are looking at the same diagram from two different directions. The affine Ẽ8 with its 9 nodes (8 finite + 1 affine) is the exact shared combinatorial skeleton.

Takeaways

  • Two ADE classifications. Simply-laced simple complex Lie algebras are classified by An, Dn, E6–8. Finite subgroups of SU(2) are classified by cyclic, binary dihedral, and the three binary polyhedral groups. Same letters, same indices.
  • The McKay quiver. Given G ⊂ SU(2), take one vertex per irrep, an edge from ρi to ρj whenever ρj ⊂ V ⊗ ρi. The result is the affine Dynkin diagram of the matching ADE type.
  • Affine versus finite. The McKay quiver is the affine extension. Deleting the ρ0 node recovers the finite Dynkin diagram that shows up in Cartan's classification.
  • Dimension = Dynkin label. The dimension of ρi equals the Dynkin mark of the corresponding node in the affine diagram. For 2I → Ẽ8, the marks (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 4, 3, 2) match the dimensions of the nine irreps exactly.
  • Why it is a deep correspondence. The equality is not a coincidence of shapes. It is the first of a long chain of ADE coincidences, Du Val singularities, cluster algebras, monstrous moonshine, each of which reveals a new geometric meaning for the A, D, E letters.
