sl2 Ladders & Weyl Reflections

Part 2 of 9. Every root carries an embedded copy of sl2(ℂ), and sl2 representation theory forces an integer constraint on every pair of roots. The reflections that follow from it generate a finite group, the Weyl group, which rebuilds the whole root system from a single root.

Part 1 put a Euclidean inner product on the real span of the roots but said nothing yet about how the roots can sit. The constraint comes from a copy of sl2(ℂ) attached to each root α, whose Cartan element can only have integer eigenvalues. By the end of this article every root α carries a reflection sα: 𝔼 → 𝔼 that permutes Δ, and these reflections generate the Weyl group W. In E8, the eight simple reflections applied to one root reach all 240.

One sl2 per root

Fix α ∈ Δ. By the four facts of Part 1, −α ∈ Δ, both 𝔤α and 𝔤−α are one-dimensional, and [𝔤α, 𝔤−α] ⊆ 𝔥. Pick nonzero eα ∈ 𝔤α and fα ∈ 𝔤−α; their bracket [eα, fα] is some nonzero hα ∈ 𝔥, equal up to normalisation to the dual Cartan element tα.

Rescaling eα and fα makes (eα, fα, hα) satisfy the standard sl2(ℂ) relations:

So 𝔤α ⊕ 𝔤−α ⊕ ℂ·hα is a Lie subalgebra of 𝔤 isomorphic to sl2(ℂ), with H ↦ hα, E ↦ eα, F ↦ fα. There is one embedded sl2 per pair {α, −α}, and these |Δ|/2 subalgebras together cover every direction in 𝔤 outside the Cartan. A semisimple Lie algebra is built from overlapping copies of one 3-dimensional algebra, so facts about sl2 transport back to 𝔤. The one we need is integrality.

Integrality, from sl2 representation theory

We use one fact about sl2(ℂ) without proof; it is a standard exercise with E raising and F lowering between H-eigenspaces. Every finite-dimensional complex representation V of sl2(ℂ) is a direct sum of H-eigenspaces with integer eigenvalues, and those eigenvalues are symmetric about 0: if k is an H-eigenvalue on V, so is −k, with the same eigenspace dimension.

The adjoint action makes 𝔤 a representation of sl2(α), with the basis acting as adhα, adeα, adfα. So the eigenvalues of adhα are integers.

Those eigenvalues are the values of the other roots β on hα: for y ∈ 𝔤β, [hα, y] = β(hα) · y, so β(hα) ∈ ℤ for every pair α, β.

Weyl reflections

The Cartan integers feed into a reflection. Fix α ∈ Δ and define sα: 𝔼 → 𝔼 by

This sα is the Euclidean reflection across the hyperplane orthogonal to α, since sα(α) = −α and sα(ξ) = ξ for ξ ⊥ α. It is linear, it is its own inverse, and sα(β) differs from β by the integer multiple −nβ,α of α. The same sl2 representation theory shows more: sα(β) is itself a root.

So each root gives a symmetry of Δ. This closure is a property of the root system; the formula alone would not guarantee it.

The Weyl group W is the subgroup of the orthogonal group O(𝔼) generated by all the reflections sα for α ∈ Δ:

W permutes Δ, and since Δ spans 𝔼 an element of W is determined by that permutation, so W embeds in the symmetric group on |Δ| letters and is finite. Every simple Lie algebra over ℂ therefore determines a finite set Δ ⊂ 𝔼 with integer Cartan integers, closed under the finite reflection group W. Classifying simple Lie algebras comes down to classifying such sets.

Orbits of the Weyl group

The orbit of α is the smallest W-stable set containing it, found by applying reflections until nothing new appears. In a simply-laced root system, where all roots have the same length, W acts transitively and there is one orbit. A2 (the sl3 hexagon) and all the ADE types are simply-laced. In a non-simply-laced system such as B2, C2, B3, G2 or F4, reflections preserve length, so there are two orbits, the long roots and the short roots.

A specific check: the B2 Cartan integers

The Cartan integers of B2 can be computed by hand. The simple roots are α1 = e1 − e2 (long, squared length 2) and α2 = e2 (short, squared length 1):

  • nα₁, α₁ = 2(α1, α1)/(α1, α1) = 2, same for nα₂, α₂; diagonal entries are always 2.
  • (α1, α2) = −1, (α2, α2) = 1, so nα₁, α₂ = 2·(−1)/1 = −2 ∈ ℤ.
  • (α1, α1) = 2, so nα₂, α₁ = 2·(−1)/2 = −1 ∈ ℤ.

The off-diagonal Cartan integers are −2 and −1, integers but unequal, which happens only when the two roots have different lengths. Their product nα₁, α₂ · nα₂, α₁ = 2 controls the angle via nα, β · nβ, α = 4 cos²θ.

One reflection on all 240 of E8

For E8 all 240 roots have squared length 2, so sα(x) = x − ⟨x, α⟩ · α. For any simple root αi, the reflection fixes the 126 roots perpendicular to αi and moves the remaining 114.

The size of the E8 Weyl group

Every word in s1, …, s8 is a symmetry of E8, and the set of all of them is W(E8), of order 696,729,600 = 214 · 35 · 52 · 7. For comparison, the Weyl group of A8 is the symmetric group S9, of order 9! = 362,880, and that of D8 has order 27 · 8! = 5,160,960. E8's is 1,920 and 135 times larger.

Every element is a product of at most 120 simple reflections, one for each positive root; 120 is the length of the longest element.

Integrality is not the only constraint. Since nα, β · nβ, α = 4 cos²θ ∈ [0, 4], the Cartan integers lie in {0, ±1, ±2, ±3, ±4}. Part 3 turns that into the crystallographic restriction: only four angles between roots are possible.
