Exceptional Atlas

Fifteen interactive explainers. One arc from the definition of a Lie algebra, through the Cartan–Killing classification and the octonion current that feeds it, all the way to E₈, the optimal sphere packing in eight dimensions, and the Leech lattice in twenty-four.

In 1888 Wilhelm Killing published a rough classification of the finite-dimensional simple Lie algebras over ℂ. Six years later Élie Cartan published a corrected and rigorously proved version. Half a century after that, Eugene Dynkin reframed the result as a short list of small diagrams, nodes connected by single, double, or triple edges, which you can draw on the back of a napkin. Four infinite families An, Bn, Cn, Dn, five singular exceptions G2, F4, E6, E7, E8. No tenth species exists, at any rank.

The five exceptions are not random. They sit on top of a second classification, Hurwitz's theorem of 1898, which says there are exactly four real normed division algebras, ℝ, ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆. The last one, the octonions, drags along a tail: G₂ is the automorphism algebra of 𝕆, F₄ is the derivation algebra of the 3×3 octonionic Hermitian matrices, and E₆, E₇, E₈ fill out the Freudenthal magic square alongside it. Everything exceptional in finite-dimensional semisimple Lie theory is an octonionic phenomenon.

And E₈ itself, once you have it, is not just an algebra: it is a lattice. Its 240 roots are the shortest vectors of the densest sphere packing possible in 8 dimensions, a fact Maryna Viazovska proved in 2016. Combine E₈ with itself three times and add the Golay code, and you get the Leech lattice Λ₂₄, the densest packing in 24 dimensions, whose automorphism group leads straight to the Monster and monstrous moonshine.

This atlas treats all of that as one story in five acts.

The Nine Simple Lie Algebras

At the end of the classification, the full list consists of these nine types. The first four are infinite families parameterised by rank; the last five are singular.
