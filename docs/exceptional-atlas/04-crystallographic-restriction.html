The Crystallographic Restriction

Part 4 of 15. Integrality of the Cartan numbers forces every angle between roots to be exactly one of four values: 90°, 120°, 135°, or 150°. No other angle is possible. This discrete quantisation is why simple Lie algebras are so rigid, and it is the first real theorem of Act II.

Act I ended with a finite configuration of vectors, the root system Δ, sitting in a real Euclidean space 𝔼, and a Weyl group W acting on it by reflections. The Weyl reflection formula has one ingredient that is not negotiable: the Cartan integer nβ, α = 2(β, α)/(α, α) must be an integer, for every ordered pair of roots (α, β). We got that integrality from sl2 representation theory in explainer 5. What we did not do yet is work out its geometric consequences.

This explainer works them out. The integrality constraint, combined with Cauchy-Schwarz, restricts the possible angles between two roots to exactly four values, 90°, 120°, 135°, and 150°. That is the crystallographic restriction. It reduces an uncountable continuum of possible pairwise configurations to a discrete list of four, and every subsequent classification argument will be a combinatorial walk through those four options.

The four legal angles

Converting each of the four integer values back to an angle:

  • Product 0 ⇒ cos²θ = 0 ⇒ θ = 90°. The roots are orthogonal, both Cartan integers are zero, and bracketing them gives zero by the bracket-additivity rule from explainer 3 (since α + β would have to live in an unrelated direction, but by orthogonality it cannot).
  • Product 1 ⇒ cos²θ = 1/4 ⇒ θ = 60° or 120°. One Cartan integer is ±1, the other is ±1. The roots have the same squared length: (α, α) = (β, β). This is the simply-laced case.
  • Product 2 ⇒ cos²θ = 1/2 ⇒ θ = 45° or 135°. One Cartan integer is ±1, the other is ±2. The squared lengths are in ratio 2:1, so |α|/|β| = √2 (or its reciprocal).
  • Product 3 ⇒ cos²θ = 3/4 ⇒ θ = 30° or 150°. One Cartan integer is ±1, the other is ±3. The squared lengths are in ratio 3:1, so |α|/|β| = √3 (or its reciprocal).

Notice the pattern in the second and third rows: whenever nα, β ≠ nβ, α, the roots have different lengths, and the "more negative" Cartan integer comes from the longer root (pairing with the shorter length in the denominator 2/(α, α) produces a larger magnitude). So the two integers are asymmetric precisely because the roots are of different lengths, and the ratio of lengths is √k, where k is the product of the two integers.

Simple roots are obtuse. For a pair of simple roots (meaning both are positive and part of a minimal generating set for the root system, the construction of simple roots is the next explainer), one can show their mutual inner product (α, β) must be ≤ 0. This cuts the 60° / 120° pair down to 120° only, and similarly the 45° / 135° pair to 135° only, and 30° / 150° to 150° only. So between two distinct simple roots, the angle is exactly one of 90°, 120°, 135°, or 150°, and nothing else.

All four types of Dynkin edge

Each of the four allowed configurations corresponds to a different kind of edge in a Dynkin diagram, the graph encoding we will build in the next explainer. The edge between two nodes α and β records whether (and how strongly) they interact:

Product Angle (simple-root) Cartan integers Length ratio Dynkin edge Seen in
0 90° (0, 0) any no edge orthogonal generators
1 120° (−1, −1) 1 : 1 single edge A, D, E
2 135° (−1, −2) 1 : √2 double edge with arrow B, C, F4
3 150° (−1, −3) 1 : √3 triple edge with arrow G2

The five entries in the right-most column are the only places triple-edged configurations show up in the classification. A triple edge means a pair of simple roots at 150° with length ratio √3, and that configuration is so restrictive that it can only appear once in a rank-2 root system, G2. No simple Lie algebra of rank 3 or more contains a triple edge. The double edge is a little more permissive: it appears in Bn, Cn, and F4, and it can only appear at the end of a chain (Bn, Cn) or between two simply-laced arms (F4). And the simple edge, corresponding to the "nice" 120° case, is what builds the entire ADE family, the types whose Dynkin diagrams have only single edges.

Why the classification is now a combinatorics problem

Here is what the crystallographic restriction buys us in one sentence: the root system of a simple Lie algebra is a finite set of vectors in Euclidean space, any two of which are either orthogonal or meet at 120°, 135°, or 150° (and in the non-orthogonal cases their lengths are in ratio 1, √2, or √3). That is a spectacularly restrictive condition, and it reduces the classification problem to a question about graphs rather than about vector configurations in the abstract.

Specifically, once we know the angles and length ratios between every pair of simple roots (a carefully chosen minimal generating set, coming in explainer 7), we can reconstruct the full root system by applying Weyl reflections. And the angle and length data for simple roots lives in a Cartan matrix, an n × n integer matrix whose off-diagonal entries tell us which of the four edge types we are looking at. Each Cartan matrix determines a Dynkin diagram (explainer 7 again), and the classification becomes: which connected graphs can be Dynkin diagrams of a positive-definite Cartan matrix?

Answering that question is exactly what explainers 9 and 10 will do. The argument is remarkably short: first we show that every admissible Dynkin diagram must be a tree (no loops) and every node must have valency at most 3. Second, once we know we have a tree with valency bounded by 3, we count the possibilities using a spherical-Diophantine inequality 1/p + 1/q + 1/r > 1 on the arm lengths of the tree. The inequality has six integer solutions, four coming from classical families and three exceptional, and that's where E6, E7, and E8 come from.

What the discreteness gives us. Before the crystallographic restriction, the classification problem was "what symmetric configurations of vectors in ℝn are closed under Weyl reflections?", an unbounded-looking question. After the restriction, the problem is "which integer matrices of a specific form have strictly positive eigenvalues?", a finite, combinatorial question that yields to a one-page proof. Integrality collapses the continuum of possible angles to just four, and that collapse is what makes the classification finite.

Takeaways

  • The Diophantine identity. nα, β · nβ, α = 4 cos²θ, where both factors are integers and cos²θ ∈ [0, 1]. The product is therefore an integer in {0, 1, 2, 3, 4}, and ruling out linearly-dependent roots (product 4) leaves {0, 1, 2, 3}.
  • Four legal angles. 90°, 120°, 135°, 150°. No other angle is compatible with integrality of the Cartan numbers for a pair of simple roots.
  • Length ratios. When the product is 2 the roots are in ratio 1 : √2; when 3, in ratio 1 : √3. Same length corresponds to product 0 (orthogonal) or product 1 (120° simple edge).
  • Simply-laced vs non-simply-laced. ADE types are simply-laced (all roots the same length, all edges single). BCFG types have two length classes, and their Dynkin diagrams contain at least one double or triple edge.
  • Handoff to Dynkin diagrams. The four legal configurations correspond exactly to the four kinds of Dynkin edge: no edge, single edge, double edge with arrow, triple edge with arrow. The next explainer uses these as the building blocks of a graph-theoretic encoding.

We have compressed the continuum of possible Lie algebras into a finite combinatorial question. The next explainer turns that combinatorics into a single diagram per algebra.
