240 Roots: Gosset Polytope and Coxeter Plane

Part 8 of 9. E₈'s 240 roots can be written down by hand. They are the vertices of Gosset's 421; projected onto the Coxeter plane they form 8 rings of 30, and projected onto a 4-dimensional subspace they fall onto two 600-cells whose sizes differ by the golden ratio.

Parts 3 and 4 met E₈ as a Dynkin diagram, the last branched tree that passes the positivity test. This article works with the 240 roots themselves: as coordinates, as the vertices of a polytope, and in the projections that made E₈ famous.

The Coxeter plane

The familiar picture of E₈ is a projection onto one particular plane in ℝ⁸, the plane where a Coxeter element acts as its slowest rotation. A Coxeter element is the product of all eight simple reflections, each used once:

Each ring is one orbit of c. Every orbit has exactly 30 roots, so there are 240 / 30 = 8 orbits; Coxeter number times rank is the number of roots in every irreducible root system. Since c rotates the plane by 12°, each orbit lands on a regular 30-gon, a Petrie polygon of 421, and the eight orbits sit at eight different radii.

The golden fold

In the library's normalisation the eight ring radii are 0.238, 0.385, 0.474, 0.573, 0.704, 0.767, 0.927 and 1.140, and they pair off in the golden ratio: 0.385 / 0.238, 0.767 / 0.474, 0.927 / 0.573 and 1.140 / 0.704 all equal φ = (1 + √5)/2. The plane alone cannot explain the pairing.

The exponents split into two sets, {1, 11, 19, 29} and {7, 13, 17, 23}. The first is the list of exponents of H4, the symmetry group of the 600-cell; the second is its image under multiplication by 7 modulo 30, the same list seen through the other square root of 5. Their eigenplanes span two orthogonal 4-dimensional subspaces of ℝ⁸, each preserved by c. Project the 240 roots onto the first one and they fall onto two concentric 600-cells: 120 roots at radius √(1 − 1/√5) ≈ 0.744 and 120 at radius √(1 + 1/√5) ≈ 1.203, a ratio of exactly φ. Project onto the second subspace and the same thing happens with the roles exchanged: a root on the inner shell in one is on the outer shell in the other. Since the two subspaces are orthogonal complements, the squared radii of each root add to 2.

Each 600-cell carries a finer structure. Its 120 vertices, read as unit quaternions, form the binary icosahedral group 2I, which contains the binary tetrahedral group 2T of order 24 as a subgroup of index 5. The five cosets of 2T are five disjoint 24-cells, so the 240 roots split into ten 24-cells, five per shell. Both subspaces are needed because E8 is built from the golden field ℚ(√5), which has two embeddings into the reals, sending √5 to +√5 or to −√5. The icosians, the ℤ[φ]-combinations of the 120 elements of 2I, form a ring that is a free abelian group of rank 8. Measured with a suitable rational quadratic form that uses both embeddings of the golden field at once (Conway and Sloane give it in chapter 8 of Sphere Packings, Lattices and Groups), that rank-8 group is the E8 lattice. The two 4-dimensional projections are the two embeddings, and φ ↦ −1/φ is what exchanges inner and outer shells.

From H3 to H4

The 600-cell itself can be built from 3-dimensional symmetry. The icosahedron has 15 mirror planes, and their unit normals, with both signs, are the 30 roots of H3. In the Clifford algebra of ℝ³, the product nm of two unit vectors is a scalar plus a bivector, and the even part of the algebra, spanned by 1 and the three bivectors, is a copy of the quaternions. Composing two reflections gives a rotation, and nm is the corresponding rotor.

The 465 unordered pairs produce exactly 120 distinct unit quaternions. When the run finishes, the page reports the inner products among them, which are the cosines of multiples of 36°: these are the 120 vertices of the 600-cell, the binary icosahedral group 2I.

The construction stops at H4. Adding the 120 odd elements of the Clifford algebra (vectors and trivectors) gives 240 unit vectors in the 8-dimensional algebra, but under its Euclidean inner product they form two orthogonal copies of the 600-cell. Their inner products include cos 72° ≈ 0.309, which never occurs between unit E8 roots, where only 0, ±½ and ±1 are possible. The Clifford algebra supplies the two copies of H4; the golden-field quadratic form above is what glues them into E8.
