F₄ and E₆

Part 11 of 15. Two rank-adjacent exceptions built on the exceptional Jordan algebra h₃(𝕆). F₄ (rank 4, dim 52) is the derivation algebra of the 27-dimensional Albert algebra, and its 24 long roots are the vertices of the regular 24-cell. E₆ (rank 6, dim 78) enlarges F₄ to the isometry algebra of the octonionic projective plane 𝕆P², and its Weyl group is the symmetry group of the 27 lines on a smooth cubic surface.

After G2, the next exception on the classification list is F4. It is a rank-4 algebra of dimension 52, and it sits at the crossroads between the classical orthogonal and symplectic families and the exceptional E-series. Its Dynkin diagram has four nodes in a row, with the middle edge doubled, F4 is the unique simple Lie algebra whose Dynkin diagram has a double edge that is not at an end of the chain. That middle position is what makes it an exception rather than a classical B/C algebra, and it has geometric consequences we will see in this explainer.

Two facts about F4 are worth knowing before any details. First, its 24 long roots are exactly the vertices of the 24-cell, the only self-dual regular 4-polytope, with 24 vertices, 96 edges, 96 triangular faces, and 24 octahedral cells. It is unique to four dimensions and has no analogue in any other dimension. Second, F4 is the derivation algebra of the exceptional Jordan algebra h3(𝕆), the 27-dimensional algebra of 3 × 3 Hermitian octonionic matrices. Both facts are consequences of the same arithmetic, and both are characteristic of F4 specifically.

Where F₄ sits

In the classification walk, F4 is reached by the non-simply-laced branch of Act II's argument. The graph pruning in explainer 9 allowed chains with a double edge at the middle, subject to the constraint that the two halves of the chain have bounded length. Running that finite check gives exactly one possible shape:

  • 4 nodes, arranged in a linear chain.
  • The middle edge (between nodes 2 and 3) is a double edge.
  • Arrow on the double edge points from node 2 (long) to node 3 (short).

The Cartan matrix of F4 is

a 4 × 4 integer matrix with diagonal 2, first-off-diagonal entries −1 everywhere except the asymmetric middle pair where A23 = −2 and A32 = −1, and other entries zero. The ratio of squared lengths between long and short simple roots is 2, so |αlong|/|αshort| = √2.

The 48 roots and the 24-cell

F4 has 48 roots in a 4-dimensional Euclidean space, split evenly between 24 long and 24 short roots. The long roots (squared length 2) are the set

There are 4·C(4, 2) = 24 such vectors, two sign choices for each pair (i, j), and C(4, 2) = 6 pairs. These 24 vectors are exactly the vertices of the 24-cell, the regular 4-dimensional polytope with 24 vertices, each at distance √2 from the origin. They are the Weyl orbit of any single long root under the Weyl group of F4, which is a reflection group of order 1152.

The short roots (squared length 1) are the other 24 roots:

8 roots of the form ±ei and 16 roots of the form ½(±e1 ± e2 ± e3 ± e4). These are also the vertices of a 24-cell, dual to the long-root 24-cell, a fact which reflects the self-duality of the 24-cell, another unique feature of dimension 4.

The exceptional Jordan algebra

Just as G2 is the derivation algebra of the octonions 𝕆, F4 is the derivation algebra of a different octonionic structure: the exceptional Jordan algebra, denoted h3(𝕆) or sometimes 𝕁3(𝕆). This is the 27-dimensional algebra of 3 × 3 Hermitian octonionic matrices, introduced in Part 10's magic-square construction. A general element has the form

a z ȳ
z̄ b x
y x̄ c

Figure 2. A general element of the 27-dimensional exceptional Jordan algebra h3(𝕆). The three diagonal entries a, b, c ∈ ℝ account for 3 real degrees of freedom; the three off-diagonal pairs (x, x̄), (y, ȳ), (z, z̄) each contribute one octonion (8 real degrees of freedom), so 3 × 8 = 24 more. Total: 3 + 24 = 27. Under the Jordan product A ∘ B = ½(AB + BA), this space forms a commutative (but non-associative) algebra, a Jordan algebra, and its derivation algebra is F4.

For ordinary associative matrices, Hermitian matrices form a Jordan algebra because the symmetrised product ½(AB + BA) of two Hermitian matrices is again Hermitian. The same thing works formally for octonionic matrices, except that because octonion multiplication is non-associative, most of the usual matrix machinery breaks down. What survives is exactly the Jordan product, and h3(𝕆) is the only such Jordan algebra that fails to be a "special" (i.e., associative-derived) Jordan algebra.

This makes h3(𝕆) the exceptional Jordan algebra, exceptional in the precise sense that it does not arise from any associative construction. Jordan algebras were invented by Pascual Jordan in 1933 as a framework for quantum observables (Hermitian operators under their symmetrised product), and the classification of finite-dimensional Jordan algebras over ℝ was completed by Jordan, von Neumann, and Wigner in 1934. The classification consists of four infinite families plus one exception: h3(𝕆). Every other finite-dimensional Jordan algebra fits into a classical family. h3(𝕆) stands alone.

And its derivation algebra is F4:

This is the exceptional Lie algebra analogue of G2 = Der(𝕆). Where G2 is 14-dimensional (the derivations of an 8-dimensional algebra), F4 is 52-dimensional (the derivations of a 27-dimensional algebra). Note 52 + 3 · 8 + 1 = 78 and 78 is not a coincidence, it is dim(E6), a preview of how the exceptional Jordan algebra will reappear in explainer 13.

Why the 24-cell?

Back to the root system. Why are the 24 long roots of F4 exactly the 24-cell? The short answer is that the Weyl group of F4, acting transitively on the long roots, is a 4-dimensional reflection group of order 1152, and that group is the full symmetry group of the regular 24-cell. So the long roots are forced to be a Weyl-group orbit of a single vector, and the Weyl group is large enough that its orbit on a unit-length starting vector traces out a regular polytope, and in dimension 4, that polytope can only be the 24-cell.

A sharper way to see it: the 24-cell is the 4-dimensional analogue of the octahedron (whose vertices are ±ei in 3D) with the additional 8 vertices ±ei ± ej / √2 thrown in (the "ambo" construction or the midpoints of certain edges of the 4-cube). This extra symmetry comes from a lucky numerical fact: the ratio of edge length to diagonal for the 3-cube is √2, and in 4D this ratio conspires to produce a regular polytope. The same trick fails in every other dimension, the analogous construction in dimension n ≠ 4 gives a non-regular polytope, which is why the 24-cell has no siblings.

F4's root system and the 24-cell's vertex set are thus the same data, viewed from two angles: F4 says "here are the 48 root vectors of a simple Lie algebra," and the 24-cell says "here are the 24 vertices of a regular 4-polytope, twice" (once for long roots, once for short). The same 4-dimensional accident underwrites both.

Why F4 is exceptional. The double edge in the middle of the F4 Dynkin diagram corresponds to the non-simply-laced "kink" between the long and short halves of the root system. In classical types Bn, Cn, the analogous kink is always at the end of the chain, producing a whole family of algebras indexed by rank. In F4, the kink is in the middle, and the finite valency constraint from explainer 9 forces the chain to have at most two nodes on each side of the double edge. That gives exactly one diagram, rank 4, and no higher or lower analogue. F4 is singular precisely because the "middle double edge" shape cannot grow.

F₄ in connection to the rest

F4 connects to the other exceptional Lie algebras in two important ways. First, it is the derivation algebra of h3(𝕆), and the exceptional Jordan algebra is the key building block for the E-series that follows. Specifically, there is a construction due to Tits and Freudenthal called the magic square, which builds a Lie algebra from any pair (𝔸, 𝔹) of normed division algebras; the entries where 𝔹 = 𝕆 produce F4, E6, E7, and E8 for 𝔸 = ℝ, ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆 respectively. F4 is the simplest of the four and is covered in more detail in explainer 15.

Second, F4 has a direct embedding inside E6: the E6 Lie algebra has an involutive automorphism whose fixed points form an F4 subalgebra. This embedding is the reason F4 and E6 share many structural features, their Coxeter numbers are both 12, for instance, and it underpins the construction of the Cayley plane as an F4-homogeneous space sitting inside an E6-homogeneous space.

Takeaways

  • The Dynkin signature. F4 has 4 simple roots in a linear chain with a double edge in the middle (between α2 and α3), arrow from α2 (long) to α3 (short). It is the only simple Lie algebra with this shape.
  • Root system. 48 roots in ℝ4: 24 long roots of squared length 2, and 24 short roots of squared length 1. The 24 long roots are the vertices of the regular 24-cell.
  • The 24-cell. The only self-dual regular 4-polytope with no analogue in any other dimension. Its 24 vertices, 96 edges, 96 triangular faces, and 24 octahedral cells match the F4 long-root system exactly. Weyl group |W(F4)| = 1152 = full symmetry group of the 24-cell.
  • F4 = Der(h3(𝕆)). F4 is the derivation algebra of the 27-dimensional exceptional Jordan algebra of 3 × 3 Hermitian octonionic matrices, the only non-special Jordan algebra in the 1934 Jordan–von Neumann–Wigner classification.
  • Dimension 52. Rank 4 + |Δ| = 48 = dim 52.
  • Handoff to the E-series. The exceptional Jordan algebra h3(𝕆) is also the substrate of E6, which acts on its 27 dimensions as the "isometry algebra" of the octonionic projective plane 𝕆P2, the Cayley plane. F4 is the stabiliser of a distinguished point of 𝕆P2 inside E6, and the two algebras are the first two steps of the Freudenthal magic square.

Next we climb one step up, from the derivation algebra of h3(𝕆) to the full structure algebra, and meet E6.

E₆ and the Cayley plane

We have now climbed to the simply-laced half of the exceptional list. The Diophantine inequality 1/p + 1/q + 1/r > 1 has three "branched" solutions: (3, 3, 2), (4, 3, 2), and (5, 3, 2), giving E6, E7, and E8. This section covers E6, the smallest, and walks through its root system, its connection to the octonionic projective plane, and the remarkable fact that its Weyl group is the symmetry group of the 27 lines on a cubic surface, one of the most celebrated classical configurations in all of algebraic geometry.

Where E₆ sits

The (3, 3, 2) Dynkin diagram has three arms of lengths 3, 3, 2 emanating from a central branch node. Counting all nodes (the branch plus 2 nodes on each length-3 arm plus 1 node on the length-2 arm): 1 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 6. So E6 is a rank-6 algebra.

In Bourbaki labelling, the main chain is α1, α3, α4, α5, α6, and α2 branches off α4 (the central node). The Cartan matrix is a 6 × 6 integer matrix with the standard ADE pattern, all −1 off-diagonal entries where an edge is present, 0 elsewhere:

Because E6 is simply-laced, all roots have the same squared length, and the diagonal entries are 2 with no asymmetric off-diagonal entries (−2 or −3) anywhere. The structural numbers: rank 6, dim 78, |Δ| = 72, Coxeter number 12, Weyl group order 51,840.

E₆ as the isometry algebra of 𝕆P²

The classical definition of E6 builds on the exceptional Jordan algebra h3(𝕆) above. F4 derivations preserve the trace of an element of h3(𝕆) (since D(1) = 0 and the trace is the coefficient of the identity). E6 comes from enlarging F4 to the full reduced structure algebra of h3(𝕆), the Lie algebra of all linear maps that preserve the cubic form

where det is a well-defined cubic function on h3(𝕆) despite the non-associativity of the octonion product (one of the small miracles that make h3(𝕆) an exceptional structure). The Lie algebra of linear maps preserving det is E6, with dimension 78, a 26-dimensional enlargement of F4 that accounts for the 52 dimensions of F4 plus a 26-dimensional "non-derivation" piece that scales and reshapes h3(𝕆).

Geometrically, the projectivisation of h3(𝕆), that is, the set of 1-dimensional subspaces of h3(𝕆), contains a distinguished 16-real-dimensional subvariety called the octonionic projective plane:

This is the largest honest-to-goodness projective plane that can be built with coordinates from a normed division algebra. Projective planes over ℝ, ℂ, ℍ exist in all dimensions, but projective spaces over 𝕆 only exist up to dimension 2; the non-associativity prevents you from going any higher. The Cayley plane 𝕆P² is therefore a unique object in the landscape of projective geometry, and its group of motions, the "isometry group" relative to the octonionic metric, is precisely E6.

More precisely: the real compact form of E6 acts on 𝕆P² by octonionic collineations. F4 sits inside E6 as the stabiliser of a chosen point of 𝕆P², and the quotient E6 / F4 is homeomorphic to the 16-dimensional real manifold 𝕆P². This is directly analogous to SO(3) / SO(2) = S2 and PGL3(ℂ) / (stabiliser) = ℂP², with the octonionic exception replacing the standard setups.

The 27 lines on a cubic surface

Here is where E6 enters classical algebraic geometry. A smooth cubic surface in three-dimensional complex projective space ℂP³ is the zero set of a homogeneous polynomial of degree 3 in four variables. Any such surface (assuming the coefficients are generic) contains exactly 27 complex lines, a famous 19th-century count due to Cayley and Salmon (1849). The 27 lines come with a rich combinatorial structure: each line meets exactly 10 of the other 26, and is disjoint from the remaining 16.

The intersection graph of the 27 lines, with a node per line and an edge whenever two lines meet, is a specific 10-regular graph on 27 vertices that can be described combinatorially in several equivalent ways. One particularly clean description uses the six distinguished points used to construct a cubic surface by blowing up ℂP² at those points. The 27 lines split naturally into three groups of sizes 6 + 15 + 6:

  • 6 exceptional lines E1, …, E6, one for each of the blown-up points.
  • 15 proper lines Lij for 1 ≤ i < j ≤ 6, the strict transform of the line in ℂP² through blown-up points i and j.
  • 6 conic lines C1, …, C6, the strict transform of the conic through the five points other than i.

The intersection rules are a short combinatorial table, derivable from the blow-up picture:

  • Ei meets Ej: never (i ≠ j).
  • Ei meets Ljk: iff i ∈ {j, k}.
  • Ei meets Cj: iff i ≠ j.
  • Lij meets Lkl: iff {i, j} ∩ {k, l} = ∅.
  • Lij meets Ck: iff k ∈ {i, j}.
  • Ci meets Cj: never (i ≠ j).

Counting for any single line: each has exactly 10 intersecting partners, a direct verification from the rules above. The Weyl group of E6, which has order 51,840, is the full automorphism group of this 27-vertex, 135-edge graph, it acts by symmetries that preserve the intersection pattern. This is why the Weyl group of E6 is sometimes called the group of the 27 lines, and the connection was understood by Cayley, Jordan, and Klein in the late 19th century, long before the classification of Lie algebras was complete.

E₆ and particle physics

E6 makes a cameo in the physics literature as a candidate grand unified theory (GUT) gauge group. The Standard Model of particle physics has gauge group SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1), a 12-dimensional product group describing the strong, weak, and electromagnetic interactions. Many attempts to unify these three forces into a single simple group use larger simple Lie algebras: SU(5) (Georgi–Glashow 1974), SO(10) (Fritzsch–Minkowski, Georgi 1975), and E6 (Gürsey–Ramond–Sikivie 1976). E6 is the smallest simple Lie algebra whose complex adjoint representation contains the Standard Model group with the right fermion content, specifically, one generation of quarks and leptons (including the right-handed neutrino) fits into the 27-dimensional fundamental representation of E6, which is exactly the Jordan algebra dimension.

This numerological match, 27 quarks-and-leptons ↔ 27 dimensions of h3(𝕆) ↔ 27 lines on a cubic surface, is the reason E6 keeps turning up in both physics and algebraic geometry despite being a "small exception." Whether any of these connections is physically meaningful remains open, but the mathematical structure is rigid enough that it deserves a second look.

Three 27s in a row. (1) The exceptional Jordan algebra h3(𝕆) has dimension 27. (2) A smooth cubic surface has 27 lines. (3) The fundamental representation of E6 has dimension 27. All three are the same object seen from three angles: the Jordan algebra's 27 coordinates, the cubic surface's 27 lines, and the E6-module's 27 weights. Knowing one tells you the other two, once you know how to translate.

Takeaways

  • F4. Rank 4, dim 52, 48 roots (24 long + 24 short). Double edge in the middle of the Dynkin diagram. The 24 long roots are the vertices of the regular 24-cell. F4 ≅ Der(h3(𝕆)), the derivation algebra of the 27-dimensional exceptional Jordan algebra, the only non-special Jordan algebra.
  • E6. Rank 6, dim 78, 72 roots. Simply-laced with arms (3, 3, 2). Reduced structure algebra of h3(𝕆), enlarging F4 by 26 dimensions. The compact real form of E6 is the isometry group of the Cayley plane 𝕆P², and E6 / F4 ≅ 𝕆P².
  • 27 = 27 = 27. The fundamental representation of E6 is 27-dimensional, equal to dim h3(𝕆) and equal to the number of lines on a smooth cubic surface. The Weyl group of E6 (order 51,840) is the automorphism group of the 27-line incidence graph.
  • Grand unified theories. E6 is a candidate GUT gauge group, containing the Standard Model and accommodating one generation of fermions in the 27.
