Lie Algebras, Cartan and Roots

Part 1 of 9. Diagonalise a maximal commuting family inside a Lie algebra and the algebra splits into one-dimensional pieces labelled by vectors called roots. The Killing form turns those roots into a finite configuration in Euclidean space, and the rest of the series classifies such configurations.

By the Cartan–Killing theorem, every finite-dimensional simple Lie algebra over ℂ is, up to isomorphism, in one of four infinite families, the classical types A, B, C, D, or one of five exceptions: G2, F4, E6, E7, E8. This article covers the part of the proof that turns a bracket into a set of vectors.

The bracket

Lie algebras come from Lie groups, groups that are also smooth manifolds, like the rotation group SO(3). Near the identity a Lie group looks like its tangent space. For SO(3) that space is 3-dimensional: infinitesimal rotations, each an axis with an angular speed. The vector space alone forgets most of the group. The Lie bracket records the second-order failure of two infinitesimal motions to commute, and that is enough to rebuild the group near the identity.

Abstractly, a Lie algebra is a vector space 𝔤 (over ℂ throughout) with a bracket [·, ·]: 𝔤 × 𝔤 → 𝔤 that obeys three laws:

  1. Bilinearity.
  2. Antisymmetry.
  3. Jacobi identity.

The bracket is not associative; the Jacobi identity takes its place. Bilinearity and antisymmetry give [x, x] = 0 for every x.

The prototype is sl2(ℂ), the 2×2 complex matrices with trace zero, with bracket the commutator [X, Y] = XY − YX. The commutator of any two matrices has trace zero because tr(XY) = tr(YX), so the bracket stays inside sl2. It is three-dimensional, spanned by

Simple algebras and the adjoint action

An ideal 𝔦 ⊂ 𝔤 is a subspace with [x, y] ∈ 𝔦 for every x ∈ 𝔤 and y ∈ 𝔦. Ideals play the role of normal subgroups: 𝔤/𝔦 inherits a bracket exactly when 𝔦 is an ideal. A Lie algebra is simple if it is non-abelian and its only ideals are {0} and 𝔤. Every finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebra over ℂ is a direct sum of simple ones, so classifying the simple ones is enough.

The Cartan subalgebra

Different elements give different eigenspace decompositions, so we diagonalise as many at once as possible. Operators can be diagonalised in one basis exactly when each is diagonalisable and they pairwise commute. Call x semisimple when adx is diagonalisable. A Cartan subalgebra 𝔥 is a maximal abelian subalgebra of semisimple elements. Any two Cartan subalgebras are conjugate by an automorphism of 𝔤, so their common dimension, the rank, is an invariant of 𝔤.

Four facts about this decomposition carry the classification. The zero eigenspace is 𝔥 itself. If α is a root, so is −α. Brackets add roots: [𝔤α, 𝔤β] ⊆ 𝔤α+β, which vanishes unless α + β is zero or a root. Each root space is one-dimensional. The first three follow from the Jacobi identity and joint-eigenspace bookkeeping. The fourth, which makes the classification finite, needs the sl2 subalgebras of Part 2.

One-dimensionality turns a question about algebras into one about finite sets of vectors. If 𝔤 has dimension d and rank r, the root spaces fill the other d − r dimensions one each, so |Δ| = d − r. For sln+1 that is n(n + 2) − n = n(n + 1). For E8, dimension 248 and rank 8, it is 240. Knowing Δ determines 𝔤 up to isomorphism.

On the rational span of the roots, (λ, λ) is a sum of squares of rationals. It vanishes only if λ is orthogonal to every root, and non-degeneracy then forces λ = 0. Choosing a basis of roots, the Gram matrix is rational and positive definite, so it stays positive definite on the real span.

Once the roots are vectors, each root has a reflection in the hyperplane perpendicular to it. Part 2 shows that these reflections permute the roots, proves the integrality used above, and assembles the reflections into a finite group, the Weyl group.
