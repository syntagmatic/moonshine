Pruning and the Diophantine Threshold

Part 4 of 9. Two short vector arguments rule out Dynkin diagrams with cycles or crowded nodes. A single inequality, 1/p + 1/q + 1/r > 1, then settles the branched trees, and the list of simple Lie algebras closes at E8.

Part 3 reduced every simple Lie algebra to a connected Dynkin diagram. This article finds the connected graphs with single, double and triple bonds that come from positive-definite Cartan matrices. Two constraints do nearly all the work: the diagram is a tree, and every node has total bond weight at most 3. What they leave is a short list of chains and one family of branched trees, whose arm lengths a single Diophantine inequality then settles.

Valency at most 3

Count a single bond as weight 1, a double as 2 and a triple as 3. Every node has total weight at most 3. Take a node v with neighbours u1, …, uk. In a tree the ui are pairwise non-adjacent, so pairwise orthogonal.

A node can therefore carry three single bonds but not four, a single and a double but not two doubles, and a triple bond only on its own, which makes G2 the only diagram with a triple bond. Together with the tree condition this leaves plain chains (An), chains with a double bond at the end (Bn and Cn) or in the middle (at most four nodes, F4), and trees of single bonds with one node of valency 3, whose arm lengths are still open.

A positivity test for any graph

Both arguments are cases of one test. Each node stands for a vector, and each bond fixes the angle between two of them at 120°, 135° or 150°; the question is whether such vectors can form a basis of Euclidean space. Scale every node's vector to squared length 2. The Gram matrix then has 2 on the diagonal and 2 cos θ = −√m for a bond of multiplicity m. Such vectors exist exactly when that matrix is positive definite.

Almost every graph fails. A graph that passes is of finite type, the Dynkin diagram of a simple Lie algebra. A graph whose Gram matrix is positive semidefinite with a one-dimensional kernel sits exactly on the boundary: these are the affine diagrams, the Dynkin diagrams of affine Kac–Moody algebras. Anything else is indefinite.

The T-shape

A branched tree is a "Y" with arm lengths (p, q, r), each counting the branch node, so it has p + q + r − 2 nodes. Sort them so that p ≥ q ≥ r ≥ 2 (an arm of length 1 is no arm).

The classification is complete

Chains of single bonds give An for every n ≥ 1. A chain with a double bond at one end gives Bn (n ≥ 2), and reversing its arrow gives Cn (n ≥ 3). A double bond in the middle gives only F4, since longer such chains fail the positivity test. The lone triple bond gives G2. The branched trees give Dn, E6, E7 and E8. Every other connected graph fails one of the constraints, so these nine, four families and five exceptions, are the complete list.

What lies past E8

(6, 3, 2) misses by the smallest possible margin: 1/6 + 1/3 + 1/2 = 1 exactly. The matrix is then positive semidefinite with a one-dimensional kernel, so no finite root system has this diagram. It is the diagram Ẽ8 of an affine Kac–Moody algebra, an infinite-dimensional Lie algebra built from E8, and the other two equality cases, (4, 4, 2) and (3, 3, 3), are Ẽ7 and Ẽ6.

Past the boundary, every T-shaped diagram with 1/p + 1/q + 1/r < 1 has exactly one negative eigenvalue. Deleting the branch node leaves three chains, a positive definite matrix one size smaller, and eigenvalue interlacing then allows at most one eigenvalue that is not positive. These Lorentzian diagrams define Kac–Moody algebras too, and a few of them, such as E10 = (7, 3, 2), are hyperbolic. None is finite-dimensional.

Affine extension and node-deletion surgery

Append a ninth node α0 = −αmax to E8's diagram and you get the affine E8 diagram Ẽ8. Each node carries a mark, the coefficient of its simple root in the highest root: marks (2, 3, 4, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2) on (α1, …, α8), and 1 on α0.

Deleting one node from Ẽ8 and reading off the connected pieces gives subalgebras of E8 of full rank. Borel and de Siebenthal showed that every maximal semisimple subalgebra of full rank arises this way, from deleting a node whose mark is prime.
