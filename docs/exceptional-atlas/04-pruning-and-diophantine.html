Pruning and the Diophantine Threshold

Part 4 of 9. Which graphs can be Dynkin diagrams? Two short vector arguments rule out cycles and crowded nodes. A single inequality, 1/p + 1/q + 1/r > 1, then settles the branched trees, and the list of simple Lie algebras closes at E8.

Part 3 reduced every simple Lie algebra to a connected Dynkin diagram. The question now is which connected graphs with single, double and triple bonds are Dynkin diagrams of positive-definite Cartan matrices. Two constraints do nearly all the work. An admissible diagram is a tree. And every node has total edge weight at most 3 (single=1, double=2, triple=3). Together these rule out 4-arm stars, cycles, triple edges adjacent to anything, and every dense configuration.

What survives is a shortlist: linear chains (An), chains with a double edge at the end (Bn/Cn), chains with a middle double edge (F4), the isolated triple edge (G2), and valency-3 trees whose arms need a length bound (D and E candidates). A single Diophantine inequality on the three arm lengths (p, q, r) gives four solution classes: Dn, E6, E7, E8. The next case, (6, 3, 2), hits equality and gives an affine diagram instead.

Valency at most 3

Claim: in an admissible Dynkin diagram, every node has total edge weight at most 3, where a single edge counts as weight 1, a double as 2, and a triple as 3.

Proof sketch. Take a node v with k neighbours u1, …, uk. In a tree the ui are pairwise orthogonal: any path between them must pass through v, so they are non-adjacent.

Project v onto the span of its neighbours. In the orthonormal basis u1, …, uk, the projection has squared length

The projection has squared length ≤ (v, v) = 1, with equality iff v is in the subspace. v is not in the span of its neighbours (it is linearly independent), so the inequality is strict:

Each (v, ui)² = (edge-multiplicity)/4. Summing gives (total edge-multiplicity at v)/4 < 1, so total edge weight is at most 3.

The bound is sharp. A node can have three single edges (1+1+1 = 3) but not four; one single plus one double (1+2 = 3) but not two doubles; one triple alone but nothing alongside. With tree-ness, the shortlist collapses to:

  • Linear chains of single edges → An, arbitrarily long.
  • Linear chains with one double edge at an end → Bn/Cn.
  • Linear chains with a double edge in the middle → F4-style, length at most 4.
  • Trees with one valency-3 branch, all single edges → Dn plus the E-candidates, arm lengths still open.
  • Isolated triple edge → G2. Exactly one candidate.

A positivity test for any graph

Not every graph is a Dynkin diagram. Each node stands for a vector, and each bond fixes the angle between two of them at 120°, 135° or 150°. The question is whether vectors at those angles can exist as a basis of Euclidean space. Scale every node's vector to squared length 2. The Gram matrix then has 2 on the diagonal and 2 cos θ = −√m for a bond of multiplicity m. Such vectors exist exactly when that matrix is positive definite.

Almost every graph fails the test. A graph that passes is a finite type and is the Dynkin diagram of a simple Lie algebra. A graph whose Gram matrix is positive semidefinite with a one-dimensional kernel sits exactly on the boundary: these are the affine diagrams, the Dynkin diagrams of affine Kac–Moody algebras. Anything else is indefinite.

What survives pruning is a shortlist whose only open parameter is the arm lengths of a branched tree.

The T-shape

A valency-3 branched tree is a "Y" with arm lengths (p, q, r), each counting nodes including the branch node. Sort p ≥ q ≥ r ≥ 2 (an arm of length 1 means no actual arm).

The total node count is p + q + r − 2.

The only solutions are the infinite Dn family (q = r = 2, any p ≥ 2) and three singleton exceptions (5, 3, 2), (4, 3, 2), (3, 3, 2) corresponding to E8, E7, E6. The (4, 4, 2) and (3, 3, 3) configurations are boundary cases where the inequality becomes equality; those correspond to affine Kac-Moody algebras rather than finite-dimensional simple Lie algebras.

So the complete solution set is:

  • Dn for n ≥ 4: the (p, 2, 2) family with p = n − 2 ≥ 2. A branch with two length-2 arms and one variable-length arm. The arm count p plus 2 gives the rank of the D algebra, so D4 has arm triple (2, 2, 2), D5 has (3, 2, 2), and so on.
  • E6: arms (3, 3, 2). A rank-6 algebra with a perfectly symmetric (3, 3, 2) branch.
  • E7: arms (4, 3, 2). Rank 7.
  • E8: arms (5, 3, 2). Rank 8, dimension 248, the largest of the five exceptional algebras. (An and Dn grow without bound.)

That is all the branched-tree Dynkin diagrams.

The classification is complete

Putting the pieces together:

  • Linear chains of single edges → An, one per n ≥ 1.
  • Chain with one double edge at the end → Bn, one per n ≥ 2. Reversing the arrow gives Cn, one per n ≥ 3.
  • Chain with a double edge in the middle, length at most 4 → F4 (and only F4, longer chains fail the valency-type bound on the central region).
  • Isolated triple edge → G2, the only rank-2 exceptional.
  • Branched trees with a single valency-3 node and simply-laced edges → Dn from (p, 2, 2); E6 from (3, 3, 2); E7 from (4, 3, 2); E8 from (5, 3, 2).

The nine families A, B, C, D, E6, E7, E8, F4, G2 are the complete list. Every other connected Dynkin-diagram shape fails one of the constraints.

What lies past E8

(6, 3, 2) misses by the smallest possible margin: 1/6 + 1/3 + 1/2 = 1 exactly. The matrix is then positive semidefinite with a one-dimensional kernel, so no finite root system has this diagram. It is the diagram Ẽ8 of an affine Kac–Moody algebra, an infinite-dimensional Lie algebra built from E8, and the other two equality cases, (4, 4, 2) and (3, 3, 3), are Ẽ7 and Ẽ6.

Past the boundary, every T-shaped diagram with 1/p + 1/q + 1/r < 1 has exactly one negative eigenvalue. Deleting the branch node leaves three chains, a positive definite matrix one size smaller, and eigenvalue interlacing then allows at most one eigenvalue that is not positive. These Lorentzian diagrams define Kac–Moody algebras too, and a few of them, such as E10 = (7, 3, 2), are hyperbolic. None is finite-dimensional. The finite list ends because the arithmetic of 1/p + 1/q + 1/r ends.

Affine extension and node-deletion surgery

Append a ninth node α0 = −αmax to E8's diagram and you get the affine E8 diagram Ẽ8. Each node carries a mark, the coefficient of its simple root in the highest root: marks (2, 3, 4, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2) on (α1, …, α8), and 1 on α0.

The affine diagram also produces subalgebras of E8 of full rank: delete one node from Ẽ8 and read off the connected pieces. Borel and de Siebenthal showed that every maximal semisimple subalgebra of full rank arises this way, from deleting a node whose mark is prime.
