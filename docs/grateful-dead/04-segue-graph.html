The Segue Graph

Which songs flowed straight into each other, and which merely shared a night, across thirty years of setlists.

A Dead segue is a conversation between songs. When "China Cat Sunflower" dissolves into "I Know You Rider" there is no pause or count-off; one song's final bars become the other's opening phrase. The band navigates by ear and by thirty years of shared vocabulary.

Some connections hardened into ritual. "China Cat" into "Rider" happened 544 times. "Scarlet Begonias" into "Fire on the Mountain" (238) became so automatic that fans shortened it to "Scarlet > Fire." Other songs had many exits. "The Other One" and "Truckin'" each lead to five different songs among the hundred most common segues, and "Playing in the Band" to four. The number of destinations a song could reach is one measure of its improvisational gravity.

The data here is the hundred most frequent directed segues, A > B, counted over the 2,076 of 2,358 shows whose setlists survive in gdshowsdb. Anything rarer than 30 occurrences falls below the cut, so "never" in what follows means "fewer than 30 times."

The Network

Figure 1. Force-directed segue network of the top 100 segue pairs. Edge width proportional to segue count. Node size proportional to total segues (in + out). Color encodes hub score: warm = many distinct partners, cool = few. Click a node to highlight its neighborhood.

Drums and Space sit at the centre as hubs. Drums touches 21 of the hundred pairs and Space 11, because after 1978 the Drums > Space passage sat in the middle of nearly every second set, and almost anything could flow into it or out of it. Strip those two away and the direct musical connections remain: "China Cat" locked to "Rider," "Scarlet" locked to "Fire on the Mountain," "The Other One" and "Playing in the Band" reaching in several directions.

The Random Walk

Treat the segue data as a Markov chain. Each song is a state. The transition probability from song A to song B is proportional to how often A > B actually happened. A random walker follows those probabilities, more likely to take well-worn paths, tracing out a synthetic chain of segues.

One wrinkle: 23 of the 71 songs in the network have no outgoing edge in the top 100. "Sugar Magnolia," "Fire on the Mountain" and "I Know You Rider" are places a segue ends. When the walker lands on one, it jumps to a random song that does have an exit, the way a new segue chain starts later in the show.

Figure 2. Markov random walk on the segue network. Click a song node to choose a starting point. "Walk 100 Steps" runs the chain; bars show the share of visits so far, and the dark tick on each bar is the chain's exact long-run share, computed by power iteration.

A hundred steps is a noisy sample. Keep walking and the bars settle onto the ticks. In the long run the walker spends 13.5% of its time in Drums and 10.3% in Space. Next come "Not Fade Away" (6.0%), "The Other One" (5.2%), "Good Lovin'" (4.3%) and "Goin' Down the Road Feelin' Bad" (3.9%). "Playing in the Band," for all its exits, ranks 23rd at 1.1%, because few segues in the top 100 lead into it. The walk rewards songs that other songs flow into, which is a different thing from being played often.

The Pairs

"Drums > Space" at 1,197 is the runaway leader, and it is scaffolding more than a musical choice. Below it, "China Cat Sunflower > I Know You Rider" at 544 is the pairing so fixed that hearing one without the other felt wrong. "Jam > Drums" and "Scarlet Begonias > Fire on the Mountain" tie at 238.

Figure 3. Top 20 segue pairs by count, over the 2,076 shows with a recorded setlist. Bar length proportional to number of times the segue occurred.

Direction matters. Only 8 of the pairs appear in both directions in the top 100. "Not Fade Away" flows into "Goin' Down the Road Feelin' Bad" 123 times and back 98 times; both have a driving beat and a sing-along chorus, so either can launch or land. "Estimated Prophet" pours into "Eyes of the World" 177 times, and "He's Gone" into "Truckin'" 50 times, while neither reverse makes the list.

The pairings also have ages. China Cat and Rider could only have been joined from 1969 on: "China Cat Sunflower" first appears in the setlists in 1968, and "I Know You Rider," played a few times in 1966 and 1967, has no performances in 1968. From 1971 to 1995 the two have nearly identical play counts year by year. This dataset has no per-year segue counts, so the other pairs' histories are left out.

Same Night, Not Back to Back

A segue is adjacency. A weaker relationship is simply appearing in the same show, possibly sets apart. The shipped data includes the 200 most frequent same-show pairs among the 40 most-played songs. Raw counts say little on their own. "Drums" and "Me And My Uncle" share 464 shows, but both were played so often that they would collide constantly by chance.

So compare each pair with what chance predicts. If song A appears at a fraction pA of shows and song B at pB, and the two are chosen independently, they should share about pA × pB × N of N shows. The catch is what "independently" means. Computed from career totals, the model ignores that songs have lifespans: two songs that both flourished in 1971 to 1974 will share far more shows than their career totals suggest, just because they were alive at the same time. The fix is to apply the same formula year by year, using each song's share of that year's shows, and add up the years.

With career totals as the baseline, China Cat and Rider come out 3.5 times more often together than chance, and Estimated Prophet and Eyes of the World 2.9 times. Several first-set pairs built around "El Paso" also look strongly linked, at more than twice chance. Switch to same-year rates and the El Paso pairs fall to about 1.05: "El Paso" was played 35 to 70 times a year in 1971 to 1973 and rarely between 1983 and 1993, so it naturally shared shows with the other songs of that period. Their apparent bond was a shared era.

What survives the stricter baseline is short and nearly all red. The top five pairs are China Cat and Rider (2.4×), Estimated Prophet and Eyes (2.2×), Me And My Uncle and Big River (1.7×), The Other One and Wharf Rat (1.6×), and Playing in the Band and Uncle John's Band (1.3×), and every one of them is also a top-100 segue. In this data, when two songs are reliably booked for the same night beyond what their era explains, it is because one leads into the other.

The bottom of the list shows the opposite pull. The most under-represented pair under the year model is "Not Fade Away" with "Sugar Magnolia," at 0.62 of chance. Those are the two most common second-set closers in the setlists (Sugar Magnolia closed 359 second sets, Not Fade Away 247), so one of them usually took the slot the other wanted. "Playing in the Band" appears in five of the bottom ten pairs, but read those with caution: its play count includes reprises within the same show, which inflates its expected co-occurrences. And because the data holds only the 200 most frequent pairs, it cannot show avoidance among less-played songs, whose pairs fall below the cutoff.
