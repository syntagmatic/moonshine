Explanation Templates

Most interactive explanations reinvent the same handful of structural patterns. What if we named them, built scaffolding for each, and let authors focus on content?

Open ten interactive explanations at random. Strip away the datasets, palettes, and jargon. The skeleton has the same four or five structures, repeated with minor variations.

One has a slider that updates a chart. Another puts two visualizations side by side and highlights their differences. A third builds complexity in layers, adding one element at a time. A fourth is a chart with prose callouts pointing at notable features. A fifth lets the reader drag variables onto encoding channels.

These reflect the small number of questions explanations answer. "What happens when I change this?" leads to a parameter explorer. "How do these differ?" leads to compare-and-contrast. "What is this made of?" leads to progressive disclosure. "What should I notice?" leads to annotation-driven narrative. "What can I discover on my own?" leads to build-your-own exploration.

The patterns cluster around a handful of archetypes, each corresponding to a distinct reader question. A template library would name these archetypes, scaffold each, and let authors spend time on content rather than rebuilding interaction plumbing.

This is a direction for d3-power-tools: a library of explanation templates that handle structural boilerplate while staying out of the way of content. Not components (those are visual primitives like axes and legends). Templates are structural: they define where controls go, how state flows, and what the interaction loop looks like. The author fills in the domain-specific parts.

Archetype 1: Parameter Explorer

"What happens when I change X?" is the most common question in interactive explanation. The parameter explorer answers with immediate, continuous feedback: drag a slider, see the chart update. A control region sits adjacent to a visualization. Every control maps to a parameter. Every parameter change triggers a re-render.

The archetype appears everywhere: learning rate schedules in ML, filter cutoff frequencies in signal processing, tax brackets in policy, physics simulations. Whenever the point is "the relationship between this input and that output is nonlinear and surprising," a parameter explorer is right.

The template handles: laying out controls alongside the chart, binding each control to a state variable, debouncing rapid input, and providing a reset button. The author provides: the chart rendering function, the parameter definitions (name, range, default), and optionally a description of what each parameter does.

Archetype 2: Compare and Contrast

"How do A and B differ?" demands juxtaposition. Compare-and-contrast places two or more conditions side by side and surfaces differences, whether through literal side-by-side charts, a toggle, or an overlay.

The demo shows two groups of bars. In "both" mode they appear side by side. In "differences" mode, the bars that differ most are highlighted. The structure surfaces differences instead of asking the reader to scan for them.

Algorithm comparisons (quicksort vs. mergesort), before/after interventions, treatment vs. control in clinical trials, and historical changes over time all use this archetype. The template handles: synchronized scales, alignment of corresponding elements, difference highlighting, and the toggle between modes. The author provides: the two (or N) datasets or conditions, and the comparison metric.

Archetype 3: Progressive Disclosure

"Build up complexity layer by layer" is the pedagogical workhorse. Start with something simple (just the axes, or just the raw points) and add complexity one piece at a time. Each layer makes sense because the reader already understands everything beneath it.

Fourier decomposition is the canonical example: start with the fundamental, add the first harmonic, then the second, watch the approximation converge. Neural networks use this too: single neuron, then a layer, then activation functions, then backpropagation. Maps layer geographic features: terrain, roads, demographics, then the analytical overlay.

The template handles: the layer stack and ordering, enter/exit animations for each layer, the buttons or scroll triggers that advance the stack, and the ability to go backward (removing layers is as important as adding them). The author provides: the layer definitions and their rendering functions.

Archetype 4: Annotation-Driven Narrative

"Here is what to notice" is the structure of data journalism. The chart is a canvas; the prose points at features: this outlier, that trend, the gap between clusters. The annotation is the argument; the chart is the evidence.

This archetype maps best to traditional writing. The interactive element lets the reader see which data supports each claim. Hover over a sentence, the relevant data points highlight. Click a callout, the chart zooms. The interaction is guided, not exploratory.

The template handles: positioning callout annotations relative to data coordinates, managing focus (which annotation is active), coordinating prose and chart highlighting, and responsive repositioning when the chart resizes. The author provides: the annotations (text, target coordinates, optional styling) and the chart itself.

Archetype 5: Build Your Own

"Assemble an explanation from pieces" is the most open-ended archetype. Rather than the author prescribing the view, the reader constructs it. Assign a variable to x, another to y, choose a color encoding, toggle chart types. This is exploratory data analysis dressed up as an explanation.

The risk is the reader gets lost. Without guidance, a build-your-own tool is just a chart builder. The archetype works with guardrails: curated variables, sensible defaults, suggested views the author pre-selected.

The template handles: the encoding assignment UI (dropdowns, drag targets), re-rendering on encoding changes, scale inference from data types, and suggested-view presets. The author provides: the dataset, the variable metadata (name, type, description), and the preset configurations.

Story discovery questions map to archetypes

Moonshine's story discovery process begins with questions. What story does this data tell? What should the reader take away? What is surprising? These questions are not random. They cluster, and the clusters align with the five archetypes.

"What happens when..." is a causal or parametric question. It leads to a parameter explorer. The story is about a relationship between input and output, and the reader needs to feel that relationship through direct manipulation.

"How do these differ..." is a comparative question. It leads to compare-and-contrast. The story is about the gap between two conditions, and the reader needs juxtaposition to see it.

"What is this made of..." is a compositional question. It leads to progressive disclosure. The story is about how simple parts combine into complex wholes, and the reader needs to see the buildup.

"What should I notice..." is an editorial question. It leads to annotation-driven narrative. The story is about specific features in the data, and the reader needs the author to point at them.

"What can I find..." is an exploratory question. It leads to build-your-own. The story is that there is no single story; the data has multiple facets, and the reader should discover the one that matters to them.

If the story discovery question type determines the archetype, then an LLM that understands the question can select the right template before generating any code. This collapses a large design decision (what structure should this explanation have?) into a classification problem (what type of question is this explanation answering?).

Templates are not components

The word "template" gets overloaded. A component is a visual primitive: an axis, a legend, a tooltip, a brush. Components are the bricks. A template is a floor plan: it says where the bricks go and what the interaction flow looks like. Swap the bricks without changing the template, or reuse the same bricks across templates.

A parameter explorer template does not care whether the chart is a line, scatter, or heatmap. It cares that controls map to state variables and state changes trigger re-renders. A compare-and-contrast template does not care whether the comparison is bars, maps, or network diagrams. It cares that the renderings share scales and the toggle is smooth.

Templates compose with the existing d3-power-tools components. Use axis, legend, and tooltip components inside any template. The template provides scaffolding; components provide visual elements.

The reusability-specificity tension

Every template bets the archetype is general enough to cover many explanations but specific enough to scaffold. Too general, and it collapses into a blank page with event wiring. Too specific, and it fits only the original use case.

The parameter explorer is the safest bet: controls on one side, chart on the other, state in between. The annotation-driven narrative is the riskiest because annotations are tied to content: placement, wording, and sequencing all depend on the specific story. A template that handles mechanics without constraining editorial choices is hard to get right.

Build-your-own is risky for a different reason. It needs the most metadata from the author: variable types, defaults, encoding constraints. Demand too much upfront configuration, and the author might as well write the tool from scratch.

Progressive disclosure falls in the middle. The layer-stack structure is common, but transitions vary. Fourier decomposition adds curves; a network diagram adds nodes; a map adds geographic layers. The template handles stack management and controls; layer-specific transitions are the author's problem.

Amber risk: template lock-in and the uncanny valley

  • Template lock-in. Once authors start using templates, switching away is costly. The template's assumptions about state management, layout, and interaction flow permeate the author's code. If the template does not quite fit, the author faces a choice: contort the content to fit the template, or abandon the template and rewrite from scratch. Neither option is good. Templates need clean ejection paths: a way to "graduate" from the template to raw code without starting over.
  • The uncanny valley of almost-fitting templates. An explanation that is 90% parameter-explorer but needs one unusual interaction (say, a two-dimensional brush instead of a slider) will fight the template at every turn. The last 10% of customization consumes 90% of the effort, and the result feels stitched together. Worse, the reader can sense the template underneath, the way you can sense a WordPress theme behind a corporate website. The explanation feels generic even if the content is novel.
  • LLM defaulting to templates. If d3-power-tools provides templates, LLMs will reach for them reflexively. This is efficient when the template fits, but dangerous when it does not. A skilled author would recognize that a particular explanation needs a custom structure and build one. An LLM, trained to minimize effort, will jam the content into the nearest template. The result is cookie-cutter explanations that answer the letter of the question but miss the spirit. Worse, the evaluator may not catch this because the template produces structurally valid output. The explanation looks right but does not communicate well.
  • Creativity constraints. The best interactive explanations often break the mold. Bret Victor's explorable explanations, Nicky Case's interactive essays, the Pudding's visual stories: these succeed precisely because their structure is tailored to their content. A template library, by definition, encourages the common case. It may subtly discourage the inventive structures that make the best explanations memorable.

What templates would look like in practice

A template is a function, not a file. You call it with a configuration object describing your content, and it returns a structured explanation with all the interaction wiring in place. Something like:

const explorer = parameterExplorer({
  container: "#my-viz",
  data: myDataset,
  params: [
    { name: "smoothing", type: "range", min: 0, max: 1, default: 0.3 },
    { name: "showOutliers", type: "toggle", default: false }
  ],
  render(svg, data, params) {
    // Your D3 code here. Called on every param change.
  }
});

Listing 1. A parameter explorer template call. The template handles layout, control rendering, state binding, and debouncing. The author provides the data, the parameter definitions, and a render function.

The render function is the escape hatch: raw D3. The template manages the structure around the chart, not the chart itself. Any chart type works, and you never rewrite slider-event-to-state boilerplate.

For the LLM workflow, template selection could happen automatically. The proposer classifies the story question into an archetype and generates code using the corresponding template. The evaluator checks fit. If the content is really progressive disclosure but the proposer used a parameter explorer, the evaluator flags it.

This is the real leverage of templates for moonshine: not the typing they save, but the vocabulary they give the evaluate-iterate loop. Instead of "the interaction model is confusing," the evaluator can say "this is a compare-and-contrast story structured as a parameter explorer; restructure." That is actionable.

Five structures, infinite content

Not all explanations fit into five boxes, but most have a structural skeleton that resembles one of five archetypes, and that skeleton is worth standardizing separately from the content. The bones are generic; the flesh is specific.

The strongest argument for templates is pedagogical. When a new author asks "how do I structure an interactive explanation?", the answer today is "look at examples." With a template library, the answer is "what question is your explanation answering?" The question determines the structure.

The strongest argument against templates is aesthetic. The memorable explanations have structure so tailored to content that you cannot imagine it any other way. Templates are ready-made, and ready-made structures fit most adequately and nobody perfectly.

Use templates for the 80% where the archetype fits cleanly. Build custom for the 20% where it does not. Templates handle the boring part (layout, state, controls) so attention goes to the story. When the template does not fit, it should be easy to set aside.
