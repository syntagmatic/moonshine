Percolation

Below a critical density of trees a fire stays small, and above it one spark can cross the whole forest. Epidemics on a lattice show the same kind of threshold.

Plant a forest at random and light one tree. Sparse forests stop the fire; dense ones burn from one spark, and the change is sharp, at the percolation threshold. Below, we locate it, measure the clusters there, ask what depends on the lattice, then run an epidemic on the same grid and let the forest regrow.

The Forest

A burning tree gets one chance to light each neighboring tree, with the ignition probability, then turns to ash. Wind raises the chance downwind and lowers it upwind: lit from the left edge, the windy damp preset burned about three quarters of the trees in our runs, the calm one about 3%.

Clusters at the threshold

Figure 4 counts clusters by size in doubling bins, leaving out a spanning one. Near the threshold they follow a power law; at 45% they bend down as large clusters become exponentially rare. The infinite-lattice slope is −187/91 ≈ −2.05 (den Nijs, 1979; Nienhuis, 1982); this forest at 59% gives about −1.8 (100 forests, −1.69 to −1.94), the edge cutting the largest clusters short.

The spanning cluster is a fractal. A solid patch fills a box as its side squared; the critical cluster, holed at every scale, as the side to the power 91/48, which follows from Figure 4's exponent (). Above the threshold the holes close beyond the correlation length and the power climbs to 2; below, the cluster ends and it falls.

What depends on the lattice

The threshold belongs to the lattice, and to whether sites or links are random; the exponents do not. Figure 6 runs sites on the square grid, links on it (each open with probability , the case the epidemic below needs), and sites on the triangular lattice. Their thresholds are 0.5927 (Newman and Ziff measure 0.59274621) and exactly 1/2 for both others (Sykes and Essam, 1964; Kesten, 1980). At each threshold the largest cluster grows with slope 1.85, 1.88 and 1.86 against 91/48 ≈ 1.90.

Epidemics on the same lattice

Put a person on every cell and infect the one in the middle. An infected person has one step to infect each susceptible neighbor, with probability , then recovers for good: SIR on a lattice. Every pair of neighbors gets exactly one chance, so the coins could be flipped in advance: open each link with probability , and the infected are those joined to the first case by open links. The final size is one bond-percolation cluster, as Grassberger (1983) first pointed out.

So the epidemic threshold is Figure 6's bond threshold, . Ignoring geometry, each new case has at most three susceptible neighbors and would suffice; the lattice needs more because an infection keeps meeting people its earlier cases already reached.

Regrowing forests

So far someone set the density. In the Drossel-Schwabl model the forest regrows: each step empty cells sprout a tree with probability , lightning hits each tree with probability , and a struck tree burns its whole cluster. With patches thicken past the threshold between strikes, yet the average density settles near 0.39 here, well below 0.593, a patchwork of patches far below and far above the threshold (Grassberger, 2002). Patches of every size give fires of every size with nothing tuned, hence the name self-organized critical, though whether it is truly critical is still argued.

In plain percolation, large fires need a density set near ; here slow growth and fast burning make fires of every size with nobody setting it. The next essay strips that slow drive and cascading release down to a sandpile.
