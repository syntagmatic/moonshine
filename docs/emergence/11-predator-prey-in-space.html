Predator-Prey in Space

The textbook predator-prey cycle is fragile. Spread over a landscape, the populations still boom and bust locally, but the totals stay nearly steady.

In the previous essay the agents wrote a field that spread across space. Here the animals themselves spread, and the question is what that does to a predator-prey cycle. The Lotka-Volterra equations treat foxes and rabbits as two numbers in a well-stirred pot, and they cycle: rabbits boom, foxes follow, rabbits crash, foxes starve. The cycle is fragile. In the models below, spatial structure steadies the totals and lets a patch that crashes be recolonised from its neighbours, though on the lattice it also makes predators harder to sustain.

The sections below cover three models: the well-mixed equations made slightly more realistic; the same equations on a landscape, where each patch cycles out of step with its neighbours; and individual animals on a lattice, where randomness and crowding produce a sharp extinction threshold.

1. The well-mixed cycle

Write \(x\) for prey density and \(y\) for predators. Prey grow at rate \(a\), predators eat them at rate \(b\), convert a fraction \(e\) of what they eat into new predators, and die at rate \(m\):

\[ \dot x = a\,x - b\,x\,y, \qquad \dot y = e\,b\,x\,y - m\,y . \]

These equations conserve a quantity, \(V = e b\,x - m\ln x + b\,y - a\ln y\), so every trajectory is a closed loop at fixed \(V\). The orbits in Figure 1 never settle and never blow up, and the cycle has no preferred size: nudge the system and it moves to a neighbouring loop for good. This neutral cycle is structurally unstable. Almost any change to the equations destroys it.

Give prey a carrying capacity \(K\) (they run out of grass) and the loops become inward spirals that settle to a fixed point. Then let predators become satiated, so each can only eat so fast. The Holling type II response \(bx/(1 + b h x)\) with handling time \(h\) turns the model into the Rosenzweig-MacArthur equations, and now \(K\) determines the outcome. Below a threshold the fixed point is stable. Above it, every start converges to a single limit cycle, and the richer the environment the deeper its troughs. This is Rosenzweig's paradox of enrichment (1971): feeding the prey destabilises the system.

Push \(K\) to 3 and the prey minimum on the cycle falls below 0.0001, against a peak near 2.8. In a real population that is zero, so the model that fixes the neutral cycle predicts extinction unless something holds the troughs up. Huffaker's (1958) mites on arrays of oranges behaved this way. On a small, open array the predators ate every prey mite and then starved. The two species went through three oscillations together only when he enlarged the array and broke it up with barriers that slowed the predators and routes that helped the prey disperse, so different patches sat at different points of the cycle.

2. Adding space

Put the Rosenzweig-MacArthur model on a grid. Each cell holds a prey density \(H\) and a predator density \(P\), runs the local equations, and exchanges animals with its four neighbours by diffusion:

\[ \partial_t H = a H\Bigl(1 - \frac{H}{K}\Bigr) - \frac{bHP}{1 + b h H} + D_H \nabla^2 H, \qquad \partial_t P = \frac{e\,bHP}{1 + b h H} - m P + D_P \nabla^2 P . \]

Below the enrichment threshold, the grid behaves like the well-mixed model. The fixed point is stable in each cell, diffusion smooths out differences, and any initial pattern fades to a flat field. (Nor can this model make Turing spots: at the fixed point the predators' growth rate does not depend on their own density and the prey are self-limiting, so there is no self-enhancing activator for diffusion to amplify.)

Above the threshold every cell oscillates, and diffusion couples neighbouring cells without synchronising them. From random noise the field organises into waves of prey chased by waves of predators. A wavefront with a free end curls into a spiral, as in the excitable media of the Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction. Released from a single point, predators spread as rings of travelling waves, and well behind the front the rings break up into irregular patches, which Sherratt, Lewis and Fowler (1995) called chaos in the wake of invasion.

In the strip, a single cell still runs through most of the well-mixed boom and bust; at \(K = 2\) its prey usually drop to within a few thousandths of zero, much as in Figure 1 (a cell near the core of a spiral swings less). The total barely moves, because at any moment some patches are in a prey boom and others in a predator boom or recovering. Each cell keeps its cycle, but the phases are spread across the landscape, so the grid mean stays between about 0.3 and 0.4 while each cell swings from near zero to above 1.

The spread of phases is also why prey persist. A patch that predators have emptied is surrounded by patches in other phases, and prey diffuse back in. In the well-mixed model the trough happens everywhere at once and nothing is left to recolonise from.

3. Individuals on a lattice

Densities are still an idealisation: a cell that has run down to a thousandth of a rabbit has no rabbits. The simplest individual-based version is the stochastic lattice Lotka-Volterra model. Each site of a square grid is empty, holds one prey, or holds one predator. Pick a site at random. A prey picks a random neighbour and, if it is empty, fills it with offspring with probability \(\sigma\). A predator dies with probability \(\mu\); if it survives it picks a random neighbour and, if that holds a prey, eats it and puts an offspring there with probability \(\lambda\). One sweep of the grid is one unit of time.

These look like the Lotka-Volterra rules, but prey can only reproduce into an empty site, so the grid itself is a carrying capacity. The mean-field version has the factor \((1 - x - y)\) in the prey growth term and, like the "+ carrying capacity" model of Figure 1, a stable fixed point with no cycle. The lattice, however, does not settle. Prey form clusters, predators eat into them from the edges, and local populations rise and fall irregularly. On a finite lattice these fluctuations persist, but they stay local: the amplitude of the population oscillations tends to zero as the lattice grows (Mobilia, Georgiev and Täuber 2007).

The chart plots stationary densities against predation rate. Each dot is a separate 64×64 lattice, run when the figure scrolls into view for 1500 sweeps and averaged over the last 300. Dashed curves are the mean-field fixed point of the same rules. The hollow ring is the live lattice, averaged over its last 100 sweeps. Near the threshold the lattice relaxes slowly, so those points are noisy.

Below a critical predation rate \(\lambda_c\), predators die out and prey fill the grid. The predator-free state is absorbing: once the last predator dies, nothing brings one back. Above \(\lambda_c\) both species persist. Mean field puts the threshold at \(\lambda(1-\mu) = \mu\), where a lone predator in a sea of prey just replaces itself, which here is \(\lambda_c = 0.111\). The lattice needs nearly twice that. On a 200×200 lattice run for 4000 sweeps, predators died out at \(\lambda = 0.195\) and persisted at 0.205, so \(\lambda_c \approx 0.20\) for these rules. Part of the reason is that a predator's offspring land next to it and compete for the same local prey; one in the middle of a cluster of its kin has nothing to eat. Near \(\lambda_c\) the transition is in the directed-percolation universality class, the generic class for a single absorbing state (Antal and Droz 2001; Mobilia, Georgiev and Täuber 2007). A sweep this small locates the threshold but cannot measure its exponents.

The same contest between local instability and spatial averaging appears with three species that chase each other rock-paper-scissors fashion. Well mixed, a finite population drifts until two of the three are gone. On a lattice all three persist as rotating spirals, but only while individuals move slowly enough that a spiral fits inside the habitat (Reichenbach, Mobilia and Frey 2007).

The predator-prey landscape makes waves and patches but, as Section 2 noted, no Turing spots, because it has no self-enhancing activator. The next essay starts from a reaction that has one, and compares it with the other explanation biology offers for how an embryo becomes patterned.
