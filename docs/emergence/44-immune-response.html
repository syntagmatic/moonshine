Immune Response

Each T-cell recognizes one specific shape. The immune system works because millions of different T-cells patrol simultaneously.

Your body holds roughly 10 billion T-cells, each carrying a randomly generated receptor that binds one specific antigen. Most T-cells never meet their match; the few that do trigger an explosive response.

The immune system solves a search problem by brute-force diversity rather than prediction — an enormous library of random detectors running in parallel.

Patrol and Recognition

A T-cell that contacts its matching pathogen activates and starts cloning. One lineage expands while the rest remain dormant.

Clonal Expansion Curve

The primary response has three phases: a lag while the matching T-cell hunts, exponential growth, and contraction where most clones die but a small memory population persists.

Reinfection and Immune Memory

On reinfection, memory cells skip the lag phase and expand faster than naive T-cells did the first time. The secondary response often clears the pathogen before symptoms develop — the basis of vaccination.
