Excitable Media

A single set of equations explains spiral waves in a beaker of chemicals, in cardiac tissue, and in half a dozen other places where rest, excitation, and recovery chase each other around a plane.

An excitable medium is any system whose parts obey three rules. Sit quietly at rest until a stimulus pushes past a threshold. Fire. Refuse to fire again until recovery completes. Three states, cycling in strict order: resting, excited, refractory, resting. Couple these cells in space so that firing triggers neighbors, and you get traveling pulses that annihilate on collision, along with a single persistent pathology: the rotating spiral.

This framework first appeared in chemistry. The Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction is a soup of cerium ions, malonic acid, and bromate in sulfuric acid that oscillates spontaneously and, spread into a thin film, organizes into turning spiral fronts. The same dynamics appear in the heart, where spirals cause ventricular tachycardia and, if they shatter, fibrillation.

1. The Excitable Cell

The simplest model that captures excitability is the FitzHugh-Nagumo equations. Two variables: an activator \(u\) that drives the cell toward firing, and an inhibitor \(v\) that slowly accumulates and shuts it down. In BZ chemistry, \(u\) is the concentration of a fast autocatalytic species and \(v\) is a slow bromide intermediate. In heart muscle, \(u\) is the transmembrane voltage and \(v\) is a lumped recovery variable tracking ion-channel gates. Different molecules, same bookkeeping.

Plot \(u\) against \(v\) and the system traces a closed loop called a limit cycle. Each lap around the cycle is one oscillation or one action potential. The shape of the cycle depends on how excitable the medium is and how fast the inhibitor recovers. The dashed curves are nullclines, the loci where \(du/dt = 0\) and \(dv/dt = 0\). Their intersection is the fixed point; its stability determines whether the cell ticks on its own or sits at rest waiting for a push.

2. The Chemistry Example: Waves in a BZ Dish

Most chemical reactions run in one direction: reactants become products, and that is it. The Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction is different. Mix cerium ions, malonic acid, and bromate in sulfuric acid and the solution starts to oscillate. It turns yellow, then clear, then yellow again, ticking back and forth like a chemical clock. No external pacemaker. The oscillation is built into the kinetics, and the two-variable FHN model of Section 1 is the qualitative reduction of the full Oregonator scheme.

Now spread that oscillating chemistry across a thin film. Each point in the dish is its own little FHN oscillator, and neighboring points are coupled by diffusion of the activator (the inhibitor barely diffuses in BZ). When one point fires, it triggers its neighbors, which trigger their neighbors, and a wave rolls outward like a ripple on a pond.

But these are not ordinary waves. When two ripples meet head-on, they do not pass through each other. Each wavefront leaves behind a trail of refractory medium that cannot fire again until the inhibitor decays. So when two waves collide, both fronts hit refractory tissue and die. This is wave annihilation, and it is the defining difference between excitable waves and the linear waves you see in water.

3. The Biology Example: Cardiac Tissue

Heart muscle is built out of the same excitable machinery, but the molecules are different. Each myocyte sits at a negative resting voltage. A stimulus pushes the membrane past threshold, sodium channels open, and voltage spikes: the action potential. Calcium enters, the cell contracts, potassium channels open, voltage falls back down, and the cell is briefly refractory while ion pumps reset the gradients. Resting, upstroke, plateau, recovery, rest.

Line up a row of such cells and poke one end. The excited cell triggers its neighbor, which triggers the next, and a pulse rolls down the row. But the pulse cannot reverse, because behind it is a trail of refractory cells that refuse to fire. Poke both ends simultaneously and the two pulses meet in the middle and annihilate, the same mechanism as BZ collision, now in 1D.

Figure 3. A 1D cable of cardiac cells. "Poke both ends" sends two pulses toward each other. They collide and die, for exactly the reason BZ waves die when they collide.

Spread the cells across a 2D sheet and you have a simple model of the atrial or ventricular wall. A small pacemaker region in the top-left fires at a steady rate, and every cell fires once per heartbeat, producing a coordinated contraction.

The pacing interval controls how often the pacemaker fires. Pace faster than the refractory period and the tissue cannot keep up: some pulses fail to propagate, and the effective heart rate drops below the pacemaker's nominal rate. This is the simplest version of rate-dependent conduction block.

4. Spirals: the Shared Pathology

Circular waves expand and die at the boundaries. Nothing persists. But break a wavefront, and something remarkable happens. The broken end has nowhere to go except to curl back on itself. It pivots around the break point, tracing a tighter and tighter arc until it forms a rotating spiral. Once formed, the spiral is self-sustaining: it emits wave after wave from its tip, each one spiraling outward, forever.

Multiple spirals can coexist. Each one rotates with a definite chirality: clockwise or counterclockwise. Neighboring spirals compete for territory. Their waves collide and annihilate along the boundaries, tiling the medium into domains. In the heart this is a catastrophe: a single spiral rotating on the ventricular wall fires the tissue faster than the sinus node, overriding the natural pacemaker. One spiral is ventricular tachycardia. If the spiral breaks into multiple fragments, the tissue devolves into fibrillation.

The "S1-S2 protocol" is how cardiologists create reentry in a lab preparation, and how it tends to happen clinically. S1 is a normal planar wave. Before the trailing tissue has fully recovered, we drop a premature stimulus (S2) onto the recovering tail. The premature beat can only spread sideways into recovered tissue, not back into refractory tissue, and a spiral is born.

BZ spirals were first reported in the 1970s by Zhabotinsky and Winfree. Winfree went on to show that the same geometry governs cardiac arrhythmias; his book When Time Breaks Down treats the heart as an excitable medium and re-entry as a topological inevitability. A defibrillator does not restart the heart. It simultaneously depolarizes every cell, wiping out the phase gradients that spirals need to rotate, and lets the sinus node resume control of an electrically blank slate.

The mathematics here is the same as in slime-mold aggregation, in calcium waves on egg surfaces, in cortical spreading depression during migraines, and in forest-fire fronts. The heart just happens to be the case where the spirals can kill you.
