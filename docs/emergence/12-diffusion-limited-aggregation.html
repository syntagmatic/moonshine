Diffusion-Limited Aggregation

Release a random walker and let it stick where it lands to grow fractal trees that resemble lightning and coral.

Start with a single particle. Release another particle far away and let it wander randomly. When the walker bumps into the first particle, it sticks. Release another. And another. The structure that grows is not round or smooth. It is a fractal tree with branches that keep branching.

This is diffusion-limited aggregation, or DLA. The growth is diffusion-limited because the random walk of incoming particles is the bottleneck. Particles arrive slowly and stick fast. That asymmetry is what makes fractals.

2. The Screening Effect

Why branches? Why not a round blob? The answer is screening. A random walker approaching the cluster is far more likely to hit an outer tip than to penetrate deep into a crevice. Tips intercept walkers before they can reach the interior. This means tips grow faster. Small bumps become big branches. Big branches develop their own bumps. The process is self-reinforcing.

Figure 2. Click "Show Heatmap" to overlay arrival probability on the cluster boundary. Bright = high probability (exposed tips). Dark = low probability (screened crevices). This gradient is why DLA produces branches, not blobs.

Screening is a geometric fact. A random walker coming from far away sees the cluster as a silhouette. The tips poke out. The crevices hide behind them. The deeper a gap, the less likely a walker finds its way in. This is the same reason lightning follows branching paths: charge diffuses through air and preferentially strikes protruding conductors.

3. Stickiness and Density

So far every walker sticks on first contact. What happens if you reduce the sticking probability? A walker that touches the cluster but does not stick continues its random walk. It bounces around near the surface. Eventually it sticks somewhere, but that somewhere is more likely to be deep inside a crevice because the walker had time to explore.

Low stickiness defeats the screening effect. The cluster becomes denser, rounder, and less branchy. High stickiness amplifies screening. The cluster becomes sparse and wildly branched. The sticking probability controls the fractal dimension: how much the structure fills space.

4. From DLA to DBM: the η exponent

Stickiness is one way to reweight where walkers land. There is a deeper knob. Zoom out from the random-walk picture and notice that the arrival probability at a boundary cell is exactly the value of a Laplacian field there: the same field we visualized as a heatmap in Section 2. The dielectric breakdown model (DBM) of Niemeyer, Pietronero, and Wiesmann makes this explicit. Instead of releasing walkers one by one, solve for the potential φ on the empty lattice with φ = 1 on the cluster and φ = 0 on a far boundary. Then grow into an empty neighbor with probability proportional to |∇φ|η.

DBM contains DLA as its η = 1 special case. When η = 1 the gradient is the arrival probability, so DBM reproduces the same statistics we get by releasing walkers. Crank η up and tips with the strongest field dominate: growth concentrates at a single needle and the tree stops branching. Dial η down and the field bias vanishes: every boundary cell is nearly equally likely to grow, so the cluster fattens into a space-filling blob. Lightning does not follow a predetermined path. The bolt you see is the end result of an invisible search: a stepped leader probing the air, extending toward high-field regions, competing tips winning and dying. That search is DBM with η somewhere around 1 to 2.

Lightning, river networks, and DLA all produce similar branching trees because they are all Laplacian growth. Lightning grows from the cloud downward, a single source branching toward a distributed ground. Rivers invert the geometry: distributed sources converging toward a single outlet. DLA uses a radial source and a point seed. Same math, different boundary conditions, similar fractal trees.

Lightning and rivers are dual problems. Flip the direction of growth, and the branching pattern inverts, but the underlying Laplacian growth model is identical. When you see a lightning bolt frozen in a photograph, it looks like a river delta turned upside down because it is one. Both are solutions to the same equation: grow where the gradient is steepest, with a little randomness mixed in.

5. Multiple Seeds

With multiple seeds, each starts its own cluster. As they grow, they compete for incoming walkers. A branch from one cluster screens territory from the others. The boundaries between clusters trace out curves resembling Voronoi cells.

This is how competing DLA structures partition space. Each cluster captures the walkers nearest to its exposed surface. The pattern is reminiscent of mineral dendrites growing on a rock face or frost crystals competing for moisture on a windowpane.

DLA was discovered by Tom Witten and Leonard Sander in 1981. The same growth rule explains the branching of electrodeposition, mineral dendrites, bacterial colonies, and dielectric breakdown. Any system where particles diffuse and irreversibly attach produces these fractal trees.
