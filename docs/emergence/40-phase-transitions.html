Phase Transitions

Exploring the Ising model and the emergence of collective order from local interactions.

At high temperatures, a magnet is just a collection of random, jiggling atoms. But as it cools, something dramatic happens. At a precise critical temperature, the atoms spontaneously align, and a global magnetic field emerges. This sudden shift from disorder to order is a phase transition.

The Ising model is the standard mathematical tool for studying these transitions. It consists of a lattice of spins, each pointing either up or down. Spins prefer to align with their neighbors, but thermal energy works to randomize them. The balance between these two forces is determined by the temperature \(T\).

Critical Fluctuations

At the critical temperature \(T_c\), the system exists in a state of beautiful indecision. Clusters of aligned spins form at every possible size, from a few atoms to the entire lattice. These "critical fluctuations" are scale-invariant, meaning they look the same whether you zoom in or out.

The Ising model shows that phase transitions are not unique to magnets. They appear in any system where local imitation competes with global noise, from the alignment of bird flocks to the spread of opinions in a society.

Universality

One of the most profound discoveries in physics is that very different systems often share the same critical exponents. This means that near the transition, the details of the individual agents don't matter. Only the dimensionality and the symmetry of the interactions determine the global behavior.

\[ M(T) \propto (T_c - T)^\beta \]
