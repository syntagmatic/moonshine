Forest Fire Model

How simple growth and destruction rules lead to self-organized criticality and power-law distributions.

Imagine a landscape where trees grow steadily on empty ground. Occasionally, lightning strikes. If it hits a tree, fire spreads instantly to every connected neighbor, burning the entire cluster to ash. This cycle of regrowth and destruction produces complex, shifting patterns that never quite settle.

This is the Drossel-Schwaab forest fire model. It is a classic example of self-organized criticality. The system is defined by two rates: the probability of a tree growing in an empty cell, \(p\), and the probability of lightning hitting a tree, \(f\). When \(f\) is much smaller than \(p\), the forest reaches a state where fire sizes follow a power law.

The Density Threshold

Before looking at the full dynamic model, consider a static version. If you plant trees at random with a specific density \(D\), at what point can a single spark burn down the entire forest?

This is a percolation problem. For a square grid, the critical threshold is approximately \(D_c \approx 0.5927\). Below this density, fires stay in small, isolated patches. Above it, a "giant component" forms, and a fire can sweep across the entire landscape.

Self-Organized Criticality

In the dynamic model, we do not set the density. Instead, the system finds its own way to the edge of the threshold. Trees grow slowly, increasing the density until the forest is connected enough for a fire to clear a large patch.

The system is "self-organized" because it requires no external tuning to reach the critical state. If the forest is too sparse, fires are small and growth dominates. If the forest is too dense, a single fire thins it out. The balance is held at the edge.

The Power Law

If we track the size of every fire over a long period, we find that the frequency of fires of size \(S\) follows a power law: \(N(S) \propto S^{-\alpha}\). This means that while small fires are common, massive, landscape-altering fires are a predictable part of the system's statistics.

Figure 3. Distribution of fire sizes on a log-log scale. A straight line indicates a power law. This signature of criticality is found in real-world wildfires, earthquakes, and heartbeats.

The forest fire model suggests that the devastating nature of large fires is not necessarily due to unique external causes, but is an inherent property of any system that grows steadily and clears itself through cascading failures.
