Predator-Prey

Two species locked in an endless oscillation that neither controls.

Imagine a meadow with rabbits and foxes. Rabbits eat grass and multiply. Foxes eat rabbits and multiply. When rabbits are plentiful, foxes thrive. But thriving foxes eat more rabbits. Fewer rabbits means starving foxes. Fewer foxes lets rabbits rebound. The cycle starts again.

This relationship is captured by the Lotka-Volterra equations, a coupled pair of ODEs in which prey density $x$ and predator density $y$ regulate each other through a shared predation term.

No species decides the cycle. The oscillation is an emergent property of growth and consumption rules. Order arises from coupling.

2. Simulating Individuals

The Lotka-Volterra equations are continuous. They assume populations are perfectly mixed fluids where every predator has an equal chance of encountering every prey. But real animals are discrete individuals living in space.

What happens if we simulate the rabbits and foxes directly? We can use a grid. Each cell is either empty, holds a rabbit, or holds a fox. The rules are simple:

  • Rabbits wander to adjacent empty cells. After a certain number of steps, they reproduce, leaving a new rabbit behind.
  • Foxes hunt by moving to an adjacent cell containing a rabbit and eating it, restoring their energy. If no rabbits are near, they wander to an empty cell and lose energy.
  • If a fox's energy drops to zero, it starves. If a fox survives long enough, it reproduces.

This is known as the Wa-Tor model (originally framed with fish and sharks by A.K. Dewdney in 1984). Let's see if the continuous math of Lotka-Volterra still holds up when the world is made of discrete, wandering agents.

In the agent-based model, oscillations still emerge, but they are noisy. Furthermore, space matters: foxes eat all the local rabbits and then starve, leaving pockets of empty space where surviving rabbits can multiply explosively. The discrete nature of the simulation also introduces the real possibility of extinction—if the red line hits zero, the foxes are gone forever.
