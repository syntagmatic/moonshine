The Kuramoto Model

Oscillators with different natural rhythms, from metronomes to fireflies, fall into step once their coupling passes a threshold. Kuramoto's model predicts that threshold from the distribution of their rhythms.

The excitable cell of the previous essay oscillates on its own under a steady drive. Here many oscillators with different natural rhythms are coupled: when do they fall into step, and how sharply? Metronomes on a board that can roll freely pull each other into step through the board's motion, with no leader (Pantaleone 2002).

Yoshiki Kuramoto's 1975 model captures this with $N$ oscillators, each with its own natural frequency $\omega_i$ and each pulled toward the others:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d\theta_i}{dt} &= \omega_i + \frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \sin(\theta_j - \theta_i) \\ &= \omega_i + K r \sin(\psi - \theta_i), \end{aligned} \]

where $r e^{i\psi} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_j e^{i\theta_j}$ is the average of the phases as points on a circle. In the second form (Strogatz 2000) each oscillator feels only the mean phase $\psi$, pulled with strength $Kr$.

1. Who Locks?

At $K = 0$ the oscillators scatter around the circle; $r$, the arrow's length, runs from 0 (incoherent) to 1 (in step).

Which ones join? An oscillator's lag $\phi = \theta - \psi$ behind the mean phase, which turns at rate $\Omega$, obeys $d\phi/dt = \omega - \Omega - Kr\sin\phi$. If $|\omega - \Omega| \le Kr$ this has a fixed point and the oscillator locks; otherwise it slips, at average rate $\Omega \pm \sqrt{(\omega - \Omega)^2 - (Kr)^2}$.

2. The Phase Transition

For a symmetric, single-peaked frequency density $g$, Kuramoto's self-consistency argument puts the threshold at $K_c = 2/(\pi g(0))$ (Kuramoto 1975, 1984; Strogatz 2000). For a Lorentzian of half-width $\gamma$ that is $K_c = 2\gamma$, and above it $r = \sqrt{1 - K_c/K}$, for infinitely many oscillators. For Lorentzian frequencies Ott and Antonsen (2008) found that the infinite system obeys one equation exactly, \[ \frac{dr}{dt} = -\gamma r + \frac{K}{2}\, r\,(1 - r^2), \] when the phases start as a wrapped Lorentzian bump, the same for every frequency. Below $K_c = 2\gamma$ coherence decays at rate $\gamma - K/2$; above it the fixed point is Kuramoto's curve again.

3. The Frequency Distribution

The threshold depends only on the density at the center: $K_c$ is 1 for the Lorentzian ($\gamma = 0.5$), $2\sigma\sqrt{2/\pi} \approx 0.80$ for the Gaussian ($\sigma = 0.5$) and $6/\pi \approx 1.91$ for the uniform density on $-1.5$ to $1.5$. Above it the rest of the shape matters. Requiring the locked oscillators, at $\sin\phi = \omega/Kr$, to reproduce $r$ gives Kuramoto's condition $r = Kr\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos^2\phi\; g(Kr\sin\phi)\,d\phi$ (Strogatz 2000). A uniform density has no tails to recruit: everyone locks once anyone can, and $r$ jumps from 0 to $\pi/4$ (Pazó 2005).

The Gaussian climbs faster than the Lorentzian (0.66 against 0.40 at $1.2\,K_c$, both on their curves) because it has fewer stragglers in its tails.

A bimodal distribution has no single threshold. Over a window of coupling two groups lock separately, one per peak, and lap each other, so $r$ beats: the standing-wave state that Martens et al. (2009) solved for two Lorentzian peaks. Stronger coupling merges the groups.

4. Fireflies

Male Pteroptyx malaccae fireflies gathered in trees in Thailand flash in synchrony about once every 560 ms (Buck and Buck 1968). A standard cartoon of how flashes can align is Mirollo and Strogatz's (1990) pulse coupling: each oscillator charges toward a threshold, fires and resets, and each flash raises the others' charge by $\varepsilon$, firing any it pushes past the threshold. With a charge that rises fast and then slowly, a pulse advances a nearly charged oscillator further in phase than a freshly reset one, so phases bunch up. 5. Oscillators on a Ring

In a ring each drummer hears only its two neighbors. Besides unison, a ring can lock into a twisted state, a traveling wave whose phase winds a whole number of turns around it (Wiley, Strogatz and Girvan 2006); about one run in five at the default settings ends twisted.

London's Millennium Bridge swayed on its opening day, 10 June 2000, as pedestrians fell into step with its sideways motion and made it larger. Strogatz and colleagues (2005) modeled the crowd as synchronizing oscillators with the bridge as the shared board, and estimated the damping a crowded footbridge needs to stay below threshold; it reopened in 2002 with dampers.
