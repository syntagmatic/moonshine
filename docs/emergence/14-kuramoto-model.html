The Kuramoto Model

Oscillators with different natural rhythms, from metronomes to fireflies, fall into step once their coupling passes a threshold. Kuramoto's model predicts that threshold from the distribution of their rhythms.

The excitable cell of the previous essay oscillates on its own under a steady drive. Here many oscillators with different natural rhythms are coupled, and the question is when they fall into step and how sharply the change happens.

Set a few metronomes ticking on a board that can roll freely, and the board's small motion pulls them into step (Pantaleone 2002). None of them acts as a leader; the coupling through the board alone synchronizes them.

Yoshiki Kuramoto's 1975 model captures this with $N$ oscillators, each with its own natural frequency $\omega_i$ and each pulled toward the others:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d\theta_i}{dt} &= \omega_i + \frac{K}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \sin(\theta_j - \theta_i) \\ &= \omega_i + K r \sin(\psi - \theta_i), \end{aligned} \]

where $r e^{i\psi} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_j e^{i\theta_j}$ is the average of all the phases as points on a circle. The second form (Strogatz 2000) shows each oscillator feeling only the group's mean phase $\psi$, with a pull proportional to the coherence $r$. For many oscillators, a single coupling parameter $K$ controls a sharp phase transition.

1. A Circle of Oscillators

Each oscillator has a phase and a natural frequency. At $K = 0$ each runs at its own rate and they scatter around the circle. Past the critical $K$, oscillators near the mean frequency lock together first, forming a cluster that grows with $K$, while those in the tails keep drifting past it (Strogatz 2000). The order parameter $r$ measures coherence: 0 is incoherent, 1 fully synced.

2. The Phase Transition

For infinitely many oscillators $r$ stays at zero until a critical coupling, then lifts continuously. Kuramoto's self-consistency argument gives the threshold for any symmetric, single-peaked frequency density $g$ as $K_c = 2/(\pi g(0))$ (Kuramoto 1975, 1984; Strogatz 2000). For a Lorentzian with half-width $\gamma$, $g(0) = 1/(\pi\gamma)$, so $K_c = 2\gamma$, and Kuramoto also solved the whole curve above threshold, $r = \sqrt{1 - K_c/K}$. The $K$ you pick in Figure 2 also drives the circle above.

3. The Frequency Distribution

A narrow frequency distribution synchronizes at weaker coupling than a wide one, because $K_c = 2/(\pi g(0))$ depends on how many oscillators sit near the middle. With the widths used below, the Lorentzian ($\gamma = 0.5$) has $K_c = 1$, the Gaussian (standard deviation 0.5) has $K_c = 2\sigma\sqrt{2/\pi} \approx 0.80$, and the uniform one (on $-1.5$ to $1.5$) has $K_c = 6/\pi \approx 1.91$. The uniform case is different in kind: there are no tails to recruit gradually, and $r$ jumps discontinuously at $K_c$ (Pazó 2005). At $N = 60$ it goes from about 0.5 at $K = 1.8$ to about 0.8 or 0.9 at $K = 2$.

A bimodal distribution has no single threshold. Over a window of coupling, two groups lock separately, one around each peak, and rotate at different speeds, so $r$ beats up and down as they lap each other: the standing-wave state that Martens et al. (2009) worked out exactly for two Lorentzian peaks. Stronger coupling merges the groups into one. With the two Gaussian peaks here at $\pm 1$ (standard deviation 0.25 each) and 60 oscillators, the two groups appear from about $K = 0.8$ and merge between $K = 2$ and $2.2$.

4. Fireflies

In Thailand, male Pteroptyx malaccae fireflies gathered in trees flash in rhythmic synchrony, about once every 560 ms (Buck and Buck 1968). A standard cartoon of how flashes can pull each other into step is Mirollo and Strogatz's (1990) pulse coupling: each oscillator charges toward a threshold, fires, and resets, and each flash pushes the charge of the others up by a fixed amount $\varepsilon$, firing any it pushes past the threshold. The charge rises quickly at first and then more slowly, like a leaky capacitor, so a pulse moves a nearly charged oscillator further in phase than a freshly reset one, and phases bunch up.

Below, each firefly only sees its eight neighbors. At $\varepsilon = 0$ the field twinkles at random; around $\varepsilon = 0.01$ neighbors lock into patches that flash together, but the patches keep their own timing; from about $\varepsilon = 0.02$ the whole field flashes as one within half a minute. 5. Oscillators on a Ring

So far every oscillator has felt the average of all the others. Most real coupling is local. In a ring-coupled Kuramoto model each drummer hears only its two neighbors, a cartoon of a drum circle locking its rhythm. Besides unison, a ring can lock into a twisted state, a traveling wave in which the phase winds a whole number of turns around the ring (Wiley, Strogatz and Girvan 2006), and when the tempos are too spread for the coupling it breaks into runs of neighbors that share a tempo. From random starts at the default settings, about one run in five ends twisted.

Click a drummer to knock its phase out of place; recovery is faster at higher coupling (in node runs of three kicked drummers, the median time back above $r = 0.8$ fell from about 1.9 s at coupling 0.5 to 0.14 s at 2). With sound on, each flash is a click pitched by ring position.

London's Millennium Bridge began to sway soon after the crowd streamed onto it on opening day, 10 June 2000: many pedestrians fell into step with its small sideways motion and made it larger. Strogatz and colleagues (2005) modeled it with the ideas developed for synchronizing oscillators, the bridge playing the part of the shared platform, and their approach estimates how much damping a crowded footbridge needs to stay below the threshold. The bridge reopened in 2002 fitted with dampers.
