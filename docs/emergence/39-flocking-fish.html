Schooling Fish

How underwater agents split and reform to survive in a fluid environment.

Fish schools move with a liquid grace that rivals the flocking of birds. While they follow similar rules of alignment and cohesion, the fluid environment introduces unique dynamics. Information propagates through the school via the lateral line, a sensory organ that detects pressure changes in the water.

This rapid communication allows the school to act as a single unit, reacting to threats with collective maneuvers that no individual could perform alone.

Splitting and Reforming

When a predator approaches, the school does not simply scatter. It splits around the threat in a coordinated "flash expansion." Once the danger has passed, the school seamlessly reforms, driven by the persistent pull of cohesion.

No single fish decides when to split the school. Each fish simply maintains a safe distance from the predator and its neighbors. The "vacuole" that forms around the predator is a collective response to individual safety rules.

The Three Rules

Like all flocking systems, schooling is governed by three primary forces:

1. Separation: Avoid collisions with nearby neighbors. 2. Alignment: Match the heading of the local group. 3. Cohesion: Move toward the average position of neighbors.
