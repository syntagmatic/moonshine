Two Ways to Make a Pattern

Biology has two explanations for how a uniform tissue becomes patterned: Turing's reaction-diffusion, which sets its own spacing, and Wolpert's positional information, which reads position off a gradient. Growing the tissue and adding noise tell them apart.

The predator-prey equations of the previous essay make waves but no Turing spots: they lack a self-enhancing activator. This essay starts from a reaction that has one.

A fruit-fly embryo is at first one cell holding thousands of nuclei, and within three hours they carry stripes of gene expression that prefigure the segments. Alan Turing's 1952 explanation for such patterns was that two reacting, diffusing chemicals can make a uniform field unstable at a spacing set by the chemistry. Lewis Wolpert's 1969 explanation was positional information: a signal fades from one end of the tissue, and each cell reads its concentration as a coordinate.

The Gray-Scott model

In the Gray-Scott model a substrate U is fed in everywhere at rate \(f\), and V makes more of itself by consuming U, through \(U + 2V \to 3V\), and is removed at rate \(f + k\):

\[ \partial_t u = D_u \nabla^2 u - u v^2 + f(1-u), \qquad \partial_t v = D_v \nabla^2 v + u v^2 - (f+k)\,v. \]

V is the activator, and U inhibits by running out. U diffuses twice as fast as V (\(D_u = 0.21\), \(D_v = 0.105\) in grid units), so a spot's depleted zone reaches beyond it and keeps a second spot away, the activation with wider-range inhibition that Gierer and Meinhardt (1972) set out. Without V nothing makes V, so the empty state \(u = 1, v = 0\) is stable and the grid starts from seeds.

Where does the spacing come from?

Turing's argument starts from a uniform state that is stable on its own. Diffusion damps a ripple of wavenumber \(q\) at a rate \(D q^2\) in each species, so the ripple grows at

\[ \sigma(q) = \max \operatorname{Re}\,\operatorname{eig}\Big(J - q^2 \begin{pmatrix} D_u & 0 \\ 0 & D_v \end{pmatrix}\Big). \]

If the inhibitor diffuses enough faster than the activator, a band of ripples grows although uniform pushes die away, and the fastest one sets the wavelength. Gray-Scott's candidate is the V-rich state below the curve; at this page's ratio of 2 its band opens only in a strip under 0.001 wide in k, for f above about 0.048.

A box that holds two or three waves forces a whole number of them, but from 64 cells up each preset keeps its wavelength within 0.6 of a cell, all between 12.7 and 14.5 cells. That spacing fixes no position, and an embryo needs its head at one end.

Positional information

In Wolpert's model cells at one end make a morphogen that diffuses and decays, so at steady state (\(D\,C'' = k_{\text{deg}}\,C\)) it falls off with decay length \(\lambda = \sqrt{D/k_{\text{deg}}}\). Each cell picks one of three fates from two thresholds: Wolpert's French flag, blue, white and red. In the fruit fly, Bicoid falls off from the anterior pole with a decay length about a fifth of the egg, and hunchback switches on above a threshold. Embryos from mothers given extra copies of bicoid shift their head structures toward the tail (Driever and Nüsslein-Volhard, 1988), as a threshold reading of a stronger gradient predicts.

The Turing row keeps its spacing, 6.9 to 7.7 cells at every length, and adds peaks; the source-sink flag keeps proportions; the fixed-decay flag does neither. Body plans need proportion. In fly species with eggs of very different sizes, expression patterns sit at nearly the same fractional positions because the Bicoid decay length scales with the egg (Gregor and colleagues, 2005). Within one species the hunchback boundary scales although Bicoid varies (Houchmandzadeh, Wieschaus and Leibler, 2002), by a means still debated.

A fish grows while its pattern runs. A young angelfish (Pomacanthus) has three stripes, and Kondo and Asai (1995) saw new ones open between the old as it grew, keeping the spacing roughly constant. Figure 6 grows the row while it runs.

At \(10^{-5}\) per step peaks are added one or two at a time, as peaks split where the gaps have stretched most: with noise seed 1, 10 peaks at 73 cells, 20 at 162, 36 at 297, about 8.3 cells apart against 7.5 in the fixed rows. Crampin, Gaffney and Maini (1999) predicted that exponential growth can double the count at once over a range of rates; this setting shows no doubling at any rate from \(2 \times 10^{-6}\) to \(10^{-3}\) (noise seeds 1 to 3). Faster growth adds peaks irregularly and lags behind, and at \(1.5 \times 10^{-3}\) no pattern forms. New peaks come from old ones splitting, not from the gaps as in the fish.

Robustness to noise

The second test is noise. A cell that reads an exponential gradient with relative error \(\sigma\) misplaces a boundary by about \(\sigma\lambda\), 4 cells for a 10% error and a 40-cell decay length, and a source stronger by a factor \(a\) moves every boundary by \(\lambda \ln a\). A Turing pattern has no source to vary and its peaks rise steeply, so noisy reading barely moves their edges. Its count varies: the embryos below start from different noise and, since a band of spacings is stable, end with 26 to 29 peaks.

Positional information puts the pattern in the same place in every embryo, as precisely as the gradient allows. In Drosophila neighbouring nuclei, 1 to 2% of the egg length apart, see Bicoid levels about 10% apart, and Hunchback reads differences that small, near the limit set by the random arrival of Bicoid molecules (Gregor, Tank, Wieschaus and Bialek, 2007). Self-organization gives sharp edges without a calibrated source but fixes neither the number of repeats nor where the first falls.

Real tissues often use both. In the mouse limb a Turing-type network of Bmp, Sox9 and Wnt spaces the digits while Fgf and Hox gradients modulate the wavelength, so the digits fan out from a narrow base (Raspopovic and colleagues, 2014; Green and Sharpe, 2015).

The next essay makes the inhibitor slow and lets only the activator diffuse. The medium then fires when pushed past a threshold and carries waves that curl into spirals.
