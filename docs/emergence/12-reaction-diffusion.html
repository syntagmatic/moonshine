Two Ways to Make a Pattern

Turing's reaction-diffusion and Wolpert's positional information both turn a uniform tissue into a patterned one. Grow the tissue or add noise and they come apart.

A fruit-fly embryo spends its first hours as a single long cell holding thousands of nuclei, and by about three hours after fertilization those nuclei carry stripes of gene expression that prefigure the head, thorax and abdominal segments. A young angelfish has three stripes and adds more as it grows. Both are uniform tissues that become patterned, and biology has two different explanations.

Alan Turing's 1952 answer was that two diffusing chemicals that react with each other can make a uniform field unstable, so peaks and troughs appear with a spacing set by the chemistry alone. Lewis Wolpert's 1969 answer was that a cell only needs to know where it is. A signal made at one end of the tissue spreads and fades, and each cell reads its local concentration as a coordinate. This essay runs both mechanisms side by side and asks what experiment could tell them apart.

A pattern with no address

In the Gray-Scott model, a substrate U is fed into every point of the surface at rate \(f\). A second species V makes more of itself by consuming U, through the reaction \(U + 2V \to 3V\), and is removed at rate \(f + k\):

\[ \partial_t u = D_u \nabla^2 u - u v^2 + f(1-u), \qquad \partial_t v = D_v \nabla^2 v + u v^2 - (f+k)\,v. \]

V is the self-enhancing activator, and U inhibits by running out. A growing V spot eats the substrate around it, and because U diffuses twice as fast as V (\(D_u = 0.21\), \(D_v = 0.105\) in grid units here), the depleted zone reaches further than the spot, so a second spot cannot start too close to the first. Short-range activation with longer-range inhibition is the combination Turing identified, and it sets the distance between spots.

At these diffusion constants the empty state \(u = 1, v = 0\) is stable, so the grid below starts from a scatter of seeds rather than pure noise. The spacing that emerges does not depend on where the seeds were placed.

The parameter map

Only two numbers are free. Setting the time derivatives to zero with no spatial variation gives the uniform states: the empty state, and a pair of V-rich states that exist only while \((f+k)^2 \le f/4\), below the curve \(k = \sqrt{f}/2 - f\). Well below that curve, the seeds tip the whole grid into the V-rich uniform state. Above it the only uniform state is the empty one, and yet that is where nearly all the patterns live. Spots, worms and labyrinths survive there because they are not uniform: each feeds on the substrate around it and cannot spread into the depleted zone next door.

No tile refers to the edge of the grid or to where the seeds were placed. The spots are as far apart as the chemistry says, whether the tile is 32 cells wide or 256. That is the signature of self-organization, and its limitation: an embryo usually needs a head at one particular end.

A pattern with an address

In Wolpert's model, cells at one end of the tissue make a signal molecule, a morphogen, which diffuses away and is degraded as it goes. At steady state, diffusion balances decay, \(D\,C'' = k_{\text{deg}}\,C\), and the solution falls off exponentially with a decay length \(\lambda = \sqrt{D/k_{\text{deg}}}\). Each cell compares its local concentration with a pair of thresholds and picks one of three fates. Wolpert drew the result as a French flag: blue near the source, white in the middle, red far away.

The fruit fly does something like this. The mother parks Bicoid mRNA at the anterior pole of the egg, and the protein made from it forms a gradient with a decay length of about a fifth of the egg's length. Target genes such as hunchback switch on above a threshold of Bicoid, and when Driever and Nüsslein-Volhard gave mothers extra copies of bicoid, the head structures of the embryos shifted toward the tail, as a threshold reading of a stronger gradient predicts.

Nothing here is self-organized. The pattern has a definite position because the source has one, and every boundary inherits the gradient's precision. The two mechanisms can now face the same two tests.

Grow the tissue

The first test is size: vary the length of a row of cells and see what each mechanism puts in it. The Turing row keeps its spacing, about 15 cells, and adds peaks as it lengthens. So does the angelfish: Kondo and Asai showed in 1995 that as Pomacanthus grows, new stripes open between the old ones and the spacing stays roughly constant. Proportions are lost. The source-sink flag keeps them exactly, with boundaries at one third and two thirds at every length, because the gradient stretches to fit. The fixed-decay-length flag does neither: its boundaries sit at fixed distances from the source, so a short tissue never reaches red and a long one is mostly red.

Most body plans need proportion. Fly embryos from differently sized eggs place their segments at nearly the same fractional positions, and how a gradient that fades over a fixed length could manage that is still open. Turing's mechanism cannot do it alone, since its only length scale is internal.

Add noise

The second test is noise. Suppose each cell reads its morphogen concentration with relative error \(\sigma\). A boundary sits where the reading crosses a threshold, so an error \(\delta C\) moves it by \(\delta C / |C'|\). For an exponential gradient \(|C'| = C/\lambda\), giving a positional error of about \(\sigma\lambda\): a 10% error with a 40-cell decay length puts the boundary about 4 cells out and leaves it ragged over several cells. Embryo-to-embryo variation in how much morphogen the source makes is different. Scaling the whole gradient by \(a\) moves every threshold crossing by \(\lambda \ln a\), so the flag slides.

A Turing pattern has no source to vary, and its peaks climb from nothing to full height over a few cells, so a noisy readout of V barely moves their edges. What it cannot fix is the count. Each embryo below starts from three seeds at random places. Peaks split and spread until they fill the row, the outermost settle a few cells from the walls, and because a band of spacings is stable rather than a single one, different embryos end up with different numbers of peaks.

Positional information puts the same pattern in the same place in every embryo and pays in precision, which is bounded by how steep and reliable the gradient is. The Drosophila embryo does well here: Gregor, Tank, Wieschaus and Bialek measured the hunchback boundary to within about 1% of egg length, roughly one nucleus, and argued that this is near the limit set by counting Bicoid molecules. Self-organization gets sharp edges for free and has nothing to calibrate, but it delivers a range of spacings, so it cannot promise how many repeats a tissue of given length will get or where the first one falls.

Real tissues often use both. In the developing mouse limb, a Turing-type network of Bmp, Sox9 and Wnt sets the periodic spacing of the digits, while Fgf and Hox gradients that run along the limb bud modulate the wavelength, so that the digit rudiments fan out from a narrow base (Raspopovic and colleagues, 2014; reviewed by Green and Sharpe, 2015).
