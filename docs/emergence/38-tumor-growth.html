Tumor Growth

Exploring how limited nutrients and diffusion gradients shape the morphology of developing tumors.

Tumors are not uniform masses. As they grow, they face intense competition for resources. Oxygen and glucose diffuse from surrounding healthy tissue, but they are consumed by the outermost layers of cells. This creates a nutrient gradient that determines where cells can thrive and where they must perish.

This process is a biological instance of diffusion-limited growth. It produces a characteristic structure: a viable rim of proliferating cells on the outside, and a necrotic core of dead cells on the inside where nutrients cannot reach.

Diffusion-Limited Branching

The shape of a tumor is often highly irregular. This branching morphology emerges from a fundamental instability. Any small protrusion on the tumor surface extends further into the nutrient-rich surrounding area. Because it has better access to resources, it grows faster than the surrounding flat regions, amplifying the protrusion into a branch.

This is the same mechanism that creates the intricate patterns of snowflakes or the branching of mineral deposits. In physics, this is known as the Mullins-Sekerka instability.

The Mathematical Framework

We can model the nutrient concentration \(c\) using the diffusion equation:

\[ \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} = D \nabla^2 c - k c \]

where \(D\) is the diffusion coefficient and \(k\) is the consumption rate by the cells. In our simulation, the growth probability of a surface cell is proportional to the local concentration \(c\).
