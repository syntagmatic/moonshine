Excitable Media

One pair of equations models the traveling and spiral waves of the Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction and of heart muscle, where spiral waves are a leading explanation for fibrillation.

The Gray-Scott patterns of the previous essay come from an activator and a faster-diffusing inhibitor. Make the inhibitor slow instead and the pair describes an excitable medium: each part rests until pushed past threshold, fires, then stays refractory until it recovers.

The Belousov-Zhabotinsky (BZ) reaction carries such waves in a thin unstirred layer (Zaikin and Zhabotinsky 1970), and a broken wave curls into spirals (Winfree 1972). Spiral waves also turn in heart muscle, where they are a leading explanation for ventricular tachycardia and fibrillation (Davidenko et al. 1992). This essay builds one model of both and measures what a wave needs to come back around.

1. The Excitable Cell

The FitzHugh-Nagumo equations (FitzHugh 1961; Nagumo, Arimoto and Yoshizawa 1962) are one of the simplest models. An activator \(u\) drives firing; an inhibitor \(v\) shuts it down, slowly, at a rate set by the small number \(\epsilon\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{du}{dt} &= u - \frac{u^3}{3} - v + I, \\ \frac{dv}{dt} &= \epsilon\,(u + a - b\,v). \end{aligned} \]

Every figure uses \(b = 0.8\). In the BZ reaction's Oregonator model (Field and Noyes 1974), built on the Field-Koros-Noyes mechanism (1972), the fast species is bromous acid and the slow one the oxidized catalyst, cerium(IV), which releases the bromide that halts autocatalysis. In heart muscle the pair stand for membrane voltage and a lumped recovery variable. With a steady drive \(I = 0.5\) (Figure 1), an unstable fixed point makes the cell tick; a stable one leaves it resting until kicked.

2. Waves in a BZ Dish

Stirred, the solution (bromate and malonic acid in sulfuric acid, with cerium) ticks between yellow cerium(IV) and colorless cerium(III) (Field, Koros and Noyes 1972). Unstirred, each point of a thin film is an FHN unit coupled to its neighbors by diffusion, \(D\,\nabla^2 u\). With no drive (\(I = 0\)) and \(a = 0.5\) each point rests until a neighbor fires, so a wave rolls out from any stimulus; below about \(a = 0.46\) the whole dish flashes by itself. Colliding fronts die in each other's refractory trails; water waves would pass through.

3. Pacing Cardiac Tissue

Heart muscle is excitable in the same way. At threshold a myocyte's sodium channels open, the voltage spikes and holds on a plateau while calcium enters, and potassium current brings it back down. The sodium channels stay inactivated until the membrane has repolarized, and until then the cell is refractory (Klabunde, Cardiovascular Physiology Concepts).

Figure 3 paces a row of FHN cells from one end. Started from rest, it follows every stimulus at intervals down to 438 steps. At 436 every second stimulus lands on refractory tissue beside the electrode and dies: 2:1 block.

4. Re-entry and Spiral Waves

A pulse that returns to tissue it has already excited needs no pacemaker. That is re-entry, simplest on a ring (Figure 5). One stimulus launches two pulses that annihilate on the far side; make one side refractory and a single pulse circulates indefinitely at the ring's own rhythm. Shrink the ring and the pulse runs into its own recovering tail, slows, and laps sooner: 993 steps on 150 cells, about 330 on 12. On 10 it dies.

A spiral is re-entry without a hole, circulating around its own tip (Figure 6). Here it turns every 470 steps, a little slower than the fastest pacing Figure 4's cable follows. Davidenko et al. (1992) filmed spirals in isolated heart muscle turning about every 180 ms, three to five times a normal heart rate; re-entry that outpaces the heart's pacemaker is widely held to cause the most dangerous arrhythmias (Fenton et al. 2002). Gray, Pertsov and Jalife (1998) traced fibrillation to spiral tips at a few sites, forming and vanishing.

The S1-S2 protocol makes a spiral on purpose: a planar wave (S1), then a stimulus behind it (S2) while its tail recovers (Figure 7). Too early, S2 dies on refractory tissue; too late, its wave spreads into recovered tissue with no free end. In between, it can spread only where the tissue has recovered, so its upper end is free and curls.

Winfree's When Time Breaks Down (1987) treated cardiac re-entry as a problem in topology. A defibrillator shock depolarizes much of the heart at once; Zipes and colleagues (1975) found in dogs that fibrillation stopped when a critical mass of the ventricles was depolarized. In the model every cell fires and recovers together, no wave is left with tissue to enter, and the sheet is quiet within about 250 steps.

The same waves gather Dictyostelium amoebae (cyclic AMP, concentric and spiral; Tomchik and Devreotes 1981) and spiral through frog oocytes (calcium; Lechleiter et al. 1991). The driven cell of Figure 1, though, fires on its own. The next essay couples many such oscillators, each with its own frequency, and asks when they fall into step.
