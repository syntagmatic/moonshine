Excitable Media

One pair of equations models the traveling and spiral waves of the Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction and of heart muscle, where spiral waves are a leading explanation for fibrillation.

The Gray-Scott patterns of the previous essay come from an activator and a faster-diffusing inhibitor. Make the inhibitor slow instead, and a similar pair of equations describes an excitable medium. Each part of an excitable medium rests until pushed past threshold, fires, and then stays refractory until it recovers. Coupled in space, the parts carry traveling pulses that annihilate on collision, and they can sustain rotating spiral waves.

The Belousov-Zhabotinsky (BZ) reaction (bromate and malonic acid in sulfuric acid, with a cerium or iron catalyst) oscillates when stirred. In a thin unstirred layer it carries chemical waves, and where a wave is broken the ends curl into spirals (Winfree 1972). Spiral waves also turn in heart muscle, where they are a leading explanation for the re-entrant arrhythmias, ventricular tachycardia and fibrillation (Davidenko et al. 1992).

1. The Excitable Cell

The FitzHugh-Nagumo equations (FitzHugh 1961; Nagumo, Arimoto and Yoshizawa 1962) are one of the simplest models. An activator \(u\) drives firing; an inhibitor \(v\) shuts it down, slowly, at a rate set by the small number \(\epsilon\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{du}{dt} &= u - \frac{u^3}{3} - v + I, \\ \frac{dv}{dt} &= \epsilon\,(u + a - b\,v). \end{aligned} \]

Every figure on this page uses \(b = 0.8\). In the Oregonator, Field and Noyes's (1974) model of the BZ kinetics built on the Field-Koros-Noyes mechanism (1972), the fast autocatalytic species is bromous acid and the slow one is the oxidized catalyst, cerium(IV), whose reaction with the organic acid releases the bromide that shuts autocatalysis down. The FitzHugh-Nagumo pair has the same fast-slow shape. In heart muscle the two variables stand for membrane voltage and a lumped recovery variable.

Figure 1 adds a steady drive \(I = 0.5\). Whether the fixed point where the nullclines cross is stable determines whether the cell ticks on its own or rests until pushed. At the default \(a = 0.5\) it is unstable, and the trajectory in the \((u, v)\) plane settles onto a limit cycle, one lap per oscillation.

2. Waves in a BZ Dish

Stirred in a beaker, the cerium-catalyzed BZ solution ticks between yellow and clear like a chemical clock, as the catalyst swings between yellow cerium(IV) and colorless cerium(III) (Field, Koros and Noyes 1972 tracked the swings with electrodes).

Spread the chemistry across a thin film and each point becomes an FHN unit coupled to its neighbors by activator diffusion, a term \(D\,\nabla^2 u\) added to the first equation. The simulation below has no drive (\(I = 0\)) and sits just short of oscillating: at \(a = 0.5\) each point rests, while below about \(a = 0.46\) the resting state turns unstable and the whole dish flashes by itself. A firing point triggers its neighbors and a wave rolls outward.

Two BZ waves do not pass through each other. Each front leaves a refractory trail that cannot fire until the inhibitor decays, so colliding fronts run into refractory medium and die. Linear water waves, by contrast, pass through each other.

3. Cardiac Tissue

Heart muscle is excitable in the same way. A myocyte rests at a negative voltage until a threshold stimulus opens its sodium channels. The voltage spikes, then holds on a plateau while calcium enters and the cell contracts. Potassium current brings the voltage back down. The sodium channels that fired stay inactivated until the membrane has repolarized, and until they recover the cell cannot fire again: it is refractory (Klabunde, Cardiovascular Physiology Concepts).

Line up such cells and poke one end. Each excited cell triggers its neighbor, and a pulse rolls down the row without reversing, because the trail behind it is refractory.

A 2D sheet of cells models the atrial or ventricular wall. A pacemaker in the corner fires at a steady rate, and at the default interval every cell fires once per beat. Pace faster than the tissue recovers and some stimuli land on refractory tissue and die, the simplest case of rate-dependent conduction block. 4. Spiral Waves

Circular waves die at the edges of the tissue. Break a wavefront, though, and the severed end curls around the break point into a rotating spiral, which emits wave after wave from its tip for as long as the tip stays in the tissue. In this model a spiral turns once every 470 steps, the same interval below which Figure 4's tissue starts blocking stimuli: a spiral drives the tissue about as fast as it can follow.

Several spirals, each turning one way or the other, can coexist. Re-entrant waves that circulate faster than the heart's own pacemaker are widely held to cause the most dangerous arrhythmias (Fenton et al. 2002). Davidenko et al. (1992) filmed spiral waves in isolated heart muscle turning about every 180 ms, three to five times faster than a normal heart rate. One steady spiral drives the tissue fast and regularly, as in a ventricular tachycardia. Fibrillation looks disordered, but Gray, Pertsov and Jalife (1998) mapped it in heart muscle and found its sources as phase singularities, spiral tips, at a few sites, forming and vanishing.

The S1-S2 protocol makes a spiral on purpose. Launch a planar wave (S1), then stimulate the lower half of the tissue behind it (S2) while its tail recovers. Too early and S2 lands on refractory tissue and dies; too late and its wave spreads without curling. In between, S2 can only spread into tissue that has already recovered, its end is free, and it curls into a spiral. In this model the window runs from about 500 to 900 steps after S1.

Zaikin and Zhabotinsky reported traveling waves in the BZ reaction in 1970, and Winfree reported its spiral waves in 1972; his When Time Breaks Down (1987) later treated the heart as an excitable medium and re-entry as a problem in topology. A defibrillator shock depolarizes much of the heart at once. Zipes and colleagues (1975) found in dogs that fibrillation stopped when a critical mass of the ventricles was depolarized. In the model, once every cell fires together, all of them are refractory together, no wave has recovered tissue to move into, and the sheet is quiet within about 250 steps.

The same behavior shows up in the cyclic AMP waves, concentric and spiral, that gather Dictyostelium amoebae (Tomchik and Devreotes 1981) and in the spiral calcium waves inside frog oocytes (Lechleiter et al. 1991).

With a steady drive, the single cell of Figure 1 fires on its own, once per lap of its limit cycle. The next essay couples many oscillators, each with its own natural frequency, and asks when they fall into step.
