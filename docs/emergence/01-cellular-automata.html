Cellular Automata

Three rules that only ever look at their immediate surroundings, and what each one manages to build: triangles and noise, a road, a creature that swims.

A cellular automaton is a grid of cells and one rule. Every cell applies the same rule, and the rule reads only a cell's immediate neighborhood. Any larger shape that appears on the grid had to be assembled by the rule running over time.

This essay looks at three systems, each smoother than the last. In Wolfram's one-dimensional rules a cell holds one bit and sees two neighbors. In Langton's ant a single moving cursor rewrites a plane. In Lenia the states, space and time step are all continuous, and the question becomes which structures survive the smoothing.

Eight neighborhoods, one bit each

In an elementary automaton the cells sit in a row and hold 0 or 1. A cell's next state depends on three bits: its left neighbor, itself and its right neighbor. A rule picks an output for each of the eight possible neighborhoods, so there are 28 = 256 rules. Stephen Wolfram's numbering lists the neighborhoods from 111 down to 000 and reads their outputs as a binary number. The table under Figure 1 is that number written out; click an output square to flip one bit.

From a single live cell, Rule 90 draws a Sierpinski triangle. Its table makes each cell the exclusive-or of its two neighbors, so row t is row t of Pascal's triangle mod 2, predictable with a pencil. Rule 30 differs from Rule 90 in only two output bits. Its left flank settles into diagonal stripes, while the right flank and the center column never show a pattern; Wolfram used that center column as a random number generator in Mathematica.

Rule 184 moves each 1 one cell to the right when the cell ahead is empty and holds it in place otherwise, which makes it the Nagel-Schreckenberg model of the traffic essay with a speed limit of one and no randomness. Press Random start and the jams, runs of stopped 1s, drift backward while cars pass through them. Rule 110 from a random start throws off particles over a periodic background: stripes that travel at different speeds and collide. Matthew Cook proved in 2004 that those collisions can be arranged to emulate any Turing machine.

Wolfram sorted the 256 rules by eye into four classes: rules whose patterns die out, rules that settle into fixed or repeating patterns, rules that look random, and rules like 110 with long-lived particles. The tag beside the rule number gives each preset's class. The same kind of rule on a square grid, reading the eight surrounding cells, is Conway's Game of Life, covered in Life and symmetry attractors.

An ant that builds a road

In 1986 Chris Langton moved the rule off the cells and onto a single cursor. An ant sits on a square grid of white cells. On a white cell it turns right 90°, paints the cell black and steps forward. On a black cell it turns left 90°, paints the cell white and steps forward. That is the whole rule, and it runs backward as easily as forward.

For the first few hundred steps the ant draws small, nearly symmetric figures. Then it builds an irregular blob that grows for thousands of steps with no visible order. Figure 2 plots the ant's distance from its starting cell: it creeps up unevenly through the blob phase, then abruptly climbs in a straight line.

Figure 2. Langton's ant from a blank grid. Left: the grid at the chosen step; black cells were last painted before the highway began, blue cells after it; the red dot is the ant. Right: distance from the starting cell. Drag the chart or slider to move through time. The dashed line marks the step from which the moves repeat, found by scanning the recorded moves when the page loads.

That line is the highway. From move 9,977 on, the sequence of turns repeats with period 104, and each period carries the ant two cells along a diagonal, laying down the same strip of cells behind it. The rule contains no road and no number 104. Both come out of ten thousand steps of history, and no known method predicts the onset short of running the ant. Leonid Bunimovich and Serge Troubetzkoy proved in 1992 that the ant's path is unbounded from any finite starting pattern. Whether it always ends on a highway is open, though every finite start tried so far does.

Smoothing every part of the rule

What survives the continuum

At the catalogued the result is awkward. For the orbium survives at and and dies at 20 and 50: a smaller time step, which should only improve accuracy, kills it. Its parameters were found at and sit at the edge of its island, and refining the time step moves the edge. Switch to and the map looks the way the continuum argument predicts. Every run with and survives, and finer resolution changes only sharpness. Coarse resolution still kills it. At the kernel's peak sits only three cells out, too coarse a ring for this creature, and at or 3 a single step can move a cell's state by a third to a half of its whole range.

States are the last ingredient. The viewer can round every cell to a fixed number of levels after each step. At , a step changes a cell by at most 0.1. With 4 levels, half a level is 0.125, so rounding erases every change and the orbium freezes where it stands. With 6 levels only the steepest changes clear the rounding, and it dies. Between 8 and 16 levels the outcome is erratic; from about 24 levels up it glides with the same mass as with continuous states. Coarse states and fine time steps do not mix: once a level is wider than anything one step can do, nothing moves.
