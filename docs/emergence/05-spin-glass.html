Spin Glass

When not everyone can be satisfied simultaneously, the system freezes into a disordered but structured state.

At a dinner party with friend pairs (same side) and rival pairs (opposite sides), loops of odd length leave at least one pair unhappy no matter how you seat them. That is frustration, the central idea behind spin glasses.

A spin glass is a material with random magnetic couplings. Some bonds want neighbors aligned and some want them opposite, and on certain triangles no assignment satisfies every bond. The system has many nearly equivalent configurations separated by energy barriers, and cooling it freezes in disorder.

The majority rule of the previous essay is this model at zero temperature with every coupling ferromagnetic. There every bond could be satisfied at once, and only geometry blocked consensus. Here the couplings themselves disagree. (The ferromagnet at finite temperature, with its phase transition, lives in the Ising essay of the lattice simulation series.)

1. Spins and Bonds

Each circle is a spin (red up, blue down). Solid bonds are ferromagnetic and want matching spins; dashed bonds are antiferromagnetic and want opposite spins. Frustration is a property of the bonds alone: whatever you do to the spins, the orange triangles stay orange.

A triangle is frustrated exactly when it has an odd number of antiferromagnetic bonds, so what matters is how those bonds sit around loops rather than how many there are.

2. Simulated Annealing

The Metropolis algorithm picks a spin, computes the energy change $\Delta E$ of flipping it, and accepts the flip if it goes downhill, or with probability $e^{-\Delta E / T}$ if it goes uphill. High $T$ accepts almost anything; low $T$ allows only downhill moves.

The schedule here starts at $T = 5$ and ends at $T = 0.05$; the slider sets how many sweeps the descent takes. Unsatisfying one extra bond costs 2 in energy, so final energies come in steps of 2. On this 30-spin lattice a 10-sweep cooling leaves most runs one to four bonds above the lowest energy any run finds. A 1000-sweep cooling brings between half and nine tenths of runs down to that lowest energy, depending on the particular bonds. Click the run chips after a few slow runs: runs that tie at the lowest energy usually sit in different configurations, because on these lattices that energy is shared by anywhere from a handful to several dozen distinct configurations (counting a configuration and its global spin flip as one).

3. The Energy Landscape

A 20-spin patch of the same lattice has $2^{20}$, about a million, configurations, few enough to check every one. Call a configuration stuck if no single flip lowers its energy, and group stuck configurations that are joined by flips costing nothing into one valley. A zero-temperature quench, which is the majority rule again, always ends in some valley. The figure lists every valley, places it by its energy, and measures how often 2000 quenches from random starts end there.

With all bonds ferromagnetic there is one lowest valley, all spins aligned, and it catches a little under half of the quenches. The other valleys are open-boundary cousins of the previous essay's frozen stripes: walls running edge to edge that no single flip can move. Switch to random bonds, 40% antiferromagnetic, and the valley count rises from 37 to somewhere between about 40 and 150 depending on the draw. The lowest energy is sometimes shared by several valleys, and the share of quenches that reach it falls to between about one in fifteen and one in four.

Even at this tiny size, frustration makes the landscape harder in two ways. There are more valleys for a greedy descent to stop in, and the lowest valleys are no longer related by a simple symmetry, so finding one tells you little about where the others are. For this planar lattice with no external field, the true ground state can still be found in polynomial time by a matching algorithm; Barahona showed in 1982 that adding a field or going to three dimensions makes the problem NP-hard.

Giorgio Parisi shared the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physics in part for solving the mean-field spin glass, in which the low-temperature phase splits into a hierarchy of states. His replica methods have since been applied to neural network memory, error-correcting codes, and random optimization problems such as the traveling salesman problem.
