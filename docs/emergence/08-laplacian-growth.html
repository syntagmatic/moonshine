Laplacian Growth

Random walkers that stick where they land grow fractal trees. The field that steers them also shapes electrical discharges, and something like it carves river basins.

Place a seed particle. Release another far away and let it walk randomly until it bumps the first and sticks. Repeat. What grows is a fractal tree of nested branches.

Witten and Sander introduced this model in 1981 as diffusion-limited aggregation (DLA): particles arrive slowly, by diffusion, and stick at once. The same arithmetic describes metal deposited from a solution, mineral dendrites on rock, and viscous fingers in a thin cell, because each grows by a diffusing quantity that obeys Laplace's equation.

Branches win over blobs because of screening. Let u(x) be the probability that a walker starting at x wanders back out to the launch circle before it touches the cluster. One step of the walk averages u over the four neighbors, so u satisfies the discrete Laplace equation, with u = 0 on the cluster and u = 1 on the circle. The chance that the next particle sticks at a perimeter site is set by how steeply u climbs away from it. At a tip the contours of u crowd together and the slope is large. Inside a fjord the surrounding branches have already absorbed the walkers, u is nearly zero, and almost nothing arrives.

With the overlay on, a handful of outer tips collect most of the walkers, and cells a few branch-widths inside a fjord receive almost none. Tips grow faster, so a small bump becomes a branch and the branch sprouts bumps of its own. This is the Mullins–Sekerka instability of a diffusion-limited front, the one that grows dendrites on a solidifying crystal. A lightning rod works the same way: a protruding conductor concentrates the field around it.

2. Stickiness and scale

Suppose a walker that touches the cluster sticks only with probability p and otherwise keeps walking. It gets several chances to slip past the outer tips before it commits, which weakens the screening that drives branching. Small clusters come out denser, with a measured dimension close to 2.

The effect has a length scale. A bouncing walker explores only a limited neighborhood before it sticks, and once the branches are much longer than that, the fjords between them are too deep to penetrate and screening returns. Low stickiness changes the texture at short range; far enough out the cluster crosses over to ordinary DLA.

3. Dropping the walkers: the dielectric breakdown model

The walkers can be dropped. Solve Laplace's equation for a potential φ with φ = 1 on the cluster and φ = 0 on a distant boundary, then add one perimeter site, chosen with probability proportional to the local field raised to a power, |∇φ|η. Niemeyer, Pietronero and Wiesmann proposed this dielectric breakdown model (DBM) in 1984 to describe the branched discharges, called Lichtenberg figures, that form when an insulator breaks down.

The exponent η tunes how strongly growth favors the most exposed tips. At η = 1 the clusters are statistically like DLA. At η = 0 the field is ignored and every perimeter site is equally likely; this is Eden growth, a compact blob with a rough edge. At large η nearly all growth goes to the strongest tip and the cluster becomes a needle. Lightning is often drawn with this model at η above 1, and it looks right, but a real stepped leader involves ionization and charge transport that the model leaves out.

4. Rivers: a different field, a similar tree

River networks look like these trees, and it is tempting to call a river a discharge run in reverse, collecting where lightning spreads. The stream-power landscape below tests how far that holds. Rain falls evenly, and each cell sends its water to its steepest downhill neighbor among the eight around it. The drainage area A of a cell counts the cells whose water passes through it, itself included. The ground rises at a uniform rate U, except along the bottom edge, held at sea level, and each cell is cut down at a rate that grows with the water it carries and the slope S to its downstream neighbor:

Closed depressions are filled before routing, as sediment would fill them, so every cell drains to the sea. The feedback has the same shape as in DLA. Where water collects, erosion is faster, which deepens the valley, steepens the slopes that lead into it and captures more area. A channel head collects more water than the hillslope around it, so channels extend headward the way DLA tips extend into empty space.

Unlike a DLA cluster, this landscape comes to rest. Uplift and erosion balance everywhere, and setting ∂h/∂t = 0 gives S = (U/K)A−1/2: slopes flatten downstream as area grows. The last readout compares simulated channel slopes with that prediction. From a fresh terrain it climbs from 0 to 1 over the first few hundred steps. Double U and it drops to 0.5, then recovers as the relief grows to twice its old height; after that the network changes only by occasional captures at the divides. A DLA cluster never settles, since each particle is frozen where it sticks.

The chart uses Strahler's ordering: channels with no tributaries are order 1, two streams of order ω join to make one of order ω + 1, and a smaller tributary does not raise the order. Horton noticed that the number of segments falls by a roughly constant factor per order, and that this bifurcation ratio Rb = Nω / Nω+1 sits between 3 and 5 for real rivers. Treating each particle as a node hanging from the neighbor it attached to, the DLA cluster and the discharge can be ordered the same way, and all three land near that range.

That agreement proves little. Kirchner showed in 1993 that almost any randomly branching tree has a bifurcation ratio near 4, so Horton's law cannot tell you which process built a network. The counts also depend on where a channel is declared to begin: the river counts only cells draining at least 20 cells, while every particle of the clusters counts.

What the two processes share is an instability: growth goes where a transported quantity (walkers in DLA, water in a landscape) is already concentrated, and the shape it builds decides where that quantity goes next. They differ in how the quantity is computed. In DLA it is a harmonic field, solved over all the empty space at once, so one tip can starve a fjord many branch-widths away. In the stream-power model it is drainage area, summed down the flow tree; a channel knows only what drains into it, and neighboring basins interact only by capturing cells at the divide. DLA grows at its tips and is frozen behind them, while a river network erodes everywhere at once and relaxes to a steady state. A DLA cluster is also a sparse fractal with D ≈ 1.71, while a drainage network reaches every cell of its basin.

For one kind of river the analogy becomes exact. Streams fed by groundwater seepage, like those in the sand of the Florida panhandle, grow at their heads in proportion to the groundwater flowing into them, and the water table obeys a Poisson equation. Devauchelle and colleagues showed in 2012 that this Laplacian growth predicts the angle at which such streams split, 2π/5 or 72°, and found that angle in the field. Rivers cut by water running over the surface, like the one simulated here, follow the stream-power law instead.
