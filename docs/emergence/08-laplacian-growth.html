Laplacian Growth

Random walkers that stick on contact grow fractal trees, because the outer tips catch most of the walkers. The same instability appears in electrical discharges and river networks.

Like the sandpile, these clusters are fed one particle at a time, but nothing relaxes: each particle freezes where it sticks, and the outer tips catch most of the newcomers. The same instability drives a model of electrical discharge; the last section asks how much of it carries over to rivers.

Witten and Sander introduced diffusion-limited aggregation (DLA) in 1981: release a particle far from a seed, let it walk at random until it touches the cluster and sticks, then release the next. Close relatives describe zinc leaves grown by electrodeposition (Matsushita and colleagues 1984) and viscous fingers in a thin cell (Nittmann, Daccord and Stanley 1985).

2. Where do the walkers land?

The cluster branches instead of filling in because of screening. The chance u(x) that a walker from x reaches the launch circle before touching the cluster is the average of u over the four neighbors, so u obeys the discrete Laplace equation, 0 on the cluster and 1 on the circle. Walkers arrive where its contours crowd, at the tips; the branches around a fjord catch its walkers first. Figure 2 freezes a cluster and counts where fired walkers would have stuck.

After 60,000 walkers, half have landed on 2.3% of the DLA cluster's 3,499 perimeter sites and 71% of the sites have caught none; the Eden blob needs 15% of its sites to catch half. Tips that catch more grow faster, so a bump becomes a branch that sprouts bumps of its own: the Mullins–Sekerka instability that grows dendrites on a solidifying crystal.

3. Stickiness and scale

Suppose a walker next to the cluster sticks only with probability p. It gets several chances to slip past the tips, which weakens screening and fills the cluster in, but only within the neighborhood it explores before sticking. Once branches are much longer than that, screening should return and the cluster cross over to ordinary DLA.

4. The dielectric breakdown model

The same growth can be computed without walkers. Solve Laplace's equation for a potential φ, 1 on the cluster and 0 on a distant boundary, and add one perimeter site with probability proportional to |∇φ|η. Niemeyer, Pietronero and Wiesmann proposed this dielectric breakdown model (DBM) in 1984 for branched surface discharges (Lichtenberg figures). At η = 1 a site grows in proportion to the field, as walkers arrive in DLA.

At η = 0 every perimeter site is equally likely: Eden growth, D = 1.99. At η = 1 the mean, 1.66, matches what Figure 1's clusters give from N(<r). Published values collected by Nicolás-Carlock and Carrillo-Estrada average 1.89, 1.70, 1.43, 1.26 and 1.11 at η = 0.5, 1, 2, 3 and 4; the eight runs here give 1.85, 1.66, 1.38, 1.15 and 1.11, scattering more at large η, where clusters hold only 130 to 180 particles.

5. River networks

River networks look like these trees. In the stream-power landscape below, rain falls evenly and each cell drains to its steepest downhill neighbor among eight; its drainage area A counts the cells whose water passes through it, itself included. The ground rises at rate U, except the bottom edge, held at sea level, and erodes at a rate that grows with the water and the slope S to the downstream cell:

Where water collects, erosion deepens the valley and steepens the slopes into it, capturing more area, so channel heads extend into the hillslope the way DLA tips extend into empty space.

Unlike DLA, this landscape reaches a steady state, S = (U/K)A−1/2, and the last readout climbs from 0 to 1 within about 200 steps. Double U and it drops to 0.5, then recovers within 200 steps while the relief grows 1.8 to 1.9 times.

In Strahler's ordering a channel with no tributaries is order 1, and two streams of order ω join to make order ω + 1. Horton noticed that segment counts fall by a roughly constant factor per order; on real rivers this bifurcation ratio is usually between 3 and 5, most often near 4. Ordered by attachment, the clusters land in or near that range too. Kirchner showed in 1993 that Horton's ratios describe virtually all possible branching networks, so they cannot tell which process built one.

Hack found that main-stream length grows faster than the square root of basin area, L ∼ Ah, so large basins are elongated; measured basins give h = 0.57 to 0.60 (Maritan and colleagues 1996, tabulated by Dodds and Rothman). The simulated river gives 0.59 to 0.68 over terrains 1 to 6 between steps 200 and 4,000, and DLA clusters from seeds 1 to 6 give 0.61 to 0.65: Hack's law cannot separate them either. The area distribution can. Real basins have P(A ≥ a) ∼ a−0.43; the river gives slopes of 0.39 to 0.50 and the cluster 0.59 to 0.65.

Both processes send growth where a transported quantity (walkers, field, water) is already concentrated. In DLA it is a harmonic field over all the empty space, so a tip can starve a fjord many branch-widths away; in the stream-power model it is area summed down the flow tree, and basins interact only at their divides.

For one kind of river the analogy is exact. Streams fed by groundwater seepage, as in the sand of the Florida panhandle, grow forward in the direction from which groundwater enters their tips, and the water table obeys a Poisson equation. Devauchelle and colleagues showed in 2012 that this Laplacian growth predicts the splitting angle 2π/5 = 72°, and found it in the field.

Every walker here may cross cells it has already visited. The next essay forbids that: a walk that never revisits a cell spreads faster and models a polymer chain.
