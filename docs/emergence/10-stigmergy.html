Stigmergy

Slime molds, ants, people on a lawn and termites with mud all coordinate through the same loop: write into the environment, let the writing fade and spread, steer toward where it is strongest.

In the 1950s Pierre-Paul Grassé watched termites rebuild a damaged nest and could find no sign that they coordinated with one another. Each termite responded to the construction in front of it, and whatever it added changed what the next one responded to. He called this stigmergy, from the Greek for mark and work: the work in progress directs the work.

The version that recurs across biology has three parts. Agents write into a shared field as they move. The field fades and spreads on its own. Agents read the field near them and steer toward more of it. For a concentration written by agents at positions ,

A slime mold as a crowd of particles

Physarum polycephalum is a single cell, often many centimetres across, that forages as a network of tubes. Cytoplasm shuttles back and forth through them; tubes that carry more flow thicken and the others shrink away. Jeff Jones (2010) reproduced the look of these networks with thousands of particles on a grid. Each particle samples the field at three points ahead of it (left, centre and right, at a fixed sensor distance and angle), rotates toward the strongest sample, and steps forward, depositing, if the cell ahead is empty; otherwise it stays put and picks a new heading. Every step the field is blurred by a 3×3 mean filter and multiplied by a decay factor.

The particles stand in for the flow of cytoplasm and the field for the record of where flow has been; neither is a piece of the real cell. The one-particle-per-cell rule matters: without it nothing stops the densest trail from capturing everyone, and the particles pile into a single thick band.

Within a few hundred steps the particles condense into a web of strands around empty cells. The sensor distance sets the mesh: a particle reacts to a strand only within about one sensor distance, so closer strands pull into one while farther ones keep their own traffic. Raise the sensor distance to 20 and the cells of the web roughly double in size. The decay factor sets the field's memory. At 0.99 each deposit lingers for about a hundred steps, and the strands grow thick and bright with past traffic. At 0.8 a mark is mostly gone within five steps, and the web still forms, but thin and faint, held up only by the particles passing now. With the turn angle at 15°, well below the sensor angle, particles see strands they cannot turn onto quickly, and the mesh becomes finer and ragged.

Real Physarum also prunes, which the particles are too crude to test. Nakagaki, Yamada and Tóth (2000) let pieces of a plasmodium fuse into one cell filling a plastic maze and put food at two exits. Over several hours the tubes in dead ends withdrew, leaving a tube along a shortest route between the food sources (or one of two, where the maze offered two of equal length).

Tero and colleagues (2010) then placed oat flakes at the positions of 36 cities around Tokyo on an agar plate shaped like the region, with bright light standing in for mountains and coastline, which the slime mold avoids. They compared the networks it formed after about a day with the rail network on total length, average travel distance between cities, and the chance of staying connected when a random link is cut. The slime mold came out comparable on all three, better on some and worse on others from plate to plate, and only some plates looked like the rail map. Their model is a flow network in which each tube's conductance grows with the flux through it and otherwise decays: the same reinforce-and-decay loop as the particles, in a different representation. The paper's claim is that a rule this simple finds networks in the efficiency range of engineered ones, not that the slime mold reproduced the Tokyo rail map.

Two bridges to the same food

Argentine ants (Linepithema humile) mark the ground with trail pheromone as they walk, in both directions. Jean-Louis Deneubourg, Simon Goss and colleagues connected a nest to food by a bridge that split into two branches. With branches of equal length, each colony ended up on one branch, chosen at random from run to run. With one branch twice as long, most colonies settled on the short one. With the long branch twice the short one and no evaporation, most colonies settle on the short branch in this model (batches of 20 typically give between 14 and 17); the rest lock onto the long one because it happened to pull ahead in the first few dozen choices. At a ratio of 1 the outcome is a coin flip, decided early and never revisited. The rule tilts the odds toward the short branch, and positive feedback turns the tilted coin into a near-unanimous decision, right or wrong.

Goss and colleagues also kept the short branch closed until the colony had settled on the long one. When they opened it, most colonies stayed put. Choose Opens at step 1500 to try it. With no evaporation no colony switches: the long branch by then holds far more than , and the few ants that try the short branch cannot catch up. Raising the evaporation sinks the steady trail on the long branch, of order , toward . Around traffic leaks back onto the short branch and a few colonies in twenty switch. Past about 0.06 the trails fade faster than the ants refresh them, and traffic splits roughly evenly whichever branch opened first. No evaporation rate makes the late colonies reliably find the short branch; the band between locked in and forgetful is narrow. Dorigo's ant colony optimization algorithms added evaporation to this rule for exactly that reason, so that a search over graphs can forget a bad early choice.

Paths across a lawn

With a durability of a few hundred steps, grass grows back almost as fast as it is trampled, the pull stays weak, and faint wear traces the six direct routes; walkers cover about 1.00 times the straight-line distance. At the default durability, wear outlasts the gaps between walkers. The diagonals bow outward toward the sides near the corners and cross the middle as a braided bundle, and the sides collect traffic from the diagonals. Walkers now travel roughly 10% farther than the straight line because they follow worn ground. Helbing and colleagues describe the result as a compromise between the direct routes, which minimize each walker's distance, and the shortest network joining the entrances, which minimizes trail length, and they compared such simulations with trails photographed on real lawns. With the attraction at zero, walkers ignore the ground and the wear traces the direct routes however long it lasts.

Mud that calls for more mud

Grassé's termites carry soil pellets. In Deneubourg's 1977 model of the first stage of termite building, fresh material carries a pheromone that attracts termites carrying pellets, so pellets pile where pellets already are. Figure 4 combines that idea with the pick-up and drop rules from Deneubourg's later model of ant brood sorting. Every pellet on the ground emits pheromone, which spreads and decays. A termite carrying a pellet drifts up the gradient and drops its load with probability ; an empty termite that walks over a pellet picks it up with probability . Here the structure writes the field and the agents write the structure.

At the default lifetime of 20 steps, the field on a lone pellet sits near and the field on a pile of ten is several times , so an empty termite is about six times as likely to pick up a lone pellet as one from a pile. Isolated pellets get carried off until the termite wanders near a pile, and within a few thousand steps nearly every pellet sits in one of a dozen or so piles. Past a lifetime of about 100 steps the background field of the scatter itself, , climbs above . Every cell then smells like a pile, termites drop pellets anywhere and rarely pick them up, and clustering stalls. With emission off, pellets are picked up and dropped at random and the scatter stays a scatter.

The cement pheromone in this model is a hypothesis that has held up poorly as chemistry. No one has isolated it from termite mud, and experiments on Macrotermes suggest that fresh soil, moisture and surface shape all influence where termites build. The model shows what a signal of this kind would be sufficient to do, and that it would work only within a limited range of lifetimes. Paper wasps come at it from the other side: they respond to the geometry of the cells already built, a discrete cue with no decay at all, and lattice models built on that cue alone (Theraulaz and Bonabeau 1995) grow recognizable nests.
