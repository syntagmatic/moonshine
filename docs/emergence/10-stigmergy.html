Stigmergy

Slime molds, ants, people on a lawn and termites coordinate through their surroundings rather than with each other: they leave marks that fade and spread, and steer toward the strongest.

The self-avoiding walk steers away from its own trail. The agents here steer toward trails, and in groups they build networks, pick the shorter of two routes and pile up pellets.

In the 1950s Pierre-Paul Grassé proposed that termites rebuilding a nest coordinate through the work itself: each addition changes what the next termite responds to. He called this stigmergy, from the Greek for mark and work.

The version that recurs across biology has three parts: agents write into a shared field as they move, the field fades and spreads, and agents steer toward more of it. For a concentration written by agents at ,

A mark can be read from about away, which sets how close two trails must be to merge.

A slime mold as a crowd of particles

Physarum polycephalum is a single cell that can spread across a dish many centimetres wide as a network of tubes; tubes that carry more protoplasmic flow thicken and the others decline (Tero and colleagues 2010). Jeff Jones (2010) reproduced these networks with particles on a grid. Each particle samples the field at three points ahead and turns toward the strongest; it steps forward and deposits if the cell ahead is empty, and otherwise picks a random heading. Each step the field is blurred over 3×3 cells and multiplied by a decay factor. Without the one-particle-per-cell rule the particles collapse into one or two dense bands.

The particles condense into a web of strands that keeps coarsening. A particle reacts to strands within about one sensor distance: at 20 the holes come out two to three times larger in area than at 9 after the same number of steps. At decay 0.99 a mark lasts about a hundred steps and strands grow thick; at 0.8 it fades within five and the web turns faint.

Tubes that grow with the flow they carry

The real cell also prunes. Nakagaki, Yamada and Tóth (2000) filled a maze with a plasmodium and put food at two exits; the dead-end tubes withdrew, leaving one along the shortest route. Tero, Kobayashi and Nakagaki (2007) modelled tubes carrying a fixed flow between the food sources. Dead ends carry no flow and fade within a few time units; loops take longer, because their two arms split the flow until one pulls ahead. At the network ends on the shortest route, as Bonifaci, Mehlhorn and Varma (2012) proved for any network whose shortest route is unique; 49 of 50 seeded mazes had settled by , the last (two near-equal routes) by 1214. Above 1 the network commits several times faster (median 8 time units at , 50 at 1), sometimes wrongly: at 2.5, 4 of 50 mazes kept a route up to 0.7% longer. At 0.5 it keeps about twice the shortest length, and only 30% of its tubes would cut the food sources apart if lost, against all of them on a single route.

Tero and colleagues (2010) found the same trade in the organism. Grown between food placed at 36 cities around Tokyo, its networks matched the rail network on average distance between cities and were slightly shorter, but the rail network was more robust: one broken link isolated part of it 4% of the time, against 14% for slime-mold networks grown with light standing in for mountains and lakes.

The double bridge

Argentine ants (Linepithema humile) lay trail pheromone in both directions. Simon Goss, Jean-Louis Deneubourg and colleagues (1989) connected a nest to food by a bridge that split in two, and within minutes one branch was clearly preferred. With equal branches either could win (the left won 12 of 26 trials); with one branch 1.4 times as long, the short one won 15 of 18, and at twice as long, 14 of 14. Termite building

Grassé's termites carry soil pellets. In Deneubourg's 1977 model, fresh material carries a pheromone that attracts termites carrying pellets. The cement pheromone itself has held up poorly: Green and colleagues (2017) found Macrotermes building organized around busy excavation sites, and Calovi and colleagues (2019) found soil moved according to surface curvature. Paper wasps respond to the geometry of cells already built, a cue that never decays, and lattice models on that cue alone (Theraulaz and Bonabeau 1995) grow recognizable nests.

Here the agents write a field and the field spreads. In the next essay the animals themselves spread, and that changes the boom and bust of predators and prey.
