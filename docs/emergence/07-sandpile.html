Sandpile

Drop grains of sand one at a time and the pile tunes itself to a critical state.

Drop grains of sand on a single spot. At first the pile just grows; once it gets steep, grains start to slide. A single grain can trigger anything from a trickle to an avalanche that rearranges the pile.

Bak, Tang, and Wiesenfeld (1987) turned this into a grid model that evolves to a critical state on its own, with avalanches at every scale and no parameter to adjust. They called this self-organized criticality.

The Abelian Sandpile

Each grid cell holds 0–3 grains. When a cell reaches 4, it topples: loses 4 grains, sends one to each of its four neighbors. Neighbors that reach 4 topple too. Grains off the edge are lost.

Click to drop a grain, or auto-drop. Early drops do nothing; cascades begin once most cells hold 2 or 3 grains. On this 100×100 grid it takes roughly 21,000 grains, an average of 2.1 per cell, before the pile stops filling up and settles into its stationary state. The model is called Abelian because the final state does not depend on drop order: dropping at A then B gives the same configuration as B then A. That commutativity is what gives the sandpile the group structure described below.

Avalanche Sizes

In the stationary state most drops trigger nothing, and occasionally one sets off a chain involving thousands of cells. Plot the probability of each avalanche size on log-log scales and the points fall on a straight line, a power law:

$$P(s) \sim s^{-\tau}$$

The same relationship covers small and giant avalanches, so there is no typical size. That absence of a scale is the signature of criticality.

The red dashed line is a least-squares fit over sizes 10 to 3,000, which leaves out the smallest sizes, where the lattice is visible, and the cutoff, where avalanches run into the edge of the grid. Avalanches are recorded only once the mean height passes 2.05, close to its stationary value of about 2.11 on this grid; the small avalanches of a pile still filling up would steepen the fit.

After a few minutes at full speed the fit on this grid settles near $\tau \approx 1.1$. The accepted value for the two-dimensional model is larger: Manna measured about 1.22 in 1991, Priezzhev, Ktitarev and Ivashkevich argued for exactly 6/5 in 1996, and Lübeck and Usadel found 1.29 on grids thousands of cells wide. The spread is a known feature of this model. The BTW sandpile does not obey simple finite-size scaling (Tebaldi, De Menech and Stella showed its avalanche statistics are multifractal), so the slope you fit depends on the grid and the fitting window. The same fit gives about 1.1 on a 100-wide grid and about 1.2 on a 60-wide one.

The Identity Element

Recurrent sandpile configurations form a group under cell-wise addition and relaxation. The identity is the unique configuration that, added to any recurrent configuration and relaxed, leaves it unchanged.

On a square grid the identity is mostly 2s and 3s, cut through by nested regions of 0s and 1s at many scales. Computing it takes two relaxations. Put 6 grains on every cell and relax. Subtract the result from 6, cell by cell, and relax again. What comes out is the identity: add it to itself, or to any recurrent pile, and relaxation returns the other summand unchanged.

Figure 2. The identity element of the sandpile group on a $48 \times 48$ grid, computed as $(6 - 6^{\circ})^{\circ}$, where $^{\circ}$ means relax. It has the four-fold symmetry of the square and nested, self-similar regions.

Larger grids add finer levels of nested detail. The pattern follows the shape of the boundary: circular boundaries produce circular identities, and triangular ones triangular.

Is It 1/f Noise?

Bak, Tang and Wiesenfeld introduced self-organized criticality as an explanation for 1/f noise, the flicker whose power falls off inversely with frequency and turns up in resistors, river levels and starlight. The sandpile has two time series whose spectra you could check.

The first is the avalanche size after each grain. It has almost no memory: each grain lands at a random site on a stationary pile, and the size of what it sets off depends on the configuration it happens to hit. Its spectrum is flat like white noise, apart from a slight rise at the lowest frequencies: a big avalanche drains grains from the pile, and the drops after it are a little quieter.

The second counts topplings per step of the avalanche's own clock. In each step every overloaded cell topples at once, and avalanches are laid end to end as the slow drive triggers them. This flow of sand has a power-law spectrum, but Jensen, Christensen and Fogedby (1989) showed that it falls as 1/f²: each avalanche is an isolated burst, and a sequence of uncorrelated bursts with a broad spread of durations gives 1/f² at frequencies above the inverse of the longest ones. Laurson, Alava and Zapperi (2005) later found the exponent somewhat below 2, equal to the exponent that relates an avalanche's size to its duration. On the 60-wide pile below the fitted slope comes out near −1.6, and it flattens out at the lowest frequencies, on time scales longer than the longest avalanches, where one burst tells you nothing about the next. Neither signal is 1/f.

Per Bak went on to argue that earthquakes, forest fires, market crashes and extinction events follow sandpile-like power laws, and that self-organized criticality is how much of nature operates. Whether or not that holds, the sandpile is the cleanest case: nobody picks a critical temperature or coupling, and the pile builds until it can avalanche and then stays there.
