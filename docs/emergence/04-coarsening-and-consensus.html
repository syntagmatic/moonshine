Coarsening and Consensus

Copy your neighbors and territories grow until one opinion holds the grid or the borders lock straight. Listen only to people who already agree with you and the population splits into a predictable number of camps.

Put a population on a grid and give each person one of two opinions at random. Each day someone looks at their four neighbors and adopts whatever most of them hold. Conformity to the people next door is the only force.

Random noise snaps into patches within a few rounds, and the patches then grow with their typical width rising as the square root of elapsed time. On a square grid that wraps around at the edges, about two runs in three end with one colour everywhere; the rest lock into straight bands that never move again. The second half of this essay makes opinions continuous and lets people ignore anyone too far from their own view. The population then settles into camps whose number you can predict from that tolerance.

1. The rule, and why the update order matters

The model is zero-temperature majority dynamics. Pick a cell at random. Count its four orthogonal neighbors. If three or four disagree with it, it switches. If exactly two disagree, it switches on a coin flip. One sweep is as many random picks as there are cells.

The obvious alternative updates every cell at once with a nine-cell vote over itself and its eight surrounding cells, which removes ties. Try both below. The synchronous vote smooths the noise and then stops with domains a few cells across: no cell on a surviving border is outvoted, and without ties nothing moves it. Under the random-order rule a cell at a step in a border faces a 2–2 tie, so steps wander along the border until they meet and annihilate, and curved borders keep retreating.

A cell on a convex bulge is outnumbered by the patch it pokes into and flips, so bulges retreat and small islands vanish. On a straight horizontal or vertical border no cell faces more than one disagreeing neighbor, so the border cannot move. That is where the frozen bands come from.

2. Domains grow as the square root of time

Measure the typical domain width $L$ as the number of cells divided by the number of neighboring pairs that disagree. A border moves at a speed set by its curvature, roughly $1/L$, and a domain of width $L$ disappears once its border has travelled a distance $L$. Time to erase domains of size $L$ therefore grows as $L^2$, which gives $L \propto t^{1/2}$. This is the Allen–Cahn law for curvature-driven coarsening, the same law that governs grain growth in annealed metals and domain growth in a quenched magnet.

Nothing in the rule mentions a length or a speed. The $t^{1/2}$ comes from border geometry alone, which is why the same exponent turns up in alloys and foams. Let cells occasionally flip against the majority and this becomes the Ising model, which below its critical temperature coarsens the same way. It has its own essay in the lattice simulation series.

3. Who wins, and how often nobody does

From an exact 50/50 start black and white win equally often. Spirin, Krapivsky and Redner found that on a periodic square grid a finite fraction of runs never reach consensus and freeze into straight stripes instead, and Barros, Krapivsky and Redner later tied that fraction to crossing probabilities in critical percolation, giving about 0.34. The sweep below measures it directly and then tilts the starting mix toward black.

At 50% the stripe fraction comes out near one third. A two-point bias makes black the likely winner, and more so on the larger grid: at 53% the 32-cell grid still gives white a few wins, while the 64-cell grid almost never does. The dynamics erase detail finer than the current domain width, so the winner is whichever colour owns the large-scale connected regions, and a small excess spread over a bigger area is a more reliable majority at that scale. Stripes thin out with bias because a stripe state needs both colours to span the grid.

4. Opinions on a continuum

Majority rule has two opinions, and every neighbor counts however much they disagree. The bounded-confidence model of Deffuant, Neau, Amblard and Weisbuch drops both assumptions. Each opinion is a number between 0 and 1, anyone can meet anyone, and when two people meet they move halfway toward each other only if their opinions differ by less than a tolerance $\varepsilon$. Otherwise they part unchanged.

A large $\varepsilon$ ends in a single consensus near the middle. With a small $\varepsilon$ opinions gather into clusters, and two clusters further apart than $\varepsilon$ never interact again, so the population freezes into several camps, each unanimous and deaf to the others.

From a uniform spread, each forming cluster captures everyone within about $\varepsilon$ on either side, so it owns a stretch of opinion roughly $2\varepsilon$ wide. That predicts about $1/(2\varepsilon)$ major clusters, the estimate Weisbuch and colleagues gave. The sweep below tests it.

The major-cluster count tracks $1/(2\varepsilon)$ closely from about $\varepsilon = 0.1$ upward and falls a little short below that, where the clusters near the two ends absorb less than their share. One major camp is typical once $\varepsilon$ passes about 0.3. The open dots stay above the filled ones well past that point, because a few people who start near 0 or 1 can miss the central cluster as it forms and stay stranded. Fortunato showed that on a complete graph every last person joins the consensus only once $\varepsilon$ exceeds 1/2.

The two models fail to agree for different reasons. Majority dynamics stops only when every border is straight, a geometric accident that grows rarer when one side starts with more territory. Bounded confidence stops because clusters more than $\varepsilon$ apart never interact, so their number is fixed by the tolerance and the width of the opinion range. In majority rule every neighboring pair of cells pulls toward agreement. The next essay keeps the lattice and makes some of those pairs pull toward disagreement.
