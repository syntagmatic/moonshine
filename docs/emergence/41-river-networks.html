River Networks

How erosion and feedback loops carve fractal drainage basins into the landscape.

Rivers are nature's arterial systems. From the air, they look like trees, with branches that split and merge at every scale. This branching pattern is a perfect example of a fractal: it remains self-similar whether you are looking at a single hillside or an entire continent.

The structure of a river network emerges from a simple feedback loop. Water flows downhill, carrying sediment with it and eroding the surface. This erosion deepens the channel, which in turn attracts more water flow. The more water a channel carries, the faster it erodes, creating a "rich-get-richer" dynamic that organizes the drainage basin into a hierarchical tree.

Horton's Laws

Geomorphologists use Strahler order to classify these branches. Headwater rills are order 1. When two order 1 streams meet, they form an order 2 stream. Robert Horton discovered that the ratios between stream counts, lengths, and areas are remarkably constant.

\[ R_b = \frac{N_\omega}{N_{\omega+1}} \]

The bifurcation ratio \(R_b\) typically stays between 3 and 5 for natural river systems, regardless of the terrain or climate.
