Epidemic Spreading

How a disease travels through contact networks, and why social structure is as important as the pathogen itself.

A disease rarely spreads through a population uniformly. Instead, it follows the specific contours of human contact. A coworker may transmit the flu, while a stranger across town remains unconnected. Consequently, the architecture of social networks, including who knows whom and how often they meet, determines the trajectory of an outbreak.

Network science allows us to model these dynamics with precision. Each person is represented as a node, and each contact as an edge. The disease "hops" from node to node across these edges. The central question for an epidemiologist is whether a single case will fizzle out or ignite a self-sustaining epidemic.

The SIR Model

The SIR model is a foundational framework that divides a population into three states. Susceptible individuals are healthy but can be infected. Infected individuals currently carry the disease and can transmit it. Recovered individuals are those who have survived the infection and gained immunity.

Network Topology

The same disease behaves differently on different networks. A random graph spreads infection at a steady rate. A small-world network lets the disease jump between distant clusters through "short-cut" edges. A scale-free network, where a few hubs have many connections, is especially vulnerable because those hubs act as superspreaders.
