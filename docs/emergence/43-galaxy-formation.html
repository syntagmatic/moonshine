Galaxy Formation

A cloud of particles collapses under gravity, and conservation of angular momentum flattens it into a rotating disk with spiral arms.

A diffuse cloud with a bit of rotation collapses under gravity, but angular momentum prevents collapse along the rotation plane. The cloud flattens into a disk, instabilities seed spiral density waves, and a galaxy takes shape — from gravity and spin alone.

All of this follows from Newton's inverse-square law applied to many particles. A few hundred particles in the browser are enough to watch it happen.

Gravitational Collapse

500 particles in a random cloud with slight rotation. Gravity is softened at close range to prevent numerical blow-ups. Particles along the spin axis fall toward the center; those in the rotation plane maintain their orbits. The cloud flattens into a disk.

Low spin gives a near-spherical collapse (an elliptical). High spin keeps the disk puffed out and slowly rotating.

Angular momentum is conserved only perpendicular to the spin axis, so gravity collapses freely along the axis but not in the rotation plane. A flat disk is the compromise.

Density Waves and Spiral Arms

Spiral arms are density waves — like traffic jams, where individual cars pass through but the jam itself moves slowly. Stars enter an arm, linger, and exit. The arms are patterns, not objects.

Galaxy Merger

Two approaching disks produce tidal forces that stretch stars into long tails and bridges. The same inverse-square law that built the disks builds the merger debris.

The longest, most graceful tails come from grazing encounters, not head-on strikes. The merged system rarely resembles either parent.

Figure 3. Two disk galaxies collide. Tidal forces stretch streams of stars into long tails and bridges. Alar Toomre and Juri Toomre's 1972 paper showed these structures emerge from pure Newtonian gravity applied to a few hundred test particles.

The Toomre brothers' 1972 "Galactic Bridges and Tails" used a few hundred test particles on early computers to reproduce the tidal tails seen in interacting galaxies like the Antennae and the Mice — structures that had puzzled astronomers for decades, all from Newtonian gravity.

The Milky Way will collide with Andromeda in about 4.5 billion years, eventually merging into a single elliptical sometimes called "Milkomeda." The physics is exactly what the simulation above shows.
