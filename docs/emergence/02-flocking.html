Flocking

Agents that each steer by their nearest neighbors can come to share one direction. Measuring that change as a transition shows that it depends on the agents moving.

The cells of the previous essay stay on a fixed grid; the agents here move through open space and steer by their neighbors. In 1987 Craig Reynolds simulated flocking with "boids", agents that each follow three steering rules based only on nearby agents. Watching the animation, it is hard to say when a scattered crowd has become a flock.

Boids

Each boid looks at the others within its vision radius and adds three steering terms. Separation pushes it away from neighbors that are too close, alignment turns it toward their average heading, and cohesion pulls it toward their average position. The boids fly at constant speed in a wrapping box, with a small random wobble in each turn. The readout under the canvas gives the polarization φ, the length of the boids' average heading: 1 when every boid points the same way, near 0 when headings are random.

Of the three rules, only alignment gives the boids a common direction. In three background runs of 1,500 steps from random starts, φ averaged … over the second half of each run with the default weights, and … with alignment set to zero. Drag alignment to zero and the readout follows. At the default weights the boids then stop gathering as well: in runs like these they stay about as spread out as a random scatter (1.7 neighbors within 25 units on average, against 1.4 for random positions), so here alignment is also what holds the flock together. Cohesion can gather them by itself if it is strong enough. Set alignment and separation to zero, cohesion to 2 and the vision radius to 100, and nearly all 150 boids pile into one swarm that churns in place with φ below 0.1.

That is a property of the rules, not of birds. Agents that only move toward the neighbors they detect while keeping a collision buffer, with no alignment term, can gather into a pile like that one, but they do not travel together; a common heading needs alignment.

Now restore alignment, set the vision radius to its smallest values and press Scatter. The boids condense into many small groups, each aligned inside but heading in different directions, so within-group φ runs well above the global φ. The radius sets how far heading information can reach in one step. Over time, groups that cross paths merge and settle on a common heading, and global φ rises slowly. (Shrink the radius without scattering and the flock splits into pieces that keep the heading they already shared, so global φ stays high.)

The Vicsek model

Boids have too many parameters for a clean experiment. In 1995 Tamás Vicsek and colleagues studied a model with alignment only. Every particle moves at the same speed v0. At each step it adopts the average heading of all particles within distance 1 of it (itself included), then adds a random angle drawn uniformly from [−η/2, η/2]. The noise η plays the role of temperature: at η = 2π the new heading is completely random.

Polarization against noise

To find where order is lost, Figure 3 runs the same code at 16 noise levels, each from a fresh random start, discarding the first 400 steps and averaging φ over the next 600. Error bars are the standard deviation of φ over those 600 steps: they show how much the order flickers, not uncertainty in the mean. The sweep runs in your browser while the figure is on screen.

 

Figure 3. Mean polarization against noise for moving particles (v0 = 0.3) and for frozen ones (v0 = 0), same density, radius and particle count as Figure 2. The vertical line is Figure 2's current noise; click the chart to move it there.

The moving particles order at low noise and disorder at high noise, and in this run φ crosses 0.5 at η ≈ …. The fluctuations peak near that crossing, where Figure 2's trace swung hardest. Both are what a phase transition looks like in a finite system, with φ as the order parameter and η as the control parameter.

A system of 400 particles is too small to tell what kind of transition this is. Vicsek's group reported a continuous one. Later simulations by Grégoire and Chaté (2004) on much larger systems found it discontinuous, and attributed the smooth curves to strong finite-size effects. Near threshold their ordered phase organizes into dense bands that travel through a sparse disordered background; the boxes where they show this are hundreds of interaction radii across (up to 1,024). A 20 × 20 box is far too small for bands, so the curve above is a rounded, finite-size version of the transition.

Why the flock has to move

The second curve in Figure 3 holds everything fixed except speed. With v0 = 0 the particles still align with their neighbors at the same noise, but stay where they started. Their best φ anywhere on the sweep was …, against … for the moving particles. Set Figure 2's speed to zero and noise to its lowest value, press Randomize, and the box fills with patches of uniform color that keep different headings.

The frozen particles fail because their neighbor network falls apart. At density 1 with radius 1, a particle has on average … neighbors, and in the frozen configurations this page generated the largest connected cluster held only … of the particles. Heading information cannot cross a gap between clusters, so each cluster picks its own direction. Moving particles leave their clusters and meet others, carrying their heading with them, so over time information can pass between any two particles.

The same mechanism matters even when the network is connected. In two dimensions, a static system whose parts try to align a continuous direction under noise cannot hold true long-range order (the Mermin-Wagner theorem): slow, long-wavelength twists of the heading field always win in a large enough system. John Toner and Yuhai Tu showed in 1995 that flocks are not bound by it. Moving agents carry heading information along their direction of travel, and that transport suppresses the twists, so a two-dimensional flock can be ordered in the thermodynamic limit where a static magnet with the same rule cannot. A 400-particle box cannot test that limit, and the frozen curve above fails for the simpler reason in the previous paragraph: its network is in pieces.

Real birds choose their neighbors differently. The boids and particles here count everything within a fixed distance as a neighbor. Ballerini and colleagues reconstructed starling flocks in three dimensions from stereo photographs and found each bird interacting with its six or seven nearest neighbors regardless of how far away they were. In their simulations a fixed-count rule held a group together far better than a fixed-radius one when its density changed. That is the weakness the frozen particles above showed: with a fixed radius, a sparse network breaks into pieces.
