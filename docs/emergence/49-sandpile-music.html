Sandpile Music

Listen to self-organized criticality. Each grain triggers a sound and avalanches become cascading rhythms.

The sandpile model produces avalanches with a power-law size distribution. Most drops trigger nothing or a small toppling. Occasionally one grain sets off a massive cascade. When you map avalanche sizes to pitch and duration, the sandpile's statistics become audible.

Small avalanches are quick, high-pitched clicks while large avalanches are deep rumbles that linger. The overall rhythm has no repeating pattern, but it has a recognizable texture. The power spectrum follows 1/f, which sits exactly between the randomness of white noise and the predictability of a periodic beat.

Notice the rhythm. In the early phase, when the grid is filling and most drops do nothing, the sound is a steady stream of high plinks. Once the pile reaches criticality and avalanches begin, the texture shifts. Silences between the deep tones are punctuated by clusters of rapid clicks. That is the audible signature of 1/f noise.

The Rhythm of Criticality

To confirm the 1/f character, we can record a sequence of avalanche sizes and look at the power spectrum. Click the button below to run 1,000 drops on a fresh pile, then view the resulting time series alongside its frequency spectrum.

Power spectrum. Log-log plot. A slope of −1 indicates 1/f noise.

The white noise comparison has a flat spectrum. The periodic signal has a single dominant peak. The sandpile's spectrum slopes downward at roughly −1, meaning low frequencies carry more power than high frequencies. This 1/f slope is the spectral fingerprint of self-organized criticality.
