Excitable Media

The same wave dynamics that create BZ spirals can cause lethal cardiac arrhythmias.

Heart muscle is an excitable medium. Each cell sits quietly at rest until a stimulus pushes it past a threshold, and then it fires. This is a rapid spike in voltage called the action potential. After firing, the cell enters a refractory period where it cannot fire again. Only after recovering does it become excitable once more. Resting, excited, refractory, resting. Three states, cycling in strict order.

Normally, a wave of excitation sweeps through the heart in a coordinated pulse, and the muscle contracts as a unit. But if a wave breaks, the broken end curls into a rotating spiral. That spiral fires the tissue faster than the natural pacemaker, overriding it. One spiral is ventricular tachycardia. Many spirals breaking apart is ventricular fibrillation.

Pulse propagation in 1D

Now line up a row of cells. Click one end to start a pulse. The excited cell triggers its neighbor, which triggers the next, and a wave rolls down the row. But the wave cannot reverse. Behind the wavefront is a trail of refractory cells that refuse to fire.

Normal Conduction

Spread the excitable cells across a 2D sheet and you have a simple model of heart tissue. A small pacemaker region in the top-left fires at a steady rate. Each firing launches a wave that sweeps across the tissue in a smooth front. Every cell fires once per heartbeat producing a coordinated contraction.

The pacing rate slider controls how often the pacemaker fires. If you pace faster than the refractory period allows, the tissue cannot keep up and some pulses fail to propagate.

Spiral Formation and Fibrillation

If a wavefront breaks, the broken end has nowhere to go but sideways. It curls around, re-entering tissue that has already recovered, and forms a self-sustaining rotating spiral. This fires faster than the natural pacemaker, taking over the rhythm.

Click "Create spiral" to break a wavefront. Then try reducing the refractory period with the slider. As cells recover faster, the spiral's wavefront becomes thinner. Eventually it breaks into multiple fragments and the tissue devolves into chaos. This is ventricular fibrillation.

A defibrillator does not restart the heart. It simultaneously depolarizes all cells, wiping out the chaotic spiral waves. With every cell in the same state at the same time, there are no gradients to sustain re-entry. The heart's natural pacemaker can then resume coordinated conduction.

The mathematics here is the same as in the Belousov-Zhabotinsky chemical reaction and in slime mold aggregation. Excitable media are everywhere in nature. The heart just happens to be the case where the spirals can kill you.
