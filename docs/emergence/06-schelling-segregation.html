Schelling's Segregation

How mild individual preferences for similar neighbors can lead to starkly segregated collective outcomes.

In 1971, economist Thomas Schelling introduced a simple thought experiment to explore the roots of social segregation. He envisioned a "checkerboard city" where two distinct groups live side by side. Each individual, or agent, possesses a modest preference: they wish for at least a small fraction of their immediate neighbors to be of the same group. This is not necessarily a demand for a majority; it is merely a desire for a minimal level of local similarity.

If this preference is not met, the agent moves to a nearby empty location. This is the entirety of the model. It contains no explicit hostility, no central coordination, and no restrictive zoning laws. Instead, it demonstrates how individual choices, which may seem reasonable in isolation, can aggregate into unexpected social patterns.

The Mechanics of Sorting

Consider a grid of 2,500 cells. Blue and orange agents are distributed randomly, with 10% of the cells remaining vacant. Each agent evaluates its eight immediate neighbors. If the fraction of similar neighbors falls below a tolerance threshold , the agent is "unhappy" and will relocate to a random empty cell.

Consider a tolerance of 50%. This implies that an agent is content if just half of its neighbors are like them. While this sounds reasonable, the resulting dynamics quickly divide the grid into solid, segregated blocks.

The Tipping Point

The relationship between individual tolerance and collective segregation is nonlinear. At 20%, mild clustering occurs. At 33%, the grid is visibly segregated. At 50% or above, the separation becomes nearly total.

Figure 2. Measuring the transition. The segregation index tracks the average homogeneity of each agent's local neighborhood. A sharp rise in this metric signifies the point where random distribution collapses into a sorted state.

Schelling's model applies wherever agents sort on any visible trait: language, income, or even seating at a cafeteria. The math does not care what the trait is.
