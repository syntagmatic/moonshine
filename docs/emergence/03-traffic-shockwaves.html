Phantom Jams

Traffic can jam on an open road with no accident or bottleneck, and the jam cuts throughput. A smooth car-following law and a four-rule automaton both reproduce it, and in both the jam moves backwards at a speed set by how quickly drivers pull away.

In 2008 Sugiyama and coauthors put 22 cars on a circular track 230 metres round and asked the drivers to cruise at about 30 km/h. Three minutes in, a cluster of five stopped cars was drifting backwards around the circle at roughly 20 km/h, while cars outside it moved at about 40 km/h. The track had no obstacle.

Each driver reacts only to the car ahead, like a boid in the flocking essay. Two models reproduce the jam: in the Intelligent Driver Model uniform flow is a solution but a small wobble grows, and in the Nagel-Schreckenberg automaton drivers slow down at random. In both the jam travels backwards and costs throughput.

On German motorways wide jams have kept their shape for about 50 minutes over 13 km (Kerner and Rehborn, 1996), and stop-and-go waves travel upstream at about 15 km/h (Treiber, Hennecke and Helbing, 2000), a speed set by how people pull away and queue.

The Nagel-Schreckenberg automaton

Without the slow car, uniform IDM flow would run forever. Nagel and Schreckenberg (1992) introduced a model that needs no disturbance: a ring of \(L\) cells holding at most one car each, integer speeds from 0 to \(v_{\max}\), and four rules applied to every car in parallel each tick, where \(d\) is the number of empty cells ahead:

  1. Accelerate: if \(v < v_{\max}\), set \(v \leftarrow v + 1\).
  2. Brake to the gap: if \(v > d\), set \(v \leftarrow d\).
  3. Dawdle: with probability \(p\), if \(v > 0\), set \(v \leftarrow v - 1\).
  4. Move: advance \(v\) cells.

With 7.5 m cells and 1 s ticks, \(v_{\max} = 5\) is 135 km/h. Rule 3 is the only randomness; Figure 5 runs the model.

At \(\rho = 0.15\) and \(p = 0.25\) jams appear and dissolve, all drifting left; at \(p = 0\) the same density flows freely once the start sorts itself out. The readout averages the last 100 ticks.

A free car averages \(v_{\max} - p\): rule 3 costs it a cell with probability \(p\) and rule 1 restores it. At \(p = 0\) every car keeps \(v_{\max}\) while \(\rho < 1/(v_{\max} + 1)\), above that the flow is \(J = 1 - \rho\), and jams are frozen leftovers of the start.

The front car of a jam waits \(1/(1 - p)\) ticks on average before rule 3 lets it leave, so the front ought to recede at \(1 - p\) cells per tick. Figure 6 measures it.

It recedes more slowly, because cars that have just left still dawdle and the ones behind catch up and stop again. The speed follows the outflow instead. A jam is a boundary between stopped cars and the thinner stream leaving its front, and because cars are conserved the boundary moves at the flow difference over the density difference. At \(p = 0.25\) the outflow is 0.51 cars per tick, the chord gives −0.58 cells per tick and the pattern moves at −0.59, or −16 km/h.

Inside the unstable band the IDM rings carry about 10 to 15 percent less than uniform flow would (less near the edges), and they lie on the jam line (Figure 7). A ring holding a jam and a free stretch mixes the two states, and as density changes the mix slides along the chord between them, so the congested branch's slope is the jam's speed. In the automaton, dawdling slows free cars and seeds jams whose outflow is 0.51 cars per tick, against 0.83 at \(p = 0\).

Slow to start

Real motorways show hysteresis: dense free flow can persist until something breaks it, and a jam's outflow is lower than the flow before it. The basic automaton has none: from evenly spaced cars or from one solid block it settles to the same flow at every density (within 0.006 on a 2,000-cell ring from \(\rho = 0.10\) to 0.18). Barlovic and coauthors (1998) got hysteresis by letting stopped cars dawdle with a higher probability \(p_0\) than moving ones.

In Figure 8, with \(p_0 = 0.75\), adding cars carries the flow up the free line to about 2,900 cars per hour at 22 cars/km, where cars can no longer keep five empty cells ahead; then a jam forms and the flow drops to about 720. Removing cars, the jam survives down to about 7 cars/km, near Barlovic and coauthors' estimate \(1/(1 + (v_{\max} - p)/(1 - p_0))\), 6.4 cars/km. The lower line's slope, \(-(1 - p_0)\) cells per tick, is the jam's speed: moving cars rarely dawdle here, so the jam stays compact and the simple front argument holds.
