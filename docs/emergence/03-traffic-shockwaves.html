Phantom Jams

Stop-and-go waves with no accident, no lane closure and no bottleneck, built twice: once from a smooth car-following law and once from a four-rule cellular automaton.

In 2008 a group of Japanese physicists put 22 cars on a circular track 230 metres round and asked the drivers to hold a steady 30 km/h. Within minutes a cluster of stopped cars had formed and was drifting backwards around the circle, against the direction of travel, while every individual car kept moving forwards. Nothing on the track caused it. The jam came from the drivers themselves.

This essay rebuilds that experiment twice. The first model treats each driver as a smooth controller that speeds up toward a target and brakes for the gap ahead. Its jams come from an instability: uniform flow is a solution, but a small wobble grows. The second model chops the road into cells and time into ticks, and its jams come from a coin flip that makes drivers occasionally slow down for no reason. Both produce the same backward-moving wave, and both pay for it in lost throughput, which we can measure.

A driver as a controller

The Intelligent Driver Model (Treiber, Hennecke and Helbing, 2000) gives each car an acceleration that depends on its own speed \(v\), the bumper-to-bumper gap \(s\) to the car ahead, and the approach rate \(\Delta v\) (how fast that gap is closing):

The first term pulls speed toward a desired \(v_0\). The second is a braking term that switches on when the actual gap is smaller than the gap the driver wants, \(s^*\), which grows with speed through the time headway \(T\) and grows further when closing in on a slower car. Here \(v_0 = 30\) m/s, \(T = 1.5\) s, a minimum gap \(s_0 = 2\) m, cars 5 m long, and comfortable braking \(b = 1.5\) m/s². The parameter left free is \(a\), the maximum acceleration, which sets how briskly a driver responds.

Put evenly spaced cars on a one-kilometre ring at the speed where the two terms balance, and nothing should happen. Every car is already at equilibrium. The figure below starts from exactly that state with one car running 1 m/s slow.

Because the ring conserves cars, the jam also has to take them from somewhere, which is why the free-flowing stretch between jams speeds up past the original equilibrium.

When a wobble grows

Whether the first wobble dies or grows is a question of linear stability, and it can be answered without running the simulation. Write the acceleration as \(f(s, v, \Delta v)\) and take its partial derivatives at the uniform state. A car's response to its leader acts like a filter: if it passes long-wavelength speed fluctuations with gain above one, each follower in the string amplifies what it received. The left side is how strongly a driver reacts to a change in gap. The right side is damping: \(f_v\) is the pull back toward the preferred speed and \(f_{\Delta v}\) the reaction to closing speed, both negative, so their product is positive. A driver who reacts sharply to the gap but corrects speed sluggishly fails the test. In the IDM, \(a\) scales every term of \(f\), so the left side grows like \(a\) and the right side like \(a^2\): brisk drivers are stable and sluggish ones are not.

Two things set the shape. At low density the equilibrium speed is near \(v_0\), where the free-road term dominates and the flow is stable at any \(a\). At the highest densities the equilibrium speed is small and changes little with the gap. In between is the unstable band, which widens as drivers become more sluggish. Standard highway values for the IDM put \(a\) near 1 m/s², close to the tip of the tongue, which is one reason the model is a good fit to real traffic: small parameter changes move a road in and out of the regime where waves form.

How fast the jam moves

Once the wave has formed, its speed hardly depends on how many cars are on the ring. Move the car slider in Figure 1 to anywhere inside the unstable band, and the readout settles to about the same value for a given \(a\): near −14 km/h at \(a = 0.8\), and near −10 km/h at \(a = 0.5\). The reason is in how a queue empties. The front of a jam recedes by one car each time a car pulls away, and a stopped car cannot pull away until the car ahead has opened a gap. Nothing in that expression refers to the traffic arriving from behind, which is why the wave speed is independent of density. The delay \(\tau\) depends on how the driver accelerates, which is why it does depend on \(a\). A measured −14 km/h with \(\ell = 7\) m means each car waits about 1.8 s after the car ahead has left.

On real motorways, wide moving jams have been tracked with loop detectors over tens of kilometres, and their downstream fronts travel upstream at about 15 km/h, in a range of roughly 10 to 20 km/h depending on the road and the mix of vehicles (Kerner and Rehborn, 1996; Treiber and Kesting, 2013). This is an empirical regularity of human driving, and the model reproduces its size once the reaction delay and queued car length are realistic. It is not a constant of nature.

A road made of cells

The IDM is deterministic. Without the initial wobble, uniform flow would run forever. Nagel and Schreckenberg (1992) found a much cruder model that produces jams from nothing. The road is a ring of \(L\) cells, each holding at most one car, and each car has an integer speed from 0 to \(v_{\max}\). Every tick, all cars apply the same four rules in parallel, where \(d\) is the number of empty cells in front:

  1. Accelerate: if \(v < v_{\max}\), set \(v \leftarrow v + 1\).
  2. Brake to the gap: if \(v > d\), set \(v \leftarrow d\).
  3. Dawdle: with probability \(p\), if \(v > 0\), set \(v \leftarrow v - 1\).
  4. Move: advance \(v\) cells.

The usual calibration makes a cell 7.5 m, the space a car takes in a queue, and a tick 1 s, so \(v_{\max} = 5\) is 135 km/h. Rule 3 is the only randomness, and it is where the jams come from.

Three results can be worked out by hand and checked against the simulation.

Free flow. A car with an empty road ahead sits at \(v_{\max}\) after rule 1, then drops to \(v_{\max} - 1\) with probability \(p\) in rule 3. On the next tick, rule 1 brings it straight back. So the speed it moves with is \(v_{\max}\) a fraction \(1 - p\) of the time and \(v_{\max} - 1\) a fraction \(p\), and the mean is \(\langle v \rangle = v_{\max} - p\). A single car simulated for 200,000 ticks gives 4.750 at \(p = 0.25\), against 4.75 predicted. At low density the free-flow branch of the fundamental diagram is therefore \(J = \rho\,(v_{\max} - p)\).

The deterministic limit. At \(p = 0\), speeds settle to \(v_{\max}\) for \(\rho\) below \(1/(v_{\max} + 1)\), when every car can keep \(v_{\max}\) empty cells in front of it. Above that density, in the steady state each car moves exactly its gap every tick, so the total distance moved per tick is the total number of empty cells, \(L - N\), and the flow is \(J = 1 - \rho\). The gaps themselves are not uniform: from a random start at \(\rho = 0.3\) they spread over every value from 0 to 5. Jams at \(p = 0\) are frozen leftovers of the initial condition; they never form spontaneously.

The jam front. The front car of a jam has speed 0. Rule 1 gives it speed 1, and rule 3 takes that away with probability \(p\), so it waits \(1/(1 - p)\) ticks on average before leaving, and the car behind cannot go until it has. The front therefore recedes at about \(1 - p\) cells per tick, the cellular version of \(\ell/\tau\) above. Tracking the front of a 200-car jam gives −0.74 cells per tick at \(p = 0.25\) and −0.48 at \(p = 0.5\). With the standard calibration, \(p = 0.25\) is 5.6 m/s, or −20 km/h, in the same range as the motorway measurements.

One feature of real traffic the basic automaton does not have is hysteresis. On motorways, flow can stay high in a dense free-flowing state until something breaks it, and once a jam forms, the outflow from it is lower than the flow that preceded it. In the Nagel-Schreckenberg model, starting from evenly spaced cars or from a single solid block gives the same long-run flow at every density: running both from \(\rho = 0.10\) to \(0.18\) at \(p = 0\) and \(p = 0.25\) on a 2000-cell ring, the two starts never differed by more than 0.006. Metastable states appear only after adding a slow-to-start rule, in which stopped cars dawdle with a higher probability than moving ones (Barlovic and coauthors, 1998).

What jams cost

The flow \(q\), in cars per hour past a fixed point, is density times mean speed. Plotting it against density gives the fundamental diagram, and both models make one. For the IDM there are two versions: the equilibrium curve, which is what uniform traffic at each density would carry, and what the ring actually carries once any wobble has had time to grow. For the automaton, the deterministic \(p = 0\) diagram is exact and the \(p = 0.25\) one is measured.

The shaded band on the density axis is the unstable range from Figure 2 at \(a = 0.8\). Nagel-Schreckenberg (orange), converted at 7.5 m per cell and 1 s per tick: the thin line is the exact \(p = 0\) diagram and the dots are 400-cell rings at \(p = 0.25\), 1000 ticks each after 500 ticks of warm-up.

Inside the unstable band, the IDM dots fall well below the equilibrium curve. Uniform flow at those densities would carry more cars per hour, but it is not what the ring settles into: the phantom jam is the steady state, and it carries less. Outside the band the dots sit on the curve, because the hard brake at the start simply decays.

The automaton's diagram shows the same loss by a different route. Every point at \(p = 0.25\) sits below the \(p = 0\) triangle, and the peak moves to lower density, because dawdling both slows free cars by \(p\) cells per tick and seeds jams that drain at only \(1 - p\) cars per tick. Neither model needs a bottleneck. In one, the jam is the unstable mode of a deterministic system; in the other, it is noise that the traffic cannot absorb once cars are close enough together.
