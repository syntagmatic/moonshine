Self-Avoiding Walks

A random walk that never revisits a site models a polymer chain. It spreads faster than an ordinary walk, by an exponent that has never been proved.

The walkers that built the clusters of the previous essay were ordinary random walks, free to cross their own paths. A self-avoiding walk (SAW) is a random walk on a grid that never visits the same cell twice.

It models a polymer chain: each step is a monomer, the walk is the backbone, and physical polymers cannot overlap themselves. SAWs expand faster than ordinary random walks. How much faster is known numerically to many digits in three dimensions and predicted exactly in two, but never proved. The figures below measure the exponent from sampled walks, and the last one follows walks grown one step at a time until they trap themselves.

2. Scaling Laws

A random walk of $N$ steps has end-to-end distance $\sim N^{0.5}$. In 2D, the SAW exponent is $\nu = 3/4$. Nienhuis predicted this in 1982 from a Coulomb gas argument that is not a proof, and it is still unproved. Lawler, Schramm and Werner later showed that if the walk has a conformally invariant scaling limit, that limit is SLE with $\kappa = 8/3$, which again gives 3/4. In 3D the best estimate, from pivot-algorithm simulations, is $\nu \approx 0.5876$ (Clisby and Dünweg 2016). Flory's 1949 argument, $\nu = 3/(d+2)$, gets close: 3/5 in 3D and exactly 3/4 in 2D, though the argument itself is far from a derivation.

On a log-log plot the slope is the exponent. The self-avoiding walks here are sampled with the pivot algorithm (Lal 1969; Madras and Sokal 1988): start from a straight rod, pick a site at random, rotate or reflect everything beyond it, and keep the result if it does not overlap itself. Repeated, this visits every $N$-step SAW with equal probability. Fitted over lengths 10 to 320 the slope comes out near 0.74, a little under 3/4 because the shortest walks have not yet settled into the asymptotic scaling; runs at 200 walks per length scatter between about 0.72 and 0.76. Growing a walk one random step at a time gives a biased sample. Among grown walks that survive to $N$ steps, compact shapes are over-represented, and fitted from 10 to 180 steps they give a slope near 0.65.

3. Trapped walks

A walk grown one step at a time, choosing at random among the unvisited neighbors, can get stuck, with all four neighbors visited and no move possible. On the square lattice every growing walk traps sooner or later. It cannot happen before step 7, and only about 1 walk in 70 traps within 10 steps, but by step 50 the risk has climbed to about 2% per step. Hemmer and Hemmer (1984) grew 60,000 such walks and found an average length of 70.7 steps, ending only about 12 lattice units from the start. In the 500 walks below the median lands in the 50s, below the mean because a long tail of survivors pulls the mean up, and roughly one walk in five reaches 100 steps.

The 3D exponent is the one Flory was after: it sets how the size of a flexible polymer in a good solvent grows with chain length. There is also no formula for the number of $N$-step SAWs on the square lattice. It grows roughly as $\mu^N$, and the growth rate $\mu$ is known only numerically, $\mu \approx 2.638158530$ (Jacobsen, Scullard and Guttmann 2016). On the honeycomb lattice it is exactly $\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2}}$, which Nienhuis predicted in 1982 and Duminil-Copin and Smirnov proved in 2012.

A self-avoiding walk steers by the trail it has left, stepping only onto cells it has not visited. The agents in the next essay also steer by trails, their own and each other's, but they turn toward the strongest marks, and the marks fade and spread.
