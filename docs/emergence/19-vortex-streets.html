Vortex Streets

An object in a flow creates alternating vortices that shed in a perfectly regular, metronomic pattern.

Hold a stick in a stream and look at the water behind it. You will see swirls peeling off alternately from one side, then the other, drifting downstream in a staggered zigzag. This is a von Kármán vortex street. It was first described mathematically by Theodore von Kármán in 1911, but the pattern itself is older than any human observer. Satellite photos show vortex streets stretching for hundreds of kilometers behind islands in the ocean.

The pattern is not designed. It emerges. A uniform flow hits a blunt obstacle, the boundary layers on each side become unstable, and the instability self-organizes into a periodic alternating shedding. One vortex growing on one side feeds the detachment of a vortex on the other. The result is a metronomic rhythm that depends only on the flow speed, the obstacle size, and the viscosity of the fluid.

A Lattice Boltzmann method computes fluid flow around a circular obstacle. At low speeds, the flow wraps smoothly around the cylinder. As the speed rises, vortices begin to shed, one from the top, then one from the bottom, forming the characteristic staggered street downstream.

2. The Reynolds Number

Whether the flow is smooth or chaotic depends on a single dimensionless quantity: the Reynolds number $Re = \rho v L / \mu$, where $\rho$ is fluid density, $v$ is flow speed, $L$ is obstacle diameter, and $\mu$ is dynamic viscosity. It represents the ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces.

At low Re the viscous forces win. The flow is laminar, meaning smooth and predictable. As Re increases, inertia begins to dominate. The boundary layer separates from the obstacle and curls into vortices. In a narrow band of Re, roughly 50 to 300, the shedding is remarkably periodic: a clean vortex street. Push Re higher still and the wake becomes turbulent, and the regularity breaks down into chaos.

3. Obstacle Shape

A cylinder is the classic case, but any blunt body placed in a flow will shed vortices. The shape of the obstacle changes the details: the width of the wake, the frequency of shedding, and how cleanly the vortices detach. Engineers characterize the shedding frequency with the Strouhal number:

$$ St = \frac{f L}{v} $$

where $f$ is the shedding frequency, $L$ is the obstacle width, and $v$ is the flow speed. For a cylinder, St sits close to 0.2 across a wide range of Reynolds numbers.

The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed in 1940 partly because wind-driven vortex shedding matched the bridge's natural frequency. Tall chimneys often have helical strakes wrapped around them to break up the regular shedding pattern and prevent destructive oscillation.
