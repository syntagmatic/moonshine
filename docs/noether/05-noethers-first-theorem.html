Noether's First Theorem, in Pictures

Part 5 of 15. Every continuous symmetry of the action gives you a conservation law, automatically, and in five lines of calculus.

This is the moment the first half of the series has been building toward. Given a Lagrangian and a continuous one-parameter symmetry , there is a quantity

that is constant along every solution of the equations of motion. The quantity is called a Noether current or a conserved charge, and the fact that it exists is Noether's first theorem. The theorem occupies the first half of her 1918 paper Invariante Variationsprobleme, where she proved it at a level of generality that covered every Lagrangian physicist was studying at the time, including the new general relativity.

Noether's theorem is not an approximation. On an exact solution of the Euler-Lagrange equations, the conserved current is exactly constant, not approximately or asymptotically. What the workshop's small numerical drift reflects is the RK4 integrator's finite step size, not any property of the theorem itself.
