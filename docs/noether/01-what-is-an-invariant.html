What Is an Invariant?

Part 1 of 15. A group acts, some things move, and the ones that don't are the start of everything.

This series is about invariance — the idea that something can be transformed and still come out recognizable. A circle stays a circle when you rotate it. The sum stays the same when you shuffle the order of the three numbers. Neither observation is deep on its own, but together they point at the principle that sits under nearly all of Emmy Noether's work: to understand what's going on in a system, ask what stays fixed when you act on it.

Before we can use invariance as a working tool, we need the vocabulary for what exactly stays fixed, what is doing the changing, and how the two relate. This first explainer fixes three ideas: group action, orbit, and invariant function. Everything else in the series is an amplification of these three.

Rotation, and what survives it

Rotate the plane by an angle around the origin. A point at moves to . Most quantities you could name — the x-coordinate, the y-coordinate, their sum — change with . A few do not. The distance from the origin is unchanged. The sum of squares is unchanged. These are invariant under rotation.

What makes them invariant is that every rotation is reversible (undo it with the opposite rotation), compositions of rotations are still rotations, and the identity "rotate by 0" is also a rotation. The set of rotations of the plane is closed under composition and inversion; it forms a group. The invariants of a group are the quantities the whole group leaves alone, not just some members.

Two observations. First, which quantities are invariant depends on the group. With the full rotation group, only radial quantities survive — anything you could compute from the distance alone. With a smaller group (say, only rotations by multiples of 90°), more quantities are invariant, because there are fewer transformations to survive. Second, invariance is always a property of a function-and-group pair. It is meaningless to ask whether "x is invariant" without naming what it is invariant under.

Symmetries of a square

The rotation group of the plane is infinite; most of the groups Noether cared about are not. The smallest non-trivial finite group we will meet in this series is , the group of symmetries of a square. There are exactly eight symmetries: the identity, three rotations (by 90°, 180°, 270°), and four reflections — two across the horizontal and vertical axes and two across the diagonals.

The word "symmetry" here is strong. A symmetry of the square is a rigid motion of the plane that carries the square exactly onto itself as a set. Among all possible rigid motions of the plane, exactly eight accomplish this. The corners may get shuffled, but the square as a shape is unchanged.

Invariance is about the whole group, not a single transformation. A function is only called invariant under if it survives all eight elements, not merely two or three. This is why the question "is this quantity invariant" always needs "under what group" attached to it.

Functions that don't care about order

A different kind of example with nothing to do with geometry: take three real numbers and consider a function of them. Some functions — the sum, the product, the sum of squares — do not care which number is labeled first. Shuffle the inputs and the output is unchanged. Others — the first coordinate, the difference of the first two, the max minus the first — single out particular inputs and jump when you shuffle.

The shuffles form a group: the symmetric group of order six. The functions that don't care about shuffles are the symmetric functions of .

Two senses of invariance

When we say something is invariant under a transformation, we can mean two different things, and keeping them straight matters.

The circle in Figure 1 is a fixed object under rotation: rotating the plane sends the circle to itself. The distance from the origin is an invariant function: it gives the same answer before and after the rotation. Both are invariants, but they are statements about different things, and the series will use both senses.
