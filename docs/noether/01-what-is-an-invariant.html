What Is an Invariant?

Part 1 of 13. A group acts, some things move, and the ones that don't move are what the rest of the series studies.

This series is about invariance, the idea that something can be transformed and still come out recognizable. A circle stays a circle when you rotate it. The sum stays the same when you shuffle the order of the three numbers. Neither observation is deep, but together they point at the principle under most of Emmy Noether's work: to understand a system, ask what stays fixed when you act on it.

To use that principle we need words for what stays fixed, what does the changing, and how the two relate. This first article sets up group actions, orbits and invariant functions, which the rest of the series builds on.

Rotation, and what survives it

Rotate the plane by an angle around the origin. A point at moves to . Most quantities you could name (the x-coordinate, the y-coordinate, their sum) change with . A few do not. The distance from the origin is unchanged. The sum of squares is unchanged. These are invariant under rotation.

Every rotation can be undone by the opposite rotation, two rotations compose to a rotation, and "rotate by 0" is a rotation too. So the rotations of the plane form a group. An invariant of the group is a quantity that every member of the group leaves alone.

Which quantities are invariant depends on the group. Under the full rotation group only radial quantities survive, the ones computable from the distance alone. Under a smaller group, say rotations by multiples of 90°, more quantities are invariant because there are fewer transformations to survive. So invariance belongs to a function and a group together. Asking whether "x is invariant" means nothing until you say under what.

Symmetries of a square

The rotation group of the plane is infinite; most of the groups Noether cared about are not. The smallest non-trivial finite group in this series is , the group of symmetries of a square. There are exactly eight symmetries: the identity, three rotations (by 90°, 180°, 270°), and four reflections, two across the horizontal and vertical axes and two across the diagonals.

A symmetry of the square here means a rigid motion of the plane that carries the square exactly onto itself as a set. The corners may get shuffled, but the shape does not move.

Functions that don't care about order

Now an example with no geometry in it. Take three real numbers and consider a function of them. Some functions (the sum, the product, the sum of squares) do not care which number is labeled first. Shuffle the inputs and the output is unchanged. Others (the first coordinate, the difference of the first two, the max minus the first) single out particular inputs and jump when you shuffle.

Two senses of invariance

The phrase invariant under a transformation can mean two different things.

  2. An object is invariant (more precisely, fixed) if for every group element .

The circle in Figure 1 is a fixed object under rotation: rotating the plane sends the circle to itself. The distance from the origin is an invariant function: it gives the same answer before and after the rotation. The series uses both senses.
