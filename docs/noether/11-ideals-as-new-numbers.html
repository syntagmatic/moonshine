Ideals as the New Numbers

Part 11 of 15. Act III begins. Unique factorization fails in , and Noether's intellectual ancestors (Kummer, Dedekind) rescue it by a move no one expected: stop factoring numbers and start factoring ideals.

The first two acts lived inside invariant theory and classical mechanics. Act III crosses into abstract algebra, where Noether did her second revolution. The launching point is a disaster in the smallest interesting ring after the integers, and the rescue operation is one of the most striking conceptual moves in nineteenth-century mathematics: when a property fails for the things you have, change what counts as a thing.

Dedekind's idea: factor ideals, not elements

Ernst Kummer in 1847, trying to prove Fermat's Last Theorem for cyclotomic fields, realized unique factorization failed in rings like and invented ideal numbers, new "divisors" that lived outside the ring and restored unique factorization as long as you included them. Richard Dedekind in 1871 cleaned up the idea: instead of phantom ideal numbers, take ideals, which are specific subsets of the ring, and factor them.

The failure of the element-level version is encoded cleanly by the class group, a finite abelian group that measures how far the ring is from being a unique factorization domain.

The road to Noether

Each step in the history of this idea is a step toward more abstraction and more generality.

1847
Kummer
"Ideal numbers" added to cyclotomic fields to restore unique factorization. Phantoms, not subsets, conceptually opaque but effective for Fermat's Last Theorem in regular prime cases.
1871
Dedekind
"Ideals" reformulated as subsets of the ring closed under internal operations. No more phantom objects; every ideal is a genuine subset of the ring. Unique factorization into prime ideals proved for rings of algebraic integers.
1921
Noether
Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen. Drop specifics (not just rings of integers; any commutative ring with a finiteness condition) and gain complete generality. The chain condition is the only axiom needed, and the entire Dedekind-ideal story falls out as a special case.

Each generation in this timeline loses a specific feature of the previous generation's setup (cyclotomic integers, then rings of algebraic integers, then any commutative ring) and gains applicability across all the cases the lost features were obscuring. Noether's move in 1921 is the most radical of the three, she is willing to work in any ring at all, provided it satisfies a one-line finiteness condition, and it is the move that makes commutative algebra, algebraic geometry, and a great deal of modern mathematics possible in their current form.
