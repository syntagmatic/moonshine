Noether's Second Theorem

Part 10 of 15. Local symmetries don't give you more conservation laws, they give you identities between the equations of motion. And it is why energy in general relativity is more complicated than you remember.

Noether's 1918 paper contains two theorems. The first, which the last five explainers have been running through its paces, says every continuous global symmetry of an action gives a conserved current. The second says what happens when the symmetry is local (parameterized by a function of position, not a constant) and the answer is subtler: you don't get a new conservation law, you get a differential identity that the equations of motion satisfy automatically. And that identity is what makes gauge theory gauge theory, and what makes energy in general relativity the delicate, chart-dependent thing that it is.

Global versus local, again

Explainer 9 ended on the distinction. A global symmetry has a single constant parameter: rotate every wave function by the same phase, or shift every coordinate by the same vector. A local symmetry has a parameter that depends on position: rotate the phase by a different amount at each point.

The subtle payoff is about energy. In any theory with a global time-translation symmetry, the first theorem gives you an energy that is conserved. General relativity's time-translation symmetry is local, the time coordinate is just one component of a diffeomorphism, and the second theorem says the matter energy-momentum tensor satisfies , the covariant divergence, not the partial one. There is, in general, no tensorial global energy in GR; only for spacetimes with an additional genuine Killing vector (an isometry) do you recover a conserved scalar energy. Hilbert and Klein found this perplexing in 1917. Noether, invited to Göttingen specifically to sort it out, wrote the second theorem and settled the matter.

1917–1918, Göttingen. Hilbert was finishing his 1915 derivation of the field equations from the variational principle and had noticed that "energy conservation" in GR was not looking like conservation in any other field theory he knew. Klein, trying to explain the puzzle, corresponded with Hilbert through 1917; both agreed the answer would require a more general version of the variational calculus. They invited Noether, who had been barred from a formal Privatdozent appointment because of her gender, and was teaching under Hilbert's name, to work on it. The result, Invariante Variationsprobleme (Nachr. Ges. Wiss. Göttingen, 1918), contains both theorems. The second theorem is explicitly the answer to Klein and Hilbert's question: "local" symmetries don't give you a global energy, they give you Bianchi identities, and that is not a bug of general relativity but a feature of any theory with a local symmetry.

First vs. second, at a glance

First theorem

  • Parameter: constant (global).
  • Output: conserved current on shell.
  • Examples: time → energy, rotation → angular momentum, global → electric charge.
  • Strength: holds only on solutions of the Euler-Lagrange equations.
  • Used: every derivation in Act II so far.

Second theorem

  • Parameter: function (local / gauge).
  • Output: differential identity satisfied by the Euler-Lagrange equations themselves.
  • Examples: local → ; diffeomorphism → .
  • Strength: holds off shell, as an identity on every field configuration.
  • Consequence: gauge redundancy, and no global energy in GR.

The most common misreading of Noether's theorem is that every gauge symmetry gives you a new conservation law. It does not. Global symmetries give conservation laws (first theorem); local symmetries give identities between the equations of motion (second theorem), and those identities manifest as gauge redundancy and as constraints on what can count as "energy" in the presence of a gravitational field. Two theorems, two different things, both in the same 1918 paper.
