Noetherian Rings

Part 12 of 15. One finiteness condition, every ascending chain of ideals eventually stops. It is the single axiom Noether added in 1921, and nearly all of modern commutative algebra runs on it.

A commutative ring satisfying this ascending chain condition (ACC) is called Noetherian. That is the whole definition, stated without reference to any particular kind of element, field, or geometric setting. Three equivalent conditions

The ascending chain condition is equivalent to two other finiteness statements about ideals, and most expositions move between them freely.

(a)
ACC. Every ascending chain of ideals satisfies for some .
(b)
Finite generation. Every ideal is finitely generated: for some finite list of elements.
(c)
Maximal principle. Every nonempty collection of ideals has a maximal element (with respect to inclusion).

Why (a) ⇔ (b). If every chain stabilizes, then an ideal that needs infinitely many generators would produce a strict chain: pick , then , then , and so on. The chain is strictly ascending because each new generator is not already captured, so it never stabilizes, contradicting (a). Conversely if every ideal is finitely generated, then the union of an ascending chain is an ideal, finitely generated by some , each of which appears in some finite step of the chain, so the chain stabilizes by the maximum of those steps.

Why (a) ⇔ (c). Given a nonempty family with no maximal element, you can pick an ideal, then strictly enlarge it, then strictly enlarge that, and so on, the result is an infinite ascending chain that never stabilizes. Conversely a strictly ascending chain is a family of ideals with no maximal element.

The three formulations say the same thing in different moods: "chains terminate" (dynamical), "ideals are finitely generated" (structural), "maximal elements exist" (existential). Each suits a different kind of proof. Chains are useful when you are building something by induction and want to know the induction stops. Finite generation is useful when you want to manipulate an ideal by manipulating a finite list of its generators. Maximal elements are useful when you want to pick the "largest" ideal satisfying some property and argue about its complement, a technique we'll use in the proof of Hilbert's basis theorem next explainer.
