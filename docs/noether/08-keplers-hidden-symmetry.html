Kepler's Hidden Symmetry

Part 8 of 15. The inverse-square force has a conservation law nobody predicted from rotations alone — and it points to a symmetry group that's one dimension larger than physics expected.

The Kepler problem — a single particle attracted to a fixed center by an inverse-square force — is the most important toy system in the history of physics. It gave Newton the moon's orbit and Kepler his three laws, and in 1926 it gave Pauli the first quantum-mechanical derivation of the hydrogen spectrum. The feature that made Pauli's derivation possible is a conservation law we did not meet in the last explainer, because it doesn't come from any rotation or translation. It is the Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector, and its existence says the Kepler problem has a larger symmetry group than you'd guess.

Not a point-transformation current

A warning is due. In explainer 5 we proved the first theorem for a symmetry that acts on the configuration variable alone: . The Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector does not come from a symmetry of that shape. It is the Noether current of a symmetry that acts on phase space — on both and — simultaneously, and in a way that depends on the velocity. Noether's theorem does extend to such symmetries (her 1918 paper was written for the fully general setting, including these), but the formula doesn't apply unmodified.

The practical consequence: you can't read LRL off the Lagrangian by staring at it the way you can read momentum or angular momentum. You can verify it is conserved by computing using the equations of motion, and that is what the animation does on every frame. Break the symmetry: add a 1/r³ correction

The LRL vector is fragile in a specific way: it is conserved for the pure inverse-square force and for essentially nothing else. The simplest perturbation that breaks it is a small term, which happens to be the leading general-relativistic correction to Newtonian gravity near a massive body. The orbit's perihelion precesses, and the LRL vector precesses with it. Pull the slider and watch.

The lesson of the Kepler problem for Noether's theorem is that "symmetry" needs to be read generously. Every one of the physicists who tried to axiomatize classical mechanics in the nineteenth century missed LRL, because they were looking for symmetries that act on positions alone. Noether's theorem, stated at its full 1918 generality, handles phase-space symmetries as naturally as point transformations — which is why her framework, and not any of the earlier attempts, became the canonical one.

What we take with us

  • The inverse-square force has a conservation law — the Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector — that doesn't come from any rotation or translation.
  • It comes from a phase-space symmetry, which Noether's framework handles but which the configuration-space formula of explainer 5 misses.
  • The full symmetry group of bound Kepler orbits is , six-dimensional, not three-dimensional.
  • That extra dimension is what makes hydrogen's degeneracy look "accidental" until you see the symmetry it comes from.
  • Break the inverse-square force with a term and the perihelion precesses — exactly the kind of effect that distinguishes Newtonian gravity from general relativity.
