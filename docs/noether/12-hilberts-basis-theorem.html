Gordan's objection

Hilbert's 1890 paper used the theorem to prove that the invariants of forms in any number of variables are finitely generated. Paul Gordan had proved the binary case in 1868 by constructing the generators, and his school had spent two decades pushing such constructions further. Hilbert's argument produced no formulas and no bounds. Gordan is reported (in Constance Reid's 1970 biography of Hilbert, and earlier accounts) to have said "Das ist nicht Mathematik, das ist Theologie": this is not mathematics, this is theology. Hilbert gave a more constructive treatment within a few years.

Noether's 1907 dissertation under Gordan was the kind of explicit calculation he favoured. In 1916 she proved the kind of bound Hilbert's proof lacked, for finite groups: in characteristic zero the invariants of a finite group acting linearly are generated in degree at most . Her 1921 Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen recast the basis theorem as the chain-condition argument above.

Gröbner bases

For polynomial ideals a constructive counterpart came later. In 1965 Bruno Buchberger gave an algorithm that turns any finite list of polynomials into a Gröbner basis, a generating set whose leading terms generate the ideal of all leading terms. It divides the S-polynomial of each pair (the combination that cancels their leading terms) by the current basis. A nonzero remainder has a leading term no current one divides, so adding it strictly enlarges the leading-term ideal. That chain of monomial ideals must stop, so the algorithm halts.

When every pair reduces to 0 the monomials left outside (green) form a basis of . Switch the monomial order: the basis and the staircase change, the number of monomials outside does not.

What a Gröbner basis buys is division. By an arbitrary generating set, a member of the ideal can leave a nonzero remainder that depends on the order of the divisors. By a Gröbner basis the remainder is the same in any order, and 0 exactly for members.
