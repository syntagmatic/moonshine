Gordan's lament

Hilbert's 1890 paper proved the basis theorem (in a slightly different form, framed in invariant theory) and deduced from it that the invariants of forms in any number of variables are finitely generated. Paul Gordan, Noether's doctoral advisor, had proved the binary case in 1868 with an explicit construction that produced the generators, and his school had spent the following decades trying to push such constructions to forms in more variables. Hilbert's proof produced no formulas and no bounds. It showed only that a finite generating set existed.

Gordan is reported (in Constance Reid's 1970 biography of Hilbert, and earlier accounts) to have said:

"Das ist nicht Mathematik, das ist Theologie."
"This is not mathematics, this is theology."

The complaint was about non-constructivity. Gordan's school had been computing invariants by hand for decades, and Hilbert settled their central question without computing anything. The community was slow to accept it. Within a few years, however, Hilbert had also given an alternative, more constructive treatment, and by 1900 the basis theorem was a foundational result.

Emmy Noether's 1907 dissertation, written under Gordan, was exactly the kind of explicit calculation Gordan favored. She spent the rest of her career developing the abstract approach Hilbert had started. Her 1921 Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen recast the basis theorem in terms of the chain condition, the form given above.

From existence to an algorithm

Gordan's complaint eventually got a complete answer. In 1965 Bruno Buchberger gave an algorithm that takes any finite list of polynomials and returns a Gröbner basis of the ideal they generate: a generating set whose leading terms generate the ideal of all leading terms. With one in hand, deciding whether a polynomial lies in the ideal is a division with remainder.

The algorithm takes a pair of basis elements, forms their S-polynomial (the combination that cancels their leading terms), and divides it by the current basis. A nonzero remainder has a leading term that no current leading term divides, so adding it to the basis strictly enlarges the ideal generated by leading terms. Those ideals form an ascending chain in , which must stop, so the algorithm halts. The chain condition that made Hilbert's proof non-constructive is the same one that guarantees Buchberger's algorithm terminates.
