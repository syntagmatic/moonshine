Kepler's Hidden Symmetry

Part 7 of 13. The inverse-square force has a conservation law that rotations do not explain, and it points to a symmetry group larger than the rotation group.

The Kepler problem, a single particle attracted to a fixed center by an inverse-square force, may be the most studied toy system in physics. Newton derived Kepler's three laws from it, and in 1926 Pauli used it for the first quantum-mechanical derivation of the hydrogen spectrum. Pauli's derivation rests on a conservation law that does not come from any rotation or translation, the Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector. Its existence says the Kepler problem has a larger symmetry group than you would guess.

Not a point-transformation current

In Part 5 the first theorem was proved for a symmetry that acts on the configuration variable alone, . The Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector does not come from a symmetry of that shape. It is the Noether current of a symmetry that acts on phase space, moving and together in a way that depends on the velocity. Noether's 1918 paper covers such symmetries, but the formula doesn't apply unmodified.

So you can't read the vector off the Lagrangian the way you can read momentum or angular momentum. You can check that it is conserved by computing with the equations of motion, which is what the animation does on every frame. Break the symmetry: add a 1/r³ correction

The LRL vector is conserved for the pure inverse-square force and breaks under almost any change to it. A simple perturbation is a small term in the potential, the form of the leading general-relativistic correction to Newtonian gravity. The orbit's perihelion precesses, and the LRL vector precesses with it. Move the slider in Figure 1 above zero to see it.

These are the dimensions of the irreducible representations of SO(4) that contain the bound states. Pauli used this symmetry to derive the hydrogen levels in early 1926, independently of Schrödinger's wave mechanics, which appeared the same winter.

The vector itself is old. Jakob Hermann and Johann Bernoulli found it for Kepler orbits around 1710, Laplace used it in 1799, and Hamilton rediscovered it in the 1840s. What stayed hidden was the symmetry behind it, because it does not act on positions alone. Noether's 1918 paper allows transformations that depend on velocities as well as positions, and that is the generality the Kepler problem needs.
