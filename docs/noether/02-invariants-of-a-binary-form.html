Invariants of a Binary Form

Part 2 of 15. A single calculation settles what survives the general linear group acting on the coefficients of a quadratic or cubic — and is the question Noether's doctoral thesis was written about.

In 1907, Emmy Noether submitted her doctoral dissertation at Erlangen under the direction of Paul Gordan. The topic was Über die Bildung des Formensystems der ternären biquadratischen Form — on constructing the system of forms for the ternary biquadratic form. The phrase system of forms names a branch of 19th-century mathematics that had been nearly ended by a single theorem of David Hilbert seventeen years earlier: the statement that the ring of polynomial invariants of a classical group acting on forms is finitely generated. Noether was trained on exactly the objects Hilbert's theorem had abstracted away from. Within two decades she would be the person who finished that abstraction.

To see what she grew up computing with, we take the simplest case — binary forms — and make the central phenomenon tactile: you apply an invertible linear substitution to the variables, the coefficients shuffle in a complicated bilinear way, and a single number called the discriminant survives the whole business up to a power of .

This is the heart of the whole classical-invariant picture. The orbit of a form is large, but it is confined to a level set of the invariants. Knowing the invariants is the same as knowing which orbit you are on, which is why Hilbert's 1890 theorem — "the ring of invariants is finitely generated" — is the structural statement that closes the subject: it says the orbit structure of any classical group action on forms is controlled by a finite list of computable quantities.

Hilbert and the end of the symbolic program

A footnote on Noether's advisor

By the 1880s, classical invariant-theorists were producing enormous books of generating relations among invariants of higher-degree forms. The symbolic methods they used ran out of steam around degree five, and no one could write down a finite generating list by hand past a certain point. Then in 1890 Hilbert published a proof that the ring of polynomial invariants of a classical group acting on forms is always finitely generated — finite, even though his proof produced no list.

Paul Gordan, Noether's doctoral advisor and a grand master of the symbolic program, is said to have grumbled:

"Das ist nicht Mathematik, das ist Theologie."
"That is not mathematics, it is theology."

Twenty years later Gordan's student would write the definitive axiomatic reformulation of the underlying theorem — the paper that opens Act III of this series. Invariants of classical groups are finitely generated because polynomial rings over fields are Noetherian: their ideals satisfy an ascending chain condition that makes every generating process stop after finitely many steps. We come back to that story in explainer 12.

Takeaways

Three things travel forward from the workshop.

  • A form is a specific algebraic object — coefficients in a fixed basis — on which a group acts by substituting new variables.
  • The discriminant is an invariant up to a power of , and an absolute invariant on .
  • "The ring of invariants is finitely generated" is the question Hilbert answered abstractly and Noether axiomatized.
