Phase and Electric Charge

Part 9 of 15. The first symmetry in this series that isn't geometric. Rotate the phase of a wave function and charge falls out, the prototype for every gauge theory.

Every symmetry we've met so far has been geometric: slide the system through space, rotate it, or slide it through time. With the Schrödinger wave function , a new kind of symmetry becomes available, an internal symmetry, one that doesn't move the particle but changes something about its description. The simplest is phase rotation: multiply by a complex number of unit modulus,

and watch what it does to physics. The answer is "nothing the observer can detect", is unchanged, the Schrödinger equation is unchanged, the probability density is unchanged. That is a symmetry. Run Noether's machine on it and a new conservation law appears, with density and a current that ties the wave function's phase gradient to the flow of probability, or, when the matter field is charged, the flow of electric charge.

Phase as rotation in the complex plane

A complex-valued wave function at a point is a magnitude plus a phase, a vector in the complex plane. A global phase rotation rotates every such vector by the same angle at once. The magnitude is invariant, so is invariant, so everything that observes the wave function is invariant.

From charge to the Standard Model

The same pattern runs the rest of high-energy physics. Every continuous symmetry of the Standard Model Lagrangian gives a Noether charge; every local version of one of these symmetries demands a gauge field; and the three gauge fields of the Standard Model (photons, W and Z, gluons) are each tied to a continuous symmetry group whose Noether charges you already know the names of.

When Noether wrote her paper in 1918 there was no gauge theory, quantum mechanics didn't exist yet, let alone quantum field theory. But the apparatus she built has exactly the right shape for the conservation laws the twentieth century eventually needed: internal symmetries of a field become global charges, local versions of the same symmetries become gauge fields, and the current that mediates the two is always the one she wrote down. Every conserved charge in particle physics is a Noether current for an internal symmetry.

Takeaways

  • Phase rotation of a complex field is a symmetry, an internal symmetry, not a geometric one.
  • The Noether current of this symmetry is probability density and probability current, conserved via the continuity equation.
  • When the matter is charged, "probability" becomes "electric charge" and the conservation law becomes charge conservation.
  • Global symmetries give Noether currents for free; local symmetries cost you a gauge field, and the gauge field you get is the prototype of QED (or, for larger groups, the rest of the Standard Model).
  • Noether's theorem generalizes straightforwardly to field theory, from 1D Lagrangian mechanics to Lagrangian densities integrated over spacetime, and gives local continuity equations instead of single conserved quantities.

Explainer 10 picks up the "global vs. local" distinction and makes it formal: Noether proved two theorems, the first (which we have been using) about global symmetries, and the second about local (gauge) symmetries, which produces relations between the Euler-Lagrange equations rather than new conserved charges.
