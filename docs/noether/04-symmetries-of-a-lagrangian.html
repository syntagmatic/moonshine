Symmetries of a Lagrangian

Part 4 of 15. The condition is stronger than "the equations of motion don't change," and the stronger condition is the one Noether's theorem will feed on.

A Lagrangian has a symmetry when a transformation of the coordinates leaves it unchanged — or changes it only by a total time derivative, a subtlety that will matter. This explainer is about getting clear on what that sentence means: we distinguish symmetries of the Lagrangian from symmetries of the equations of motion, we introduce the infinitesimal form of a transformation, and we set up the calculation that Noether's theorem will feed on in explainer 5.

Symmetry of L vs symmetry of the equations of motion

A symmetry of the Lagrangian is a stronger condition than a symmetry of the equations of motion. Two Lagrangians and that differ by a total time derivative give the same Euler-Lagrange equation — the boundary term from integrating by parts vanishes. So a transformation that changes by a total derivative is a symmetry of the equations of motion even though it isn't a strict symmetry of . That is why quasi-symmetries are still interesting: they still give Noether conservation laws.

Hamilton's principle compares paths with fixed endpoints, so this boundary term has no effect on which path is stationary, and the Euler-Lagrange equations are unchanged. Noether's theorem still fires; the conserved current just picks up an extra term from the boundary piece, which we will meet explicitly in the next explainer.

A strict symmetry leaves pointwise unchanged; a quasi-symmetry leaves unchanged up to a total time derivative. Both produce Noether conservation laws. The weaker condition is the one we actually want, because real physics systems are full of examples — the Galilean boost for a free particle, rigid-body rotations for some Lagrangians, gauge transformations for field theories — whose Lagrangians change by boundary terms but whose equations of motion are invariant.
