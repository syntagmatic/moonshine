Noether's Second Theorem

Part 9 of 13. Local symmetries give identities between the equations of motion instead of new conservation laws, which is why energy in general relativity is harder to define than in mechanics.

Noether's 1918 paper contains two theorems. The first, used throughout Parts 5 to 8, says every continuous global symmetry of an action gives a conserved current. The second covers local symmetries, like the local phase rotation of Part 8, whose parameter is a function of position. A local symmetry gives a differential identity that the equations of motion satisfy automatically. That identity is the source of gauge freedom, and of the difficulty of defining energy in general relativity.

Global versus local, again

A global symmetry has a single constant parameter: rotate every wave function by the same phase, or shift every coordinate by the same vector. A local symmetry has a parameter that depends on position: rotate the phase by a different amount at each point.

This changes the status of energy. In a theory with a global time-translation symmetry, the first theorem gives a conserved energy. In general relativity, time translation is local, one component of a diffeomorphism, and the second theorem says the matter energy-momentum tensor satisfies , the covariant divergence, not the partial one. There is, in general, no tensorial global energy in GR; only for spacetimes with an additional genuine Killing vector (an isometry) do you recover a conserved scalar energy.

Hilbert and Klein brought Noether to Göttingen in the spring of 1915, for her expertise in invariant theory, while Hilbert was working on the variational derivation of the field equations he presented that November. The status of energy conservation in the new theory stayed puzzling, and Klein and Hilbert argued it out in an exchange of letters that Klein published in 1918. Noether, barred from a formal Privatdozent appointment because she was a woman and lecturing under Hilbert's name, worked on the question. The result, Invariante Variationsprobleme (Nachr. Ges. Wiss. Göttingen, 1918), contains both theorems. The second answers the puzzle: a local symmetry does not give a conserved energy in the usual sense. It gives an identity among the field equations.

The two theorems side by side

The first theorem takes a constant parameter and gives a current with that holds on shell, only on solutions of the Euler-Lagrange equations. Time translation gives energy, rotation gives angular momentum, and global gives electric charge. The second theorem takes a function and gives a differential identity among the Euler-Lagrange equations that holds off shell, for every field configuration: for local and for diffeomorphisms. Its consequences are gauge redundancy and the lack of a general global energy in GR.

A common misreading of Noether's theorem is that every gauge symmetry gives a new conservation law. It does not: gauge symmetries fall under the second theorem.
