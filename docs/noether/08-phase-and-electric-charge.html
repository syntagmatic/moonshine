Phase and Electric Charge

Part 8 of 13. The first symmetry in the series that isn't geometric. Rotating the phase of a wave function gives conservation of charge, and making the rotation local leads to gauge theory.

Every symmetry so far has been geometric: shift the system in space or time, or rotate it. The Schrödinger wave function allows an internal symmetry, one that changes the description of the particle without moving it. The simplest is phase rotation, which multiplies by a complex number of unit modulus,

No observer can detect the change: and the Schrödinger equation are both unchanged. Noether's theorem turns this symmetry into a conservation law with density and a current that ties the phase gradient of the wave function to the flow of probability, or, when the matter field is charged, the flow of electric charge.

Phase as rotation in the complex plane

At each point, a complex wave function has a magnitude and a phase, like a vector in the complex plane. A global phase rotation turns every such vector by the same angle. The magnitude does not change, so neither does .

From charge to the Standard Model

The same pattern runs through the rest of particle physics. Continuous symmetries of the Standard Model Lagrangian give Noether charges, and making a symmetry local requires a gauge field. The Standard Model's gauge fields (the photon, the W and Z, the gluons) each come from a local symmetry group in this way.

When Noether wrote her paper in 1918, quantum mechanics did not exist yet and gauge theory was only beginning. Her framework still fits the later physics: electric charge, baryon number and lepton number are Noether charges of internal symmetries, and energy, momentum and angular momentum are the charges of spacetime symmetries.

Noether's paper contains a second theorem, about local symmetries like this one. It produces relations among the Euler-Lagrange equations rather than new conserved charges, and it is the subject of the next article.
