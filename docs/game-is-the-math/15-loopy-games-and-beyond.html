Loopy Games and Beyond

Part 15 of 15. The capstone. Games that can loop forever, surreal numbers that are bigger than infinity, the corners of combinatorial game theory still under research, and where to go next if you want to keep playing.

We started this series with a promise that the games we play and the mathematics we use to analyze them are the same activity. Fourteen explainers later we have kept the promise in fourteen specific cases. We can play Nim and tell you the optimal move from any position; we can decompose Hackenbush trees with the colon principle; we can compute Grundy values from first principles; we can prove that every impartial game is a Nim heap in disguise via Sprague–Grundy; we can read a partizan game value and locate the corresponding surreal number on the number line; we can cool a hot game and pull a thermograph out of it; we can stare at a Hex board and know without finding a strategy that the first player wins; we can parse a long-chain Dots and Boxes endgame and call out the loony move before the beginner does. The arc the series promised has been walked.

This final explainer is different in register. There is no playable game and no theorem to prove. Instead we look at the parts of combinatorial game theory we deliberately did not cover: games that can loop forever and break the recursive definition of game value; surreal numbers that are bigger than every real (and reciprocals smaller than every positive); pieces of the modern research landscape, misère quotients, scoring play, partizan games beyond the "all small" world, that sit just past where we stopped. We close with a reading list specific enough to act on, and a tile grid of every theorem the series taught.

If you have come to the series in order, Nim, Hackenbush, Grundy and mex, Sprague–Grundy, misère, the partizan games of Act II, Hex and Go and Dots and Boxes in Act III, you have walked the same arc Berlekamp, Conway, and Guy walk in the first half of Winning Ways. If you have read this series in some other order, the arc is recoverable from the index page; the explainers cross-reference each other but most can stand alone. Either way, this last explainer assumes you have played the games in the previous fourteen, not just read about them. The shape of CGT is recoverable from the play, and the play is what we have asked you to do all along.

One stylistic note for this final explainer. We will use less mathematical notation than the previous fourteen and more prose, because the parts of the field we are pointing at are mostly things you should hear about and read about, not compute on the spot. The figures are static; the script at the bottom of the file just renders KaTeX and draws a couple of SVG diagrams. There is nothing to click. If you have been reading on a tablet or in a small browser window where the interactives in earlier explainers were sometimes finicky, this final explainer is the easiest of the fifteen to read in any environment.

When the recursion stops working

Every game value we computed in this series came out of the same recursive definition. To compute the value of a position, look at all the positions you can move to, compute their values, and take some operation on those values, the mex for impartial games, the simplicity-theorem rule for short partizan games. The recursion bottoms out at terminal positions whose value we know directly: zero options, value zero. The whole machinery rests on the assumption that you can get to the bottom, that every chain of moves eventually ends.

That assumption has a name. A game is finite (or "short," in Conway's vocabulary, when the number of distinct positions is also bounded) if every play must end after finitely many moves no matter what either player does. Nim is finite: each move strictly decreases the total number of stones, so any play ends in at most turns. Hackenbush is finite: each move removes at least one edge. Domineering is finite: each move covers two empty squares. Hex is finite: each move places a stone and never removes one. Dots and Boxes is finite: each move draws a line and never erases one. Every game we have analyzed has a strict numerical decrease per move, which forces termination.

What does the play of this game actually look like? If both players are allowed to keep moving back and forth between and forever, the game has no winner and no loser; it is a draw by infinite play. If a rule forces somebody to break the cycle eventually (say, a clock or a "no repeated positions" rule), the analysis depends on which rule. If we declare that the player who cannot move loses (normal play), then position is a loss for the player to move and position always has the move to available, so is a win. From the only move is to , which is winning, so is losing. The classification is well-defined; the Grundy value, on the other hand, is not, because the recursion that defines it has no base case to descend to.

Loopy games, Conway's name for games where the same position can be reached more than once during play, have a real theory. It does not collapse, it just becomes more elaborate. Game values for loopy games take values in an extended structure that includes symbols for "draws by infinite play" alongside the ordinary numbers, and the analog of mex is replaced by a more complicated operation over a richer value system. The classic source is volume 2 of Winning Ways, which spends a chapter (chapter 11, "Games Infinite and Indeterminate") building the theory and applying it to specific loopy games like Fox and Geese.

The shape of the result is that loopy games admit three classes of position rather than two, winning, losing, and drawn by infinite play, and the analog of the Grundy value carries an extra component encoding which of those it is. The clean theorem that every impartial game is a single Nim heap becomes the messier theorem that every impartial loopy game is a "loopy Nim heap" with two coordinates. We will not build this here; we point you at the chapter and trust that the existence of the theory is enough to know that loopy games are not a wall, just a longer climb.

A few names from that chapter are worth knowing in passing. Conway distinguishes stoppers from non-stoppers: a stopper is a loopy game where, although the position graph contains cycles, every play between optimal players still terminates (because each optimal player has a finitely-decreasing strategy that does not enter a cycle). Stoppers behave well; the theory of values for stoppers is much closer to the finite-game theory we already built. Non-stoppers (games where optimal play really can loop forever) are the genuinely strange ones, and they require values like and for "drawn by Left forcing the loop" and "drawn by Right forcing the loop." There is also , the "deathless universal draw," for positions where neither player can escape the loop. The arithmetic on this extended value system is its own chapter.

The most-studied loopy game is the impartial game on a directed graph called Generalized Geography: positions are vertices, moves are along directed edges, and a move is forbidden if it would revisit a vertex already used in this game. This rule turns Generalized Geography into a finite game (positions cannot repeat), but the underlying graph can have cycles, and the analysis exposes the complexity hidden inside loopy structures. Generalized Geography is also the canonical example of a PSPACE-complete game (Schaefer 1978) which is one reason "we should just compute it" usually does not scale.

What "loopy" rules out for us. Every game in this series is finite by construction. We never had to think about draws because none of our games could produce one. Real games people play (chess and checkers and Go included) are not finite under their bare rules; they need an external rule (the threefold-repetition rule in chess, ko in Go, the 50-move rule, etc.) to force termination. Those rules are what convert the natural infinite game into a finite one we can apply combinatorial game theory to. Without them, even chess endgames are technically loopy.

The most familiar loopy game in mass culture is the ko fight in Go. A ko is a position where capturing one stone would immediately recreate the previous board position, and the rule of ko forbids that recapture for at least one move. Without the ko rule, Go would be loopy, you could imagine an "endless" sequence of capture and recapture in a single board cell. With the ko rule, Go becomes finite (the rule cuts the cycle). The CGT analysis of ko fights, especially in Berlekamp's "Mathematical Go" program, requires the loopy-game machinery to handle correctly; the temperature theory we sketched in Explainer 13 needs an extension just to discuss the ko temperature accurately. The "ko threat", a move you make elsewhere on the board to force your opponent to spend a tempo before you reopen the ko, is a play-by-play artifact of the underlying loopy structure.

Chess and checkers have their own loopy patterns, mostly handled by external rules. The threefold-repetition rule in chess is what stops a game from cycling forever between two king-and-pawn positions; without it, the king-and-pawn endgame would not always terminate. Checkers (under tournament rules) has a 50-move rule for similar reasons. These rules are not part of the abstract game; they are externally imposed cycle-breakers that turn the game into the sort of finite object CGT can compute on. The trade-off is a familiar one: convert a beautiful infinite object into a slightly ugly finite one in order to apply the existing theory, or invent new theory for the infinite object directly. CGT has done both, with the first approach (rules-make-it-finite) being more common in practice and the second (loopy-game theory proper) being more elegant in principle.

Surreal numbers larger than every real

In Explainer 7 we built the surreal numbers from Conway's recursive Left/Right construction and we stopped at the rationals. That was a deliberate scope choice. The surreal construction does not stop at the rationals, it does not stop anywhere. Conway's On Numbers and Games walks the construction past the reals and into transfinite territory, picking up infinite numbers, infinitesimal numbers, and a class of "numbers" so big that it cannot itself be a set.

The construction, briefly. A surreal number is born on day as a pair of sets of earlier surreal numbers, subject to the rule that no element of is greater than or equal to any element of . That day picks up every real number as a Dedekind cut of dyadic rationals (so π and √2 join the party), and it also picks up genuinely new things.

The arithmetic looks like the ordinary arithmetic of real numbers extended: addition, multiplication, division, comparison all generalize, and they generalize uniquely. Addition is commutative on surreals (unlike ordinal addition, which has ). Subtraction works. Division by nonzero works. The surreals form an ordered field, the largest possible ordered field, in a sense Conway makes precise. They are not "another notion of number"; they are the field that contains every other ordered field, with the ordinals embedded as a proper sub-class.

The historical accident of how this came together is worth a paragraph. Conway built the surreal numbers in the early 1970s, originally as a side project while working on the analysis of Go endgames. He noticed that game positions could be assigned values that satisfied the same arithmetic as ordinary numbers, and that the resulting "numbers" included not just the rationals but also Dedekind-like cuts producing every real, and beyond that the entire transfinite tower. Conway wrote it up in On Numbers and Games in 1976. Donald Knuth heard about it shortly after, was so taken with the idea that he wrote a 119-page novella titled Surreal Numbers introducing the construction in dialogue form, and gave the system its name (Conway's own name for them was just "numbers"). The novella was published in 1974 (two years before the formal book) and it is partly responsible for the surreals being known outside of CGT circles at all. The fact that this entire infinite-extension number system was discovered in the course of analyzing Go endgames is the kind of pedagogically perfect historical accident the present series cannot improve on, and we have not tried to.

We stopped at the rationals in Explainer 7 because every short partizan game has a rational game value (specifically, a dyadic rational). To need the infinite surreals you have to look at infinite games, transfinite games, or specific finite constructions whose Left/Right options use the infinite surreals already. The mathematical structure is there from day onward; the games that exercise it sit further into Conway's construction than we have any business going. The relevant chapters of On Numbers and Games are 8 (Day ), 9 (real numbers), and 10 (infinite games), in that order.

Why we stopped where we did. The series goal was for you to be able to compute. Computing with finite surreal numbers is something you can do on paper. Computing with requires fluency in transfinite ordinal arithmetic and a willingness to manipulate proper classes. Both are doable, neither is "first course in CGT." If you want to go there, the door is the back half of Conway's book.

One last note before we move on. The fact that we got infinite ordinals out of a Left/Right game-construction is not as exotic as it sounds, but it does carry a moral. Conway built the surreals because the natural game-value system forced him to: the simplicity theorem applied to the Left set "all integers" and Right set "empty" must produce some value, and the only consistent choice is "the smallest thing larger than every integer," which is exactly the order-theoretic definition of . The infinite ordinals were sitting in the game-value world all along; they just had to be noticed. This is a structural argument for the title of the series. The mathematics does not stop at the rationals because the games do not stop at the rationals.

What we did not cover

The series picked a route through combinatorial game theory and stuck to it. The route was: impartial games (Nim through Sprague–Grundy), partizan games (Hackenbush colors through temperature), in-the-wild games (Hex, Go endgames, Dots and Boxes). Three acts and a closing essay. That route is one of several reasonable spines through CGT; another reasonable spine would have started with the surreal number system and built games on top of it; another would have started with computational complexity and built up to "and we cannot even decide who wins generalized Hex." We picked the play-first spine because the playing-first promise is one of CGT's distinctive virtues, and because the Conway–Berlekamp–Guy program in Winning Ways follows roughly the same arc.

Several large branches sit just off the route we took. Here are the ones we are most aware of, with pointers to the standard entry points for each.

Misère quotients and the indistinguishability theory

Explainer 5 introduced misère play and showed that even Nim (the cleanest impartial game in normal play) develops a wart at the all-ones endgame when you flip the winning condition. We stopped there because the full misère theory is genuinely a research area. Plambeck and Siegel built a deep theory of misère quotients in the 2000s: equivalence classes of impartial-game positions that behave identically under all sums, with an associated commutative monoid structure that classifies the misère-play universe far more finely than Grundy values do. For some games (Dawson's Chess is the classical example) the misère quotient has been computed and the game is "tamed"; for others it has not, and the game is an open problem.

Start with: Plambeck & Siegel, "Misère quotients for impartial games," Journal of Combinatorial Theory A 115 (2008). Then: Siegel, Combinatorial Game Theory (AMS 2013), chapter VI.

Scoring play

Every game in this series declared a winner by a "last move wins" or "last move loses" rule. Real games (Go, Othello, Mancala) declare a winner by counting points instead, and in scoring play the goal is to maximize the score difference rather than to be the player who cannot lose. The combinatorial game theory of scoring play has a separate algebraic structure (Milnor and Hanner started it in the 1950s; Ettinger, Stewart, Milley, Renault, Larsson, and others have extended it more recently) and the values live in a structure called the scoring universe. Scoring CGT is the right framework for analyzing real Go endgames at full generality, beyond the temperature theory we sketched in Explainer 13.

Start with: Milnor, "Sums of positional games," Contributions to the Theory of Games 2 (1953). For modern treatment: Larsson, Nowakowski, Santos, "Scoring games: the state of play," in Games of No Chance 5 (Cambridge 2019).

Partizan games beyond "all small"

The partizan theory we built (game values, simplicity theorem, temperature, sums) works cleanly inside a class called the all small games, where every position has a finite stopping value. Beyond that class lies the broader universe of partizan games, with values that include "infinitesimals shifted by integers" (Conway's stars, ups, downs, and double-ups), and finer structure called atomic weights for measuring which infinitesimal sums are positive. The all-small theory is one of the technical highlights of Winning Ways; we sketched it without committing to its development. Beyond all small, you get into game values that are not numbers and require separate theory.

Start with: Berlekamp, Conway & Guy, Winning Ways, volume 1, chapters 8 and 9 (Stars, Ups, and Downs; The Heat of Battle). For the modern treatment: Siegel, Combinatorial Game Theory, chapter V.

Mancala-family games (Sowing games)

Mancala is one of the oldest known games, physical boards have been excavated from contexts dating back two millennia, and the rules vary widely across the world. The combinatorial game theory of mancala-family games (Kalah, Oware, Bao, and dozens of regional variants) is genuinely hard, because the "sowing" mechanic creates long-range dependencies between the pits and many positions are partizan in a way that does not yield to standard tools. Some specific small variants have been solved by computer search; Kalah(6,4) (six pits per side, four stones per pit) was solved by Geoffrey Irving's group in 2000. Most of the family is open.

Start with: Irving, Donkers, Uiterwijk, "Solving Kalah," ICGA Journal 23 (2000). For the broader family: Russ, The Complete Mancala Games Book (Marlowe 2000).

Wythoff's game

Wythoff's game is two-heap Nim plus one extra move type: you may also remove the same positive number of stones from both heaps simultaneously. Bouton's XOR rule does not apply, and the losing positions form a beautiful pattern related to the golden ratio: the th losing position is , where is the golden ratio. The Beatty sequences and partition the positive integers, and that partition is the win/loss classification. Wythoff is the classic "almost Nim with a twist" and we omitted it because it does not slot cleanly into any of our acts.

Start with: Wythoff's original paper, "A modification of the game of Nim," Nieuw Archief voor Wiskunde 7 (1907). Modern treatment: Fraenkel, "How to beat your Wythoff opponent on three fronts," American Mathematical Monthly 89 (1982).

Kayles and the octal games

Kayles is the bowling game: a row of pins, each turn you knock down one pin or two adjacent pins, last mover wins. Its Grundy value sequence is eventually periodic with period 12, and that periodicity is the springboard into the broader study of octal games, a family of impartial games parameterized by a base-8 code that determines which subtraction patterns are legal. Most octal games have eventually periodic Grundy sequences; a few famous small ones (octal 0.6, "Grundy's game") do not have a known period and might never. Octal-game periodicity is a quiet open problem of CGT, and the "ultimately periodic conjecture", that every finite octal game has eventually periodic Grundy values, has been open since the 1960s.

Start with: Berlekamp, Conway & Guy, Winning Ways, volume 1, chapter 4 (Hackenbush) and volume 2, chapter 16 (The Emperor and his Money). For computational results: Achim Flammenkamp's octal-game tables online.

Computational complexity of game positions

"Compute the Grundy value of a Hackenbush position" is a polynomial-time problem; "compute the Grundy value of a Geography game" is PSPACE-hard. Computational complexity is a parallel theory to CGT proper: it asks not "what is the value" but "how hard is the problem of computing the value." Many natural CGT questions are PSPACE-complete or worse, including the deciding-the-winner problem for generalized Hex, generalized Go, and generalized Othello on boards. The complexity perspective is important context for any "we should just compute it" sentiment.

Start with: Hearn & Demaine, Games, Puzzles, and Computation (A K Peters 2009). For the foundational results: Schaefer, "On the complexity of some two-person perfect-information games," JCSS 16 (1978).

The set-theoretic view of surreals

Conway's surreal numbers form a proper class, not a set: the collection is too big to fit inside ZFC the way a set does. The same construction simultaneously produces all the ordinals (as the "left options of natural numbers, right empty" surreals born on each ordinal day), so the surreals contain a copy of the ordinal class On as a sub-class. The interaction with set theory, what an ordinal really is when viewed as a game, how the surreals relate to non-standard models of analysis, what the cardinality of the surreal "field" looks like under various foundational choices, is its own subject and we do not touch it.

Start with: Knuth, Surreal Numbers (Addison–Wesley 1974). It is a novella, and it is more friendly than its reputation suggests. Then: Ehrlich, "The absolute arithmetic continuum and the unification of all numbers great and small," Bulletin of Symbolic Logic 18 (2012).

Endgame databases and computer-verified game solutions

Adjacent to combinatorial game theory but not part of it: the engineering project of computing the value of every position in a game by exhaustive search and storing the result. Schaeffer's checkers solution (2007) used this approach to prove that perfect play in checkers is a draw, a 14-year computation that built a database of every drawn or won position. Connect Four was solved similarly by Allis (1988) and re-proved by computer search. Awari (a mancala variant) was solved by Romein and Bal (2002). These are not CGT proofs in the Conway–Berlekamp sense; they are computer-assisted exhaustive searches, but the questions they answer (who wins from the start? what is the longest forced win?) are CGT questions, and they are sometimes the only available answer for games where structural theory has not provided one.

Start with: Schaeffer, One Jump Ahead: Computer Perfection at Checkers (Springer, 2nd ed. 2009). For the broader project: van den Herik, Uiterwijk, van Rijswijck, "Games solved: now and in the future," Artificial Intelligence 134 (2002).

Where to go next

If you want to keep going, the literature is small enough to read cover to cover and large enough to keep you busy for years. A few books are essential and most of the rest are optional. Below is the short list, with the chapter recommendations specific to the topics this series touched.

The four essentials

  • Berlekamp, Conway & Guy, Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays, 4 volumes (A K Peters, 2nd edition 2001–2004). The bible. Volume 1, chapters 1–4: Nim and Hackenbush, exactly the ground we covered in Explainers 1–4. Volume 1, chapters 5–8: partizan game values, Domineering, simplicity theorem (Explainers 6–9). Volume 1, chapter 6: temperature and cooling (our Explainer 10). Volume 2, chapter 11: loopy games (this explainer). Volume 3 chapter 16 is octal games. Volume 4 contains Conway's solo treatments of specific classics.
  • Conway, On Numbers and Games (A K Peters, 2nd edition 2001). Conway's solo book. Chapters 0–7 build the surreal number system; chapter 7 reaches the simplicity theorem (our Explainer 8). Chapters 8–11 push past finite construction into infinite surreals, real numbers, and infinite games, the territory we sketched in this explainer. Chapter 14 is the original "Hackenbush is everywhere" derivation.
  • Siegel, Combinatorial Game Theory (AMS Graduate Studies 146, 2013). The modern graduate-level treatment. Chapter II is Sprague–Grundy proven cleanly (our Explainer 4). Chapter III treats partizan game values formally (Explainers 6–8). Chapter IV is temperatures and the thermograph machinery (Explainer 10). Chapters V–VI treat the all-small theory and the infinitesimal universe we listed under "what we did not cover." Chapter VI is the misère theory.
  • Albert, Nowakowski & Wolfe, Lessons in Play: An Introduction to Combinatorial Game Theory (A K Peters, 2nd edition 2019). The undergraduate-friendly door into the subject, written specifically as a course textbook. If Winning Ways feels too playful and Siegel feels too formal, this sits in between. Chapter 2 is Nim and Sprague–Grundy; chapters 3–6 are partizan values and temperature; chapter 8 surveys the modern landscape briefly.

For specific topics in this series

  • Nim and the XOR rule. Bouton's original 1901 paper, Nim, A Game with a Complete Mathematical Theory, Annals of Mathematics, second series, vol. 3. Five pages, freely available via JSTOR. The whole modern theory grew out of this short note.
  • Sprague–Grundy. Sprague, "Über mathematische Kampfspiele," Tôhoku Mathematical Journal 41 (1935), and Grundy, "Mathematics and games," Eureka 2 (1939). They proved the same theorem independently a few years apart and neither knew about the other for a while.
  • Surreal numbers. Knuth's Surreal Numbers (1974), which invented the name. It is a 119-page novella in which two grad students stranded on a beach reconstruct Conway's theory from a stone tablet. It really is a novella; it really does work.
  • Hex. Browne, Hex Strategy: Making the Right Connections (A K Peters 2000), and Hayward & van Rijswijck, "Hex and combinatorics," Discrete Mathematics 306 (2006). Strategy stealing is in any introductory text but Browne goes deep on actual play.
  • Go endgames and temperature. Berlekamp & Wolfe, Mathematical Go: Chilling Gets the Last Point (A K Peters 1994). The book that put CGT into the hands of professional Go players, and the source for the temperature-theoretic Go endgame analysis.
  • Dots and Boxes. Berlekamp, The Dots and Boxes Game: Sophisticated Child's Play (A K Peters 2000). The complete long-chain theory, written by the man who developed it.
  • Surveys of the field. Aviezri Fraenkel's Combinatorial Games bibliography (online, regularly updated through 2012, ~600 pages of references). It is the master list of essentially everything published in the field. If you want to find every paper on a particular game, search Fraenkel.

Adjacent and parallel

  • Hearn & Demaine, Games, Puzzles, and Computation (A K Peters 2009). Computational complexity of games; how to prove a game PSPACE-complete, EXPTIME-complete, or undecidable.
  • Games of No Chance, volumes 1–5 (Cambridge / MSRI publications, 1996–2019). Five proceedings volumes from the periodic combinatorial-games workshops; each volume is 20–30 papers with current research, surveys, and computational results. Volume 5 (2019) is the most recent.
  • Demaine, Algorithmic Lower Bounds Through Combinatorial Games. A series of MIT lectures (free online) translating CGT-style decision-problem reductions into proofs of computational hardness for puzzle and game problems. Connects the world of this series to the world of complexity theory.

Where you stand at the end

Fifteen explainers ago, "combinatorial game theory" was probably a phrase you had heard attached to Conway, surreal numbers, or maybe a Numberphile video. You now have the following:

  • The XOR rule for Nim, in your fingers, with Bouton's two-line proof. Multi-heap, any size; you can win at Marienbad against anyone who has not read the same five pages as you.
  • The colon principle for Hackenbush trees and the recognition that Green Hackenbush positions are Nim heaps in disguise, different vocabulary, same theory.
  • The mex operator and the Grundy value as a single integer that captures everything strategic about an impartial position. You can compute it bottom-up by hand on game trees of reasonable size.
  • The Sprague–Grundy theorem as the structural reason every impartial game looks like Nim, plus the consequence that the Grundy value of a sum of games is the XOR of the individual values. The whole impartial universe collapses into Nim.
  • Misère play as the half of the universe where the clean theory cracks. You can play misère Nim correctly at the all-ones endgame, and you know that the more general misère theory is genuinely harder than the normal-play theory it replaces.
  • Blue-Red Hackenbush and the recognition that partizan games have rational game values, then surreal game values, then values that are not numbers at all.
  • Conway's surreal number construction as game values, with the simplicity theorem as the rule that picks out which value a Left/Right pair has. You can build the first dozen surreals by hand and place them on a number line.
  • Domineering and the realization that some game values are "hot", both players want to move there, and that hot games need temperature theory rather than just Grundy values.
  • Cooling and thermographs as the machinery for handling sums of hot games. You know which component to move in by looking at temperatures.
  • Hex's no-draws theorem and Nash's strategy-stealing argument: a proof that the first player wins which finds no winning strategy. A hard mathematical fact extracted from a soft logical move.
  • Temperature applied to real Go endgames (Berlekamp's program), so that "where to play next" becomes a question with a quantitative answer rather than a feel.
  • Dots and Boxes long-chain rules: the rule of long chains, the loony move, double-crossed handouts, and the parity argument that turns the endgame into a counting problem.
  • And, from this last explainer: an appreciation that loopy games, infinite surreals, misère quotients, scoring play, and several other research directions sit just past where we stopped, with named entry points into each.

What you have is not a complete picture of combinatorial game theory, there is no such thing, the field has too many open problems for that, but a coherent, computationally grounded tour you can keep building on. The reading list above is what to do next if you want to. Or you can stop here, find a board, and beat your friends. That was always the other goal.

A few questions to test your fluency, none of them rhetorical. Which Nim positions in three heaps with sizes at most 4 are losing for the player to move? (You should be able to enumerate them in under a minute, by computing the XOR of every triple.) What is the Grundy value of a Hackenbush stalk of length 6 attached at the ground? (One number, computed by a single recursive sweep.) What is the game value of a 2×3 Domineering rectangle, viewed as a partizan game with Left placing vertical dominoes and Right placing horizontal? (A surreal number with a small denominator.) On a Hex 3×3 board, who wins from the empty position, and what is the first-move strategy you would use? (You can derive the winner without finding a strategy; if you also want a strategy, the center cell is the standard answer.) In Dots and Boxes on a 4×4 dot grid (a 3×3 box grid), how many long chains do you need on your side of the parity to force a win? (Modulo dot-grid parity, the answer is in the rule of long chains.) Each of these questions has a definite answer, you have the tools to compute it, and that is what we promised at the start.

If a question above stumped you, the corresponding explainer has the answer worked out. Treat the series as a reference now, not a tutorial.

And a softer test, the one a friend would actually try first: pick any of the games from the series, sit down with someone who has not played it, and teach them to play. If you can do that without referring back to the explainer, you have it.

And one harder question for the road. We claimed in Explainer 4 that the Sprague–Grundy theorem makes Nim "the universal impartial game", every impartial game is Nim in disguise. That is true for normal play. It is not true for misère play (Explainer 5 gave the counterexample at the all-ones endgame), and it is not even sensibly stated for partizan play (the partizan universe has no single "universal game" the way Nim is the universal impartial game; the closest analog is the surreal numbers themselves, which form an algebraic system rather than a single game). Knowing why each of these statements is true and which one of the three regimes you are in is most of what makes a CGT question well-posed. If you can phrase the question correctly the answer is usually within reach of the theory; if you cannot, that is the first thing to fix.

The deepest pleasure in CGT, in our experience, is exactly that moment when a game position you cannot think your way through suddenly resolves itself because you remember which regime you are in. The Hackenbush position becomes a Grundy value because Sprague–Grundy says it must. The Domineering rectangle becomes a number because the partizan theory permits it. The Hex board has a winner without an exhibited strategy because strategy stealing finds one without finding it. The Go endgame becomes a sum-of-temperatures problem because Berlekamp showed it could. None of these moves is forced; all of them are available the moment you recognize the regime. That recognition is the skill the series tried to build, and the rest is practice.

Closing

This series started with a child's game played with matchsticks (Marienbad Nim, 1961) and ended with a research area that still has open problems older than most of us (the misère universe, Wythoff, octal-game periodicity, scoring CGT). The arc was always meant to be short enough to read in a sitting and broad enough to leave you somewhere new. The plan held: fifteen explainers, fifteen playable games (and one closing essay where the game is the entire shelf of books we just listed), one consistent pedagogy of play first, theorem second, abstraction third.

The promise we opened with was that the games we play and the mathematics we use to analyze them are the same activity. The proofs of that promise live in the explainers themselves: every Grundy value computation is a game position; every surreal number is a game value; every temperature is the answer to "how badly do both players want to move here." There is no separate mathematical object floating above the games, there is just the game, and the activity of playing it carefully is the activity of doing the mathematics. The title of the series was meant literally and we hope the meaning has come through.

One thing the series did not do: it did not turn you into a tournament player at any of these games. CGT gives you the theoretical apparatus and the worst-case-optimal strategies; the gap between "knowing the optimal strategy" and "winning at speed against a strong opponent" is its own discipline (search heuristics, opening libraries, time management, psychological play) that we do not touch. If you want to be a competitive Hex or Go player, this series is a foundation but not a replacement for the relevant practice. Conversely, if you have ever competed at one of these games and never been told why the long-chain count or the temperature mattered, the series fills in the why. Foundations and practice live in the same building, on different floors.

The other thing the series did not do, and was never going to: it did not pretend that combinatorial game theory is a finished subject. Several of the games we played in earlier explainers have open problems still hanging over them. Sprouts is solved up to about by computer search and only conjecturally beyond. The general question of which positions in misère play are "wild" is open. The pursuit of a clean partizan analog of the Sprague–Grundy theorem (an analog that would compress the partizan universe the way Grundy values compress the impartial universe) has been pursued for fifty years without complete success. CGT is still small enough to be worked on by a single mathematician, and still rich enough that the new results in Games of No Chance 5 are not just refinements of known things. If any of this catches you and you want to do research, the field is open.

What you do with it now is up to you. The literature above will keep you busy for as long as you want. The games themselves will keep you company without any reading at all. We are done.

If you ever explain a Grundy value to a friend at a bar, or talk a beginner through Bouton's XOR rule with a handful of coins, or use the rule of long chains to win a Dots and Boxes game against someone who has never thought about parity, that is the series doing what it was supposed to do. CGT is a body of knowledge that travels well in conversation, because the games are universal and the theorems are short. Take it with you.

Thanks for climbing it with us.
