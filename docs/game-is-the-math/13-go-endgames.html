Go Endgames and CGT

Part 13 of 15. Go is enormous. The endgame is not. When the dust settles into independent local fights, each fight is a small partizan game with a value and a temperature, and the temperature theory we built in Explainer 10 tells you which fight to play in next.

Go has a search-tree branching factor that defeats brute force and a global structure that defeats most analytic theories. We are not going to analyze a full Go board with combinatorial game theory. Nobody does — not because nobody has tried, but because the theory of partizan game sums only becomes tractable once a position has decomposed into pieces that do not interact, and a midgame Go position interacts everywhere with everything.

The endgame is different. Late in a Go game, after the big strategic moves are settled, the board breaks into geographic regions whose stones cannot reach each other. A move in the upper-left corner does not change the local outcome of a fight in the lower-right. Each region is a small partizan game in the sense of the previous explainers, with Left options (Black plays here), Right options (White plays here), and a definite numerical value. The game we are actually playing is the sum of those games, in the precise sense.

That is exactly the situation Explainer 11 set up and Explainer 10 told us how to play. The fight with the highest temperature is the fight worth most points to the next move. If you take the cool ones first you give your opponent a free pass on the hot ones. Professional Go players have known this in feel for centuries; starting in the late 1980s Elwyn Berlekamp made it a theorem and started solving small Go endgames exactly.

This explainer hand-works one specific endgame. Three independent fights, three values, three temperatures, one strategy, one verifiable score. We are not going to attempt full CGT-Go — the literature on that is graduate-shaped and lives in Berlekamp and Wolfe's Mathematical Go. We are going to make the decomposition idea concrete, show that temperature picks the move pros pick, and then point at where to read more.

Live Black and White stones bound each region; the empty points inside each region are still in dispute. We will treat each region as an independent partizan game, compute its value and temperature, and see what temperature theory says about the order in which the regions should be played.

Each region is bounded so that no stone played inside one region can affect another — the games are genuinely independent in the CGT sense, not just visually separated.

A few honest words about the position. We constructed it so that the fights do not interact. Real Go endgames have to be checked for that property — sometimes a stone in one corner attacks a group in a neighboring region, in which case the regions are not independent and we cannot just add their game values. There is a whole subculture of Go problem composition aimed at producing positions with clean decomposition, and Berlekamp's Mathematical Go spends most of its first chapter explaining how to recognize one and how to weld together small independent games into puzzles whose answers can be checked exactly. For us, the constructive shortcut is to start from independence and then read off the values.

So the three fights have temperatures 2, 1, and ½ respectively. They are all three "switches" — the formal name for partizan games of the form with . Switches are the simplest hot games. They are also exactly the kind of game you encounter in a clean Go endgame: a region where the player who moves first in it locks in the outcome, but until somebody moves, the outcome is balanced between the two stops.

The decomposition theorem we are using. When a Go position factors into independent regions, the value of the whole position is the partizan sum of the local values. Two players playing the sum should each move in the component that maximizes their local gain — adjusted for what the opponent will do in response, which is what temperature theory captures.

That sentence is a paraphrase of Theorem 11 of Berlekamp and Wolfe's Mathematical Go, which is itself an application of Conway and Hanner's earlier work on cooling and temperature. The proof is in Explainer 10 of this series — we are using a result we already built.

The temperature strategy in action

We have three fights with temperatures 2, 1, and ½. The total game is the sum . Whose turn is it? It does not matter much — the temperature theory tells both players the same thing.

Take the hottest fight first. The player to move should play in Fight A, because that is where one move shifts the local score by the most. If you let your opponent play in Fight A, they will gain two points of swing relative to taking a cooler fight. There is nothing subtle about it — the cooler fights will still be worth their points later, but the hottest fight will not.

Now reset and try Black opens with Fight C (temperature ½) instead. White immediately plays Fight A (temperature 2), Black plays B (temperature 1), and we tally: Black gained 1 in C, lost 2 in A, gained 1 in B, for a net of 0. Two whole points worse than playing the hot move first.

The two-point gap is not arbitrary. The fight Black abandoned by playing C first was Fight A, where the swing between Black-plays-it and White-plays-it is the gap between the stops, . Black traded a +2 in A for a +1 in C and then had to absorb a −2 in A from White's reply: a single move shifted by one place in the temperature ordering, and the locked-in score moves by the difference of the locked-in stops in the components that switched hands.

The bookkeeping is genuinely subtle in general. The Berlekamp-Wolfe analysis handles it cleanly via "thermography," which draws a temperature-vs-value graph for each component and reads the optimal play off the picture. In larger examples — for instance endgames where six or seven fights have temperatures interleaved across half-integer boundaries — you need the full machinery from Explainer 10 to get the right answer move-by-move. We are showing you the easiest case in which the hottest-first heuristic is exactly optimal: three switches with distinct temperatures, played to completion. In that case the heuristic reduces to a one-line argument, and you can verify the score by hand.

The strategy in one sentence. When the game decomposes into independent components and it is your turn, play in the component with the highest temperature. The opponent will play in the next-highest. Continue until the temperatures all drop to zero (i.e., the position is fully cooled and the remaining components are pure numbers).

Play around with Figure 3 a bit. Notice that Black actually cannot hold the score above +2 — White's hottest-first responses always give White at least 1 point of swing. The locked-in maximum score for Black, against optimal play, is the sum of locked-in mean values plus the alternating sum of temperatures starting from the hottest. In our position the means are 0 + 0 + ½ = ½, and the alternating sum of temperatures (Black gets the hottest, White takes the next, Black takes the third) is +2 − 1 + ½ = +1½. The two add to +2 exactly. The temperature theory makes this number computable, with no game-tree search at all.

Why this works for Go and (mostly) does not work elsewhere

Let us be careful about what we have shown. We claimed a Go endgame decomposes into a sum of independent partizan games and that temperature theory tells you how to play that sum. Both claims have load-bearing assumptions.

Independence. Two regions are independent in the CGT sense only when no possible move in one can affect the legal options or the eventual outcome of the other. In Go this means the regions are separated by living groups with full eyespace, so no future fight inside one region can flow into the other through a capturing race or a ko. Constructing such positions is a craft. Real game records often look like they have independent regions but actually have one or two connecting threads that the analysis has to be careful about. The Berlekamp school spent the early 1990s developing techniques to identify and exploit clean decomposition in real games, and one of the surprises was that more late-stage positions decompose cleanly than people had assumed.

Switches and other simple hot games. The three fights in our example were all switches — the pure form . Real Go fights are sometimes switches, sometimes more complicated objects with mixed integer-and-fraction components, sometimes loopy positions involving ko (which is a different beast — explainer 15 will say a little about loopy games). The full theory generalizes well past switches, but the further you go from switches the less the temperature is a single number — it becomes a function of how cool the rest of the board has become, and you need thermographs.

Why other games are harder. The same temperature-theoretic toolkit applies in principle to any partizan game that decomposes. In practice, Go has the unusual feature that its endgame is naturally a sum of small components — most other games that humans play are not like this. Chess, for example, almost never decomposes; the king is a single piece on one board and every move is potentially relevant to every threat. Hex (Explainer 12) does not decompose either: it is one connected board where the threats go corner to corner. The CGT approach to game endings works best for games where the geometry forces independence, and Go is the canonical example.

Honesty noteWhat we have done is not a Go analysis. It is a CGT exercise dressed in Go costume. Real Go positions, even genuinely decomposable endgame ones, have larger and more varied local games than our three switches. Worked examples in Berlekamp and Wolfe's book run for chapters; some real-game endgames they analyze contain dozens of components with temperatures spanning two orders of magnitude in fractional units.

Treat the example here as the simplest non-trivial application of the temperature theory we built in Explainer 10 — enough to convince yourself the machinery is real and points at something true, but not enough to claim you have learned Go endgame analysis.

Berlekamp and the rise of CGT in Go

Combinatorial game theory and Go found each other in the late 1980s, primarily through Elwyn Berlekamp.

Title page layout of Berlekamp & Wolfe's Mathematical Go: Chilling Gets the Last Point, rendered from text. A K Peters, first edition 1994.

Elwyn Berlekamp (1940–2019)

Mathematical Go: Chilling Gets the Last Point.
Berlekamp & Wolfe, A K Peters 1994. (Also: numerous research papers 1991–2018.)

Berlekamp was an electrical engineer and information theorist by training, with a doctorate from MIT and a Berkeley professorship in EE and mathematics. He co-authored Winning Ways with Conway and Guy in 1982 and spent much of his subsequent career applying CGT to specific games — Dots and Boxes, Amazons, and most famously Go. The Go work was unusual because the games CGT had treated successfully up to that point were small toys; Go was not.

Berlekamp's insight was that CGT did not need to solve Go all at once. The endgame of Go often decomposes, the resulting local games are often simple enough to compute, and the temperature theory says exactly how to combine them. He produced positions where his CGT analysis correctly predicted the optimal move when high-ranked professional players had played sub-optimally — and, in some famous cases, found endgame moves that improved on the moves played in published professional games.

The 1994 book with David Wolfe is the standard reference. It develops the theory of "chilling" (a technical operation closely related to cooling, applied so that terminal positions normalize correctly for Go scoring) and works through long chains of examples. A second book, Games of No Chance (1996, edited by Nowakowski), collects related research papers from the wider community Berlekamp had inspired.

Figure 4. The historical thread connecting CGT to Go runs through Elwyn Berlekamp and the 1990s. Before this, "Go is too complicated for math" was the consensus in the math-of-games community; after, Go endgames became a paradigmatic application of partizan game theory.

If you want to read further

  • Berlekamp & Wolfe — Mathematical Go: Chilling Gets the Last Point (A K Peters 1994). The complete development. Starts with chilling, builds the local-value calculus, and works through dozens of positions.
  • Berlekamp, Conway & Guy — Winning Ways, vol. 2 (A K Peters 2003), chapters on temperature and on Go-style applications. The original temperature material is in vol. 1; the Go connection appears in vol. 2.
  • Müller — surveys on computer Go and CGT, particularly "Computer Go" (Artificial Intelligence 134, 2002). Locates CGT's contribution within the broader history of Go programs.
  • Siegel — Combinatorial Game Theory (AMS 2013), chapters on temperature and switches, for the modern formal treatment of the underlying theory.
  • Spight — accessible online articles on Go endgame analysis and CGT, written by a Go player who learned the theory from Berlekamp.

Takeaways

The CGT approach to Go endgames is not "use math to play Go." It is "find the parts of Go where the math we have actually applies, and use it there." Two things are doing work:

  • Decomposition. A Go endgame breaks into independent local fights. This is a property of late-stage Go that does not generalize to most other games.
  • Temperature ordering. Once we have independent components, the temperature theory from Explainer 10 tells us exactly which one to play first. We did not have to invent a new theorem — we just had to recognize that a Go endgame is the situation the theorem was built for.

Three things we are not claiming, in case the chapter has been too triumphalist:

  • We are not claiming CGT solves full-board Go. It does not. CGT solves Go endgames that decompose into computable components, which is a subset of late-game positions.
  • We are not claiming temperature theory beats human professionals across the board. It beats human professionals on specific constructed and identified positions where the decomposition is clean and the local games are small enough to compute. On positions where the decomposition is muddy, professionals play better than the theory.
  • We are not claiming CGT is the right framework for game-AI work in general. Modern computer Go is dominated by Monte Carlo tree search and neural networks (AlphaGo, KataGo) which do not use CGT at all. CGT and search-based AI are complementary tools that target different parts of the game.

The honest summary is that combinatorial game theory gave Go endgame analysis a new vocabulary and, in Berlekamp's hands, a new set of techniques that occasionally outperformed top human play on constructed positions. That is more than most branches of math have achieved in any game humans actually play, and it is a long way from solving Go.
