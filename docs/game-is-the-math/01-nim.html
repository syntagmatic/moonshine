Nim

Part 1 of 15. Three heaps on a table. Take what you want from one heap on each turn. The player who takes the last object wins. It looks like arithmetic has nothing to say about it. Arithmetic has everything to say about it.

The rules of Nim fit in a sentence. Several heaps of objects sit on a table. Two players alternate turns. On each turn, the player picks one heap and removes any positive number of objects from it — one, two, all of them, doesn't matter, as long as it is at least one and comes from a single heap. Whoever takes the last object wins.

The computer moves second, and the computer has read the rest of this explainer. Lose a few times on purpose. The losing is the point.

If you played naturally, the computer probably kept winning. That is not a bug. Most of the "obvious" first moves from (3, 5, 7) — turn the 7 into a 6, turn the 5 into a 4, take the whole 3-heap — land in a position where it is now impossible for you to win against correct play. From the reader's seat, losing looks random; from the computer's seat, losing is a choice you made on move one.

Now click "Tutorial (1, 2, 3)." The starting position is smaller and simpler and, crucially, it is worse. From (1, 2, 3), you cannot win at all. Every move you make — every one, we checked — gives the computer a position from which it can march to victory. Try. You will lose. Try again with a different first move. You will lose. The difference between Marienbad (3, 5, 7) and the tutorial (1, 2, 3) is that from Marienbad there exists a winning move for you, and from (1, 2, 3) there does not.

That is a strong distinction and it deserves a name. We call a position losing (for the player to move) if every legal move leads to a winning position for the opponent, and winning (for the player to move) if at least one move leads to a losing position for the opponent. The terminal position — all heaps empty — is losing, because the player to move has no legal moves and by convention has already lost (the previous player took the last stone).

The definitions look circular, but they are not: we can work them out position by position, starting from the empty board and building up. A position is losing iff it has only winning options; a position is winning iff it has at least one losing option. There is no third category. From the Marienbad start, you have winning moves — at least one exists, and the rest of this explainer is about finding them.

Where are the losing positions?

We have claimed (1, 2, 3) is losing and (3, 5, 7) is winning, and we want to know which other positions are which. The honest move is to compute — losing means "every option is winning" and winning means "at least one option is losing," and we can resolve every small position by backward induction from the empty board. Binary unlocks the pattern

Write each heap in binary. For the losing tutorial position (1, 2, 3):

Stack the rows and count the 1s in each column. Column 4 (the 4s place) has no 1s — 0, even. Column 2 (the 2s place) has two 1s (from 2 = 010 and 3 = 011) — 2, even. Column 1 (the 1s place) has two 1s (from 1 = 001 and 3 = 011) — 2, even. Every column has an even number of 1s. The whole position is "all columns even."

Now look at Marienbad (3, 5, 7):

Column 4: from 5 = 101 and 7 = 111, two 1s — even. Column 2: from 3 = 011 and 7 = 111, two 1s — even. Column 1: from 3, 5, and 7 all ending in 1, three 1s — odd. Two columns even, one column odd. The position is not all-columns-even, so by our provisional rule it should be winning for the player to move — and indeed, we claimed above that Marienbad is winning for you.

One more check. (2, 4, 6) = 010, 100, 110. Column 4 has two 1s (from 4 and 6) — even. Column 2 has two 1s (from 2 and 6) — even. Column 1 has zero 1s — even. Every column is even. The rule predicts this is losing. And indeed, look back at Figure 2 with the slider at c = 6: the cell (2, 4) is colored red.

The pattern fits: losing positions are exactly the positions where every column of the binary rows has an even number of 1s. "Every column even" is equivalent to saying the bitwise XOR of the heap sizes is zero — each bit of the XOR is exactly the column-count mod 2. Many heaps

Nothing in the argument used the fact that there were three heaps. The same theorem applies to any number of heaps. The step from three heaps to eight is trivial because the theorem is pointwise about columns of the binary representation. The XOR does not care how many rows you stack; each column's parity is independent. This kind of structural invariance is one of the things that makes combinatorial game theory beautiful — we will see it again and again, in forms that look less like arithmetic and more like algebra, but the same pattern-that-survives-decomposition shape is always there.

Charles Bouton, 1901

Nim is old as a pastime — variants were played in China for centuries before the West noticed. What is not old is the mathematics of it.

Facsimile of the title page layout of Bouton's 1901 paper, rendered from text. Original ran in the Annals of Mathematics, second series, volume 3.

Charles Leonard Bouton (1869–1922)

Nim, A Game with a Complete Mathematical Theory.
Annals of Mathematics, Series 2, Vol. 3, No. 1/4 (1901), pp. 35–39.

Bouton was an associate professor of mathematics at Harvard. He had been a student of Felix Klein at Göttingen and wrote his doctoral thesis on groups of transformations. In 1901 he published a short paper giving the complete analysis of Nim — the theorem above, its proof by induction, and the observation that the whole game reduces to binary arithmetic. His title used the phrase "complete mathematical theory" deliberately: the game has a closed-form answer, not just a search procedure.

The paper is five pages long. It invents notation for positions, distinguishes "safe" (what we called losing) from "unsafe" positions, and closes with the remark that the same analysis can be extended to more than three heaps — which we just did in Figure 4.

The 1901 paper is the earliest result in what would become combinatorial game theory. Sprague and Grundy extended the idea in the 1930s, showing that every impartial game behaves like a Nim heap of some size. Conway took it further in the 1970s with partizan games and surreal numbers. All of that still lies ahead of us. For now, what we have is Bouton's theorem — a single XOR and a game that used to look intractable folding up into five pages.

Takeaways

Nim's whole theory fits in a sentence: the position is losing iff the XOR of the heap sizes is zero. The proof fits in two inductive steps. The optimal strategy is a three-line algorithm: compute the XOR, find a heap with a 1 in the XOR's top bit, reduce it to heap-XOR-with-total-XOR.

Three things we will reuse in every later explainer:

  • Losing-positions-are-a-set. The distinction between winning and losing positions is an invariant property of a game, checkable without looking at how the game got there. The next explainer generalizes this from Nim to any impartial game.
  • XOR as structure, not trick. The XOR is not a coincidence — it is the Grundy value of a Nim heap, and under the Sprague–Grundy theorem (Explainer 4) it extends to every impartial game. Nim is not just a game with a complete theory; in a precise sense, it is the game with a complete theory, and every other impartial game is a sum of disguised Nim heaps.
  • Play first, theorem second. We asked you to play Marienbad and the tutorial before we gave you the XOR rule. The losing is what made the theorem inevitable — first the feeling that the computer is doing something you could do, then the pattern, then the proof. We will keep doing it this way.
