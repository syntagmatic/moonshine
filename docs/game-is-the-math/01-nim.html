Nim

Part 1 of 10. Three heaps on a table. On your turn, take any number of objects from one heap. Whoever takes the last object wins. The whole game turns out to be a question about binary arithmetic.

Two players alternate. On each turn a player picks one heap and removes at least one object from it, up to the whole heap. Whoever takes the last object wins.

From you have 15 legal first moves, and exactly three of them win: take one from any heap, reaching (2, 5, 7), (3, 4, 7) or (3, 5, 6). The other twelve hand the computer a win. Now try (1, 2, 3). There, every one of your moves loses.

Call a position losing (for the player to move) if every move leads to a winning position, and winning if some move leads to a losing position. The empty board is losing: the player to move has no move, because the opponent just took the last object. Every other position can be classified by working upward from the empty board, since each move makes the heaps smaller.

Charles Bouton, 1901

Charles L. Bouton, a mathematician at Harvard, published the analysis above as "Nim, a Game with a Complete Mathematical Theory" in the Annals of Mathematics (second series, vol. 3, 1901, pp. 35–39). He called the losing positions "safe combinations", since they are safe to leave for your opponent, and gave the game its name. The paper is usually counted as the first result of combinatorial game theory. In the 1930s Sprague and Grundy showed independently that every finite impartial game is equivalent to a single Nim heap (Part 3).
