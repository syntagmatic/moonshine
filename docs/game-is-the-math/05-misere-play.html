Misère Play

Part 5 of 15. Flip the win condition — last move loses — and watch the theory bend. Misère Nim is almost normal Nim, with one endgame twist. Misère Hackenbush is a research area. This is where Act I finds its edge.

Every game we have played so far has used the normal-play convention: whoever makes the last legal move wins. In Nim you scramble to be the player who empties the final heap; in Green Hackenbush you want to chop the last remaining stick; in the Sprague–Grundy theorem the entire theory rests on "a position with no moves available is a loss for the player to move." Normal play is the default for a reason — it plays nicely with mex, with XOR, with game sums. Everything lines up.

Flip the rule. In misère play, the player who makes the last move loses. Out loud: you are trying to force your opponent into the last move, not to make it yourself. The mechanics of each game are unchanged — you still take from Nim heaps, still chop from Hackenbush trees — only the win condition flips. It sounds like a small tweak.

It is not a small tweak. For almost every game, misère play demolishes the clean algebraic structure we just built. The Sprague–Grundy theorem does not survive. Game sums do not behave. Misère theory is still an active research area, with its own name ("misère quotients"), its own toolkit, and open problems that serious people work on today. But there is one game where misère play is almost the same as normal play, and that one game is the one we learned on: Nim.

This explainer walks that one exception carefully. It also shows where the walk ends — where a simple-looking game jumps from "one line of strategy" to "nobody has a clean theory." That honesty is the shape of Act I's ending.

Where does the divergence happen? Usually only once, and usually only when a single big heap remains. Until that moment, both computers play identical XOR-zeroing moves — misère Nim and normal Nim have literally the same optimal strategy as long as at least two heaps contain two or more objects. The divergence is a one-move correction to the endgame. Play a few games and the pattern surfaces on its own.

The endgame: when every heap is 0 or 1

Start from the end. Suppose the position has collapsed so that every heap contains either 0 or 1 object. Call a heap of size 1 a "one" and count how many ones there are — call that number . Nothing else is left.

Under normal play, the rule is ruthless: whoever is to move on a position with any ones at all can just take a one, and as long as the game continues. The player who takes the final one wins. So "one position" with ones is a win for the player to move iff , and a loss iff (no moves left, you just lost).

Under misère play, the rule flips. Taking the final one loses. Now the player to move wants to not be the one taking the last one. The base case flips too: at there are no moves available, which under misère means your opponent just took the last one and handed themselves the loss — you win on arrival. With , the parity of decides everything. If is odd, you are doomed: you take a one, leaving an even number, your opponent takes another, leaving an odd number, and this ping-pongs until the last-bead loss lands on you. If is even, you are fine: your opponent takes first and inherits the losing odd count.

The theorem, stated precisely

The misère Nim theorem says that the entire game is governed by the endgame rule above plus the XOR rule we already know — and which rule applies depends on whether the position has any "fat" heap (a heap of size 2 or more). It is one of those theorems where the statement is longer than the proof, once you believe the statement.

Misère Nim theorem

In plain English: play the standard XOR-zero strategy for as long as any heap still holds two or more objects. The instant the position is about to collapse to all ones, switch your objective from "leave XOR zero" to "leave an odd number of ones." That single-sentence switch is the misère-vs-normal difference.

Play: Misère Marienbad

Marienbad is the name commonly given to the Nim starting position (1, 3, 5, 7), popularised by Alain Resnais's 1961 film Last Year at Marienbad where two characters play across a table. In the film the game is played misère: the player who takes the last match loses. The rest of the film would have been shorter if one of them had known the theorem.

Where misère stops being clean

For Nim alone, misère play is one corrected endgame away from normal play. Every book on combinatorial game theory spends a few pages on this correction and moves on. The temptation is to assume the same kind of correction works everywhere — that "misère = normal + one endgame patch" is a general tool. It is not.

Consider Green Hackenbush. Under normal play, a Green Hackenbush position has a single Grundy value, the Sprague–Grundy theorem applies, and the game decomposes cleanly into sums. Under misère play, the Grundy value is not enough information to decide who wins. Even pure Nim already shows this: the positions (1, 1) and (2, 2) both have Grundy value 0, yet under misère (1, 1) is winning for the player to move and (2, 2) is losing. Any invariant strong enough to decide misère outcomes has to distinguish them. For Green Hackenbush the situation is worse: there is no compact replacement for the Grundy value. Sprague–Grundy, which was Act I's crown jewel, simply does not hold in the misère world.

Three Green Hackenbush positions of increasing structural complexity. Under normal play, the Sprague–Grundy theorem reduces each to a single Grundy value and then to Nim. Under misère play, only the leftmost (pure stalks) retains that reduction.

Figure 5. Three Green Hackenbush positions, from tractable misère to research area. Left: two stalks, which under misère is literally the same as misère Nim on the stalk lengths — the theorem from the previous figure applies. Middle: a Y-tree, where the branching means the Colon Principle (which reduces trees to Nim under normal play) does not carry over to misère. Right: a loop with an attached stem, where normal-play theory uses the Fusion Principle to contract the loop; that reduction fails under misère. Misère Green Hackenbush in general has no compact closed-form outcome rule — the honest answer is that you analyse each position directly, or work inside a misère quotient (which we are not going to develop here).

Honesty moment. Misère Hackenbush, misère Domineering, misère Sprouts — most misère games do not have a tidy closed-form theory. The right way to think about misère play, and the way that research has taken in the last twenty years, is through misère quotients: for each specific rule set, you quotient the universe of positions by an equivalence relation that preserves misère outcome under sums, and you work inside that quotient. Some games have finite misère quotients; some do not. Nim's misère quotient turns out to be tiny — just the modification we saw — which is why misère Nim has a one-paragraph theorem. The general theory is a moving target, and it is a target we are not going to hit in this series.

The Winning Ways chapter on this (Volume 2, chapter 13: "Survival in the Lost World") is the classic reference. Thane Plambeck's work on misère quotients, starting around 2005, is the modern continuation.

End of Act I

Act I started with a child's game played with matchsticks and ended with a research area the authors of Winning Ways christened "the Lost World." The journey in between was every layer of the normal-play theory: Nim, Green Hackenbush, mex and Grundy values, the Sprague–Grundy theorem that unifies them. We can compute Grundy values by hand, reduce any impartial game to Nim on the fly, and play a dozen small impartial games competently.

Normal play is one half of the combinatorial game theory universe. The other half is partizan games — games in which Left and Right have different moves available from the same position. Grundy values are inadequate for partizan games. In their place comes a much richer object called a game value, and among the game values are hidden the surreal numbers — Conway's construction of every real number (and every transfinite ordinal) from the bare bones of Left and Right play. Act II picks the story up there.
