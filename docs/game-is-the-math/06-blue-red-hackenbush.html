Blue-Red Hackenbush

Part 6 of 15. The first partizan game. Left chops blue, Right chops red, and the answer to "what is this position worth" is no longer a non-negative integer. It is a fraction. By the end of this explainer, half of an edge will exist.

Every game in Act I was impartial. Both players had the same moves available from any position: in Nim either player could take from any heap, in Green Hackenbush either player could chop any green edge. The Sprague–Grundy theorem we built up to in explainer 4 said something strong about that universe: every impartial game is, in disguise, a Nim heap of some size. We labeled positions with a single non-negative integer, the Grundy value, and that integer told us the entire story.

Today we change one rule and the universe changes underneath us. The board now has two colors of edges. Left, by convention drawn in blue, may chop blue edges only. Right, drawn in red, may chop red edges only. As before, gravity is in force: every edge no longer connected to the ground falls. As before, the player who cannot move loses (normal play). The only thing we have done is split the moves between the two players.

That single change makes Grundy values inadequate. There is no longer "the player to move" in the abstract, the moves of Left and Right diverge, and a position can favor one player over the other. The thing we will compute instead is a game value: a signed quantity whose magnitude says how strongly one side leads, and whose sign says which side. Positive means Left is ahead, negative means Right is ahead, zero means the second player wins.

The values we are about to find are not all integers. The first surprising one is , and it really is half of an edge, in the precise sense that two copies of the position add up to a single blue edge. Conway's construction of the surreal numbers, the subject of the next explainer, is what gives this fraction a home. Today we discover the fraction by play.

What an integer position looks like

Before we talk about fractions, let us talk about positions where the answer is an honest integer, because they are the easy case. A stalk of blue edges and nothing else has value . A stalk of red edges and nothing else has value . Every blue edge is a free move for Left, every red edge is a free move for Right, and the difference between the two counts is the lead.

The reason this works is "tempo." Every Hackenbush move uses one turn for one player. If Left has blue edges of moves and Right has nothing to play, Right runs out of moves immediately and Left wins by moves. We say the position has value . The same logic in reverse: red edges and no blues is value .

Mixed-color stalks complicate this story because chopping an edge can take edges of the other color with it. A blue edge underneath a red one keeps that red edge attached to the ground; if Left chops the blue, the red falls, and Right loses a move. Now the lead is no longer just (blues − reds), Left can use a single blue chop to delete reds. The accounting becomes more delicate, and that delicacy is where the fractions come from.

Sums of games, two stalks at a time

A sum of two Hackenbush positions is the disjoint union: two boards drawn side by side, sharing only the ground. A move in the sum is a move on either board. The player to move chooses which board to move on, and then makes a legal move on that board. The game ends when no legal move exists on either board.

The BR + BR sum is the test of the 1/2 claim. Each component, on its own, is worth 1/2. Their sum is worth 1, which is the same as a single blue edge, and indeed, in any sum-of-games sense, BR + BR is the same game as a single blue edge: in any combined position involving BR + BR, you may swap it out for a single blue and the winner does not change. We will prove this rigorously in explainer 8 when we state the simplicity theorem; today, you can convince yourself by building the position BR + RB (one of each) with the buttons below and watching the readout: the value drops to zero. The two halves cancel because RB, the color-swap of BR, has value -1/2. Sums and negations of game values behave exactly the way sums and negations of numbers do.

How the computer is playing. For each Right move under consideration, the computer computes the value of the resulting sum and picks the move that minimizes that value. For each Left move under consideration (during the hint feature in Figure 1), it picks the move that maximizes the value. This is just "best-response play" against the partizan game value. Because the values are exact dyadic rationals computed by the simplicity rule, the computer is playing optimally, there is no search and no heuristic.

From integers to dyadics: where the values live

The values we have seen so far are all dyadic rationals, fractions whose denominator is a power of 2. That is not an accident. A Blue-Red Hackenbush position whose game tree has bounded depth has a value with a power-of-two denominator, and the bound on the denominator grows with the depth of the tree. A position of depth can produce a value with denominator at most .

The simplicity rule explains why. When you compute for a position whose Left and Right options have already been computed, you look for the simplest dyadic in an open interval . The "simplest" rule prefers integers; if there is no integer strictly between, it prefers half-integers; then quarters; then eighths; and so on. Each level of the recursion can introduce at most one new factor of 2 in the denominator, so the denominator stays controlled.

The simplicity rule also explains why the values are all numbers in the ordinary sense, and not stranger objects like , the impartial game value we met under the name "Grundy 1", that show up in fully impartial games. Blue-Red Hackenbush is so committed to the Left/Right asymmetry that no impartial structure ever arises. Every position is a number. We will see games later (Domineering in explainer 9, temperature theory in explainer 10) where the values are not all numbers, and the contrast will be sharp.

The integers we already understood are sitting inside this number system as the special case "all one color." The fractions are new, and they correspond to positions where the colors interact, where chopping one color removes edges of the other color along with it.

Why these are numbers and not just labels

A reasonable suspicion at this point is that we are just slapping fractional labels on positions for bookkeeping reasons, and that "1/2" is not really half of anything, it is just a name. The reason the labels really are numbers is that they obey arithmetic.

Specifically, the following four facts hold for Blue-Red Hackenbush positions and their game values:

  • Sums add. for any two positions and . We saw this in Figure 4.
  • Negation flips colors. , where is the same position with all edge colors swapped. A pure red stalk of length is the negation of a pure blue stalk of length , and indeed has value .
  • Inverses cancel. for any position . So BR added to its color-swap RB is a draw, value 0, second player wins. Verify this in Figure 4 by building BR and RB and playing the sum.
  • Comparison is consistent. If two positions have values and with , then any third position has the property that the winner of is at most as good for Left as the winner of . The numerical order on values matches the game-theoretic order on positions.

Each of these is a real theorem, and together they say that the assignment "position → game value" is a homomorphism from the additive monoid of game positions into the additive group of dyadic rationals. The values are not labels. They are arithmetic.

The cleanest proof of all four facts uses the formal definition of the surreal numbers, which is the subject of the next explainer. Conway's construction starts by saying "a number is a pair of sets of numbers, with every Left element less than every Right element," and runs a transfinite recursion that produces, in order, the integers, the dyadic rationals, all real numbers, and then beyond into the transfinite. Blue-Red Hackenbush stalks turn out to be the most concrete way to see the early stages of that construction. Every position we have built today is a surreal number under a different name.

Takeaways

Three things to carry forward into the rest of Act II.

  • Partizan games have signed values. A position has a single number attached to it. Positive favors Left, negative favors Right, zero is a second-player win. The value is computed recursively from the positions Left and Right can move to, via the Conway form .
  • Simplicity rule (working version). When every Left option's value is strictly less than every Right option's value, the position's value is the simplest dyadic rational strictly between the largest Left option and the smallest Right option. "Simplest" means smallest power-of-two denominator, then closest to zero. We will prove this theorem in explainer 8.
  • Values add. The disjoint sum of two positions has value equal to the sum of the values. Negation swaps colors. The arithmetic of partizan game values is just dyadic-rational arithmetic.

The explainer you have just played through gave you four kinds of value: positive integers (pure blue), negative integers (pure red), zero (no edges or a perfectly balanced sum), and dyadic fractions (mixed stalks). The next explainer takes the same recipe and runs it to its conclusion. Conway's surreal numbers are the universal answer to "what number system do partizan game values live in?", and their construction recovers (among other things) every real number. We will start from the empty board, build the surreal number 0, then build 1 and -1 and 1/2, and watch the entire dyadic rational line materialize out of pure Left/Right options.
