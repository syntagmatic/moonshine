Temperature and Cooling

Part 8 of 10. Some games are hot: both players want the next move there. Others are cold numbers that nobody wants to touch. Temperature measures how much the next move is worth, and in a sum of hot games it tells you where to play.

The 2×2 Domineering board from Part 7 is {1 | −1}: if Left moves, what remains is worth +1; if Right moves, −1. Both players want to be the one who moves. Numbers are the opposite. In a position worth exactly 2, every move either passes the advantage to the opponent or wastes a turn, so neither player wants to move there.

The temperature puts a number on "wants to move", and it helps decide where to play in a sum of hot games, the situation a Go player faces in the endgame.

The value a game reaches when Left moves first and both sides then play well is its Left stop, L(G); with Right moving first it is the Right stop, R(G). For a hot switch these are just a and b. Their average is the mean, and half their gap is the temperature:

The mean is what the position is worth on average if a coin decides who moves. The temperature is how far each player gains, relative to that mean, by being the one to move. For {6 | −2} the mean is 2 and the temperature is 4: moving first lands Left 4 above the mean, and Right 4 below it.

The cold cases behave differently. With a < b, each player's move helps the opponent, so nobody moves; {a | b} is then a number, the simplest one strictly between a and b (the simplicity rule from Part 6). {1 | 4} is 2, not the average 2.5. With a = b the game is a + ∗: both stops equal a, the temperature is 0, and the only thing at stake is who gets the last move.

Cooling: taxing every move

The name comes from an operation called cooling. Cooling a game by t charges each player t points for every move they make:

Numbers are left alone, since nobody wants to move in them anyway. A cooled switch is {a − t | b + t}: Left's reward shrinks, Right's does too, and the two stops walk toward each other. At t = (a − b)/2 they meet at the mean, the move is no longer worth its tax, and the game freezes. Its temperature is the tax at which it freezes.

Plotting the cooled game's stops against t gives the thermograph. For a switch it is a triangle standing on the value axis with a vertical mast above it. The height of the apex is the temperature; the mast stands at the mean.

The standard convention in the literature flips the value axis so that Left's wall is on the left; here values increase to the right.

Switches are the only games whose thermographs are this simple. For a game with deeper options the walls are computed recursively from the options' own cooled values, and they bend where a different option becomes the best one to take. The thermographs of real Domineering and Go positions are staircases of such segments.

In {6 | −2} + {2 | 0}, Left moving first takes 6 in the hot switch, Right answers by taking 0 in the other, and the total is 6. Taking the 2 first instead lets Right take −2, for a total of 0. With Right moving first the total is 0 by the same argument. So the stops of the sum are 6 and 0, around a mean of 2 + 1 = 3. Moving first is worth 3 points relative to the mean, and 3 = 4 − 1: the mover collects the hottest temperature, and the opponent answers with the next one.

and playing in the hottest remaining switch on every turn achieves them for both players. A short induction on the number of switches proves it. As a check we compared exhaustive minimax, this formula and hottest-first play on 40,000 random sums of up to five hot switches, and all three agreed every time.

Outside switches the rule weakens. A move in a general game can create a new threat or change the temperature of what is left, and then playing in the hottest component can be the wrong choice. Hottest-first is a good heuristic for general sums, not a theorem. Berlekamp's refinements, such as the strategy he called Thermostrat, use the whole thermograph of each component and come with bounds on how much they can lose.

Go endgames

Late in a game of Go the board often splits into regions separated by settled groups, where a stone in one region cannot affect the outcome in another. The whole position is then the sum of the regional games, and each region's local value can be worked out on its own. This decomposition made Go the main application of the theory.

The "Three Go fights" preset in Figure 3 is an idealized example: one fight where the first player to move gains two points either way, {2 | −2}; one worth a point either way, {1 | −1}; and a lopsided one where Black can take a point and White can only deny it, {1 | 0}. With Black (Left) moving first, hottest-first gives +2 − 1 + 1 = +2, which matches the formula: means 0 + 0 + ½ plus the alternating sum 2 − 1 + ½. Opening in the coolest fight instead gives Black +1 − 2 + 1 = 0, two points worse.

Go players have two conventions for the size of a gote move. "Deiri" counting uses the full difference between the two local outcomes. "Miai" counting uses half of it, which for a switch is exactly the temperature.

Berlekamp and David Wolfe carried the theory much further in Mathematical Go: Chilling Gets the Last Point (1994). Their key tool, chilling, is essentially cooling by one point. Chilled, many late endgame positions become numbers and infinitesimals that add exactly, which settles fights down to the last point where ordinary counting cannot. The approach has limits. Regions must truly be independent, and ko fights make positions loopy, which the theory of sums above does not cover. Strong Go programs today use search and neural networks rather than this theory.

Cooling and thermographs were introduced by Conway in On Numbers and Games (1976) and developed in Winning Ways (1982) by Berlekamp, Conway and Guy.
