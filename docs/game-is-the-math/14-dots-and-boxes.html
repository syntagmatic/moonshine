Dots and Boxes

Part 14 of 15. Two players, a grid of dots, a pencil. Take turns drawing one edge between adjacent dots; whoever closes the fourth side of a unit square claims that box and goes again. Children play this on the back of restaurant menus. Berlekamp wrote a book about it. The book is right.

Everybody who went to school has played this. The dots sit on the page in a small grid; two of you trade turns drawing one of the segments between adjacent dots; if your segment closes a one-by-one square you claim that square (initials in the middle) and you must immediately move again. When every segment is drawn the game is over, and whoever has more squares wins. The rules are seven sentences and the game can be played anywhere a flat surface and a pen exist.

What schoolchildren almost never realise is that competent Dots and Boxes — the version where both players see what is coming — is a deep game. The end of a Dots and Boxes match is dominated by long thin sequences of boxes called chains, by a precise parity rule that decides the winner from the chain count, and by a counterintuitive technique called the double-cross in which you give boxes away on purpose. None of this is accessible to the typical playground tactic of "claim every box you can." The whole point of the theory is that the playground tactic is wrong, and that knowing why is enough to beat almost anybody who has not read the book.

If you played the 3×3 board with the greedy computer once or twice, you probably won or tied. Three by three is small enough that nothing dramatic happens. Switch to 4×4 and the dynamics change. Now there are sixteen boxes to fight over, and somewhere around move twenty the position locks up: every remaining segment, if drawn, gives away at least one box. From that point onward the game is no longer a question of "who can grab the next square" but of "who is forced to open the next chain."

That is the moment Dots and Boxes becomes the game Berlekamp wrote about. Up to that moment, the game is decided by your willingness not to play stupid moves; after that moment, it is decided by an arithmetic property of the chains you and your opponent collaboratively built without realising it.

Anatomy: chains, loops, and short pieces

A chain in Dots and Boxes is a maximal run of unfinished boxes such that each box (except the two ends) shares an open edge with exactly two neighbours, and the ends are either dead ends or open at one boundary. Stripped of jargon: when the position has frozen up so that any move gives a box away, the unclaimed boxes have organised themselves into long thin strings — that is the chain — plus a few short pieces and possibly some loops.

The figure below shows the four kinds of pieces you will see in any frozen Dots and Boxes endgame. A chain of length 1 is a single isolated box. A chain of length 2 is a domino. Chains of length 3 are still small. Chains of length 4 or more are what the theory cares about — they are the long chains, and the entire endgame turns on how many of them there are.

Why is length 4 the cutoff? Because a chain of length 3 or shorter cannot be double-crossed profitably. The double-cross technique gives up the last two boxes of a chain in exchange for handing the next move to the opponent; on a chain of length 3 you would give up two out of three boxes to keep one, and you are losing money. On a chain of length 4 the trade is two for two, breakeven on that chain but profitable on the next chain. On a chain of length 5 the trade is two for three, profit. Everything above length 4 only gets better. Berlekamp's bookkeeping treats the boundary at 4 as the place where the trade becomes worthwhile, and so do we.

The long chain rule

Here is the result that does the most work in competitive Dots and Boxes — it is the parity rule, sometimes called the chain rule or long chain rule, and it is one sentence long.

The long chain rule. In a Dots and Boxes game played to its end, the first player wants the number of (long chains plus loops) to have a particular parity. With "chain control" — the ability to apply the double-cross — the player who is not forced to open the first long chain wins. The rule pins the favourable parity to the parity of the total dot count: if the number of dots on the board is odd, the first player wants an even number of long chains; if the number of dots is even, the first player wants an odd number of long chains.

The rule is a parity argument and we will sketch it in a moment, but first feel it. Why parity?

The parity argument has two ingredients. First, count moves. A Dots and Boxes board with dots has edges in total: the horizontal edges form an grid and the vertical edges form an grid. (For our 4×4 dot board: 12 horizontal plus 12 vertical equals 24 edges.) The total number of moves played in the game is therefore . Boxes-claimed turns do not count as separate moves: when you close a box you draw an edge as part of that move and then continue with another edge. So the final number of edges drawn is fixed, but the number of turns taken depends on who claims boxes.

Second, count doublecrosses. Each long chain that gets played out under chain control is double-crossed: the controller takes all but the last two boxes of the chain and then plays a "hard-hearted handout," sacrificing the last two boxes back to the opponent in order to force the opponent to open the next chain. This means each long chain consumes one sacrificed pair of boxes from the controller's column and forces the opponent to play the first edge of the next chain. The party who is forced to open the first long chain therefore opens every subsequent chain too, by induction, and ends up taking only the sacrificed pairs while the other party takes the bulk of every chain.

You do not have to memorise the formula; competitive players think of it as "compute the target parity once for the board size, then steer." The deeper point is that chain count is the right thing to steer.

The double-cross technique

The double-cross is the move that makes the long chain rule do work. Without it, "chain control" is just a name; with it, chain control becomes a sequence of forced moves. The figure below walks through it on a single chain of length 5.

Figure 4. The double-cross on a chain of length 5. Your opponent has just opened the chain by drawing the dashed edge — that exposes the leftmost box for you to claim. Greedy play takes all five boxes (plus another move each time), then is forced to open the next chain. Double-cross play takes only the first three boxes, declines the last two by drawing the parallel edge inside the chain (the "hard-hearted handout"), and forces the opponent to take the last two and immediately open the next chain. You score 3 instead of 5 here, but you keep control — and on the next chain (assume length 7) you score 5 instead of 0. Net swing: from −2 to +6. Long chains compound.

The technique looks self-defeating until you do the arithmetic. On a single chain in isolation, greedy is strictly better — five boxes beats three. The double-cross is only worth it because it lets you keep handing the opening move to your opponent on every subsequent chain. The break-even chain length is 4: on a chain of exactly 4 boxes, double-crossing is 2 boxes for you and 2 for them, no worse than greedy (where you take all 4 and then open the next chain). On chains longer than 4, double-crossing is profitable. On chains of length 3 or shorter, just take everything.

This is the missing ingredient. The long chain rule says which player should be in control, the double-cross is the technique that maintains control once you have it. You can verify both in Figure 1: switch the computer to "chain-aware," play a 4×4 game, and watch what happens around move 18 or 20. When the position freezes, the chain-aware computer counts long chains, decides whether it should be opening or being opened, and either pours moves into manipulating the chain count or starts playing pre-emptive double-crosses.

Honesty moment. The chain-aware computer in Figure 1 is a heuristic, not optimal play. It detects long chains in the frozen subgraph, applies double-crosses when control is profitable, and avoids being the first to open a long chain when it can. But it does not search the full game tree, it does not handle loops with the loopy double-double-cross, and it will lose to a careful human in some 5×5 positions. Truly optimal Dots and Boxes is hard: the game on a 4×4 board (16 boxes) was first solved by computer in the 1990s; the 5×5 game was solved in 2002 by Joseph Barker and Richard Korf and the answer is a draw with optimal play; the 6×6 game (which the original 1889 puzzle book used) is at the edge of what is known.

Our heuristic plays well enough to feel the long chain rule and to demonstrate the double-cross. It is not the computer that beats Berlekamp.

Why is this so much harder than Nim?

Nim's whole theory fits in a sentence (Bouton: ). Dots and Boxes does not. Why?

The structural answer is that Dots and Boxes is a scoring game, not a last-player-loses game. The Sprague–Grundy machinery from earlier in this series classifies positions of impartial last-player-wins games into Grundy values; for a scoring game the relevant theory is the theory of partizan games and surreal numbers (Act II), and even that is not the full story for Dots and Boxes. The opening and middle of a Dots and Boxes game is dominated by the question of which player can force the chain count to land on the right parity, which is itself a kind of impartial subgame layered on top of a scoring game. The endgame, after the position freezes, behaves like a sum of "chains" each of which has a value that depends on whether the player has chain control or not. Berlekamp's The Dots and Boxes Game develops a calculus of these chain values that is recognisable as combinatorial game theory but is more bespoke than the elegant universal machinery of Nim or even the surreal numbers.

Why is it not in Winning Ways the same way Nim is? The short answer is that Winning Ways does discuss it (Volume 2, Chapter 16: "Dots-and-Boxes"), but the chapter is much longer than the Nim chapter, and Berlekamp followed up with an entire dedicated 1982 book because the full theory needs more room than even Winning Ways could comfortably give it. Dots and Boxes is the canonical example in the series of "a children's game whose theory turns out to be a research area." Hex is a children's game with a one-line theorem; Nim is a children's game with a five-page paper; Dots and Boxes is a children's game with a book.

Berlekamp was an electrical engineer who became one of the founders of combinatorial game theory. With John Conway and Richard Guy he wrote Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays, the four-volume work that organised the field and is the source the rest of this series leans on. He spent nearly forty years thinking about Dots and Boxes specifically, returning to it again and again, and ultimately decided the game deserved a book of its own.

The book is short — under 150 pages — and almost every page contains a position to play through, a chain to count, or a worked example of the long chain rule and the double-cross. The opening chapters develop exactly what we have sketched in this explainer: the rules, the chain anatomy, the parity argument, the double-cross. The middle chapters develop a calculus of chain values that lets a player evaluate complex frozen positions by hand. The closing chapters treat openings and middles, where the question is no longer how to play the chains but how to manipulate the chain count itself.

Berlekamp also ran an annual undergraduate Dots and Boxes tournament at Berkeley for many years. The tournament rules were the standard ones we have used; the players who won repeatedly were the ones who had read the book. That is the cleanest endorsement a piece of game theory can get.

Takeaways

Dots and Boxes is the moment in this series where a children's game refuses to fold up neatly. Nim has a five-page complete theory; Hex has a one-line existence proof; Dots and Boxes has a 145-page book and an open research frontier. The reason is that scoring games on grids do not flatten into a single Grundy value or a single surreal number. The right object is a chain decomposition of the frozen endgame, controlled by a parity argument and maintained by the double-cross.

Three things to carry forward:

  • The long chain rule. When the position freezes, count the long chains (length 4+) plus loops. The first player wants this count to have a particular parity that depends only on the board size. Whichever player has the favourable parity will, with chain control, take the bulk of every chain.
  • The double-cross. On any chain of length 4 or more, the controller takes all but the last two boxes and gives the last two back. This sacrifice is what maintains chain control through subsequent chains. On loops the analogous move is the double-double-cross, sacrificing four boxes instead of two.
  • Scoring games are different. Dots and Boxes does not fit cleanly inside the Sprague–Grundy or surreal-number worlds we built in Acts I and II. The theory exists, it is precise, it is correct — but it is bespoke to the game. That is allowed; combinatorial game theory has room for both universal machinery and per-game craft, and many of the most interesting real games (Go, Dots and Boxes, Amazons) live in the per-game corner.

The next and final explainer turns the corner from games we can analyse to the wider universe of games combinatorial game theory has not finished mapping yet. Loopy games — games where positions can repeat indefinitely — break some of the assumptions we have been quietly relying on, and the standard references stop being one-volume textbooks and become research papers. We will close the series there.
