Green Hackenbush

Part 2 of 10. A game played on drawings of plants turns out to be Nim in disguise, and proving it gives us our first precise sense of two games being the same.

Green Hackenbush is played on a drawing of green edges, some of them rooted in a ground line. On your turn you chop any edge. Every edge that is no longer connected to the ground through surviving edges falls away. Whoever removes the last edge wins. The game is John Conway's, and it looks nothing like Nim. It is Nim.

A stalk is a heap

The simplest position is a stalk: a vertical chain of edges standing on the ground. Chopping the edge at height , counting from the ground, removes it and everything above, leaving a stalk of length . So from a stalk of length you can reach a stalk of any length from 0 to , which is exactly the list of heaps you can reach from a Nim heap of size . A garden of stalks

Several stalks side by side make a garden, and a move chops one edge of one stalk. That is Nim on the stalk lengths, move for move. The computer below plays the garden by reducing each stalk to its length, running Bouton's rule from Part 1, and translating the Nim move back into a chop. It knows nothing about Hackenbush.

Forks: the colon principle

A fork, where two branches leave the same vertex, is less obvious. Take branches of lengths 2 and 3 meeting at the ground. Adding the lengths gives 5, but standing on the ground the two branches are just two stalks, a Nim position worth .

The same thing holds above the ground. This is the colon principle: if the branches meeting at a vertex reduce to stalks of lengths and , you can replace them with a single stalk of length hanging from without changing the winner of any game that contains them. Each branch may itself be a tree, reduced first by the same rule.

What "equivalent" means

We have been saying a stalk and a heap are the same game. Conway's definition makes that precise:

For impartial games there is a shortcut: exactly when is a second-player win. For a stalk and a heap of the same size, the second player wins by mirroring: whatever the opponent removes from one, remove the same amount from the other.

The stalk theorem and the colon principle are both equivalences in this sense, and that is what makes the reduction in Figure 4 legitimate: each step swaps a piece of the position for an equivalent piece, so the winner never changes. Every finite impartial game is equivalent to a single Nim heap. The size of that heap is the game's Grundy value, the subject of Part 3.
