Green Hackenbush

Part 2 of 15. Two games that look nothing alike, played side by side, produce the same moves in the same order. That is what it means for games to be equivalent, and it is how the rest of the series will work.

In the previous explainer we met Nim and Bouton's XOR rule. Nim looked simple because it is simple: heaps of objects, take from any one, last move wins. The theorem we proved was that a position is losing exactly when the XOR of the heap sizes is zero. We played the Marienbad start, , and watched that XOR drop to zero each time the computer moved.

Today's game looks nothing like Nim. It is played on a drawing of a plant. The board is a collection of green edges connecting nodes, with one or more edges rooted in the ground. On your turn you chop any edge; then gravity takes over, and every edge no longer connected to the ground through a chain of surviving edges falls off. The player who removes the last edge wins. That is Green Hackenbush. It was invented by John Conway, and its theory is the hinge of everything that comes later in this series.

Here is the claim we will verify by play. A single vertical chain of edges, rooted in the ground (a "stalk") is the same game as a Nim heap of size . Not "analogous." Not "related." The same game, in a sense we will make precise. And Green Hackenbush on any sum of stalks is Nim on the stalk lengths. The Marienbad position is three stalks of heights 3, 5, 7, and winning it is Bouton again.

A stalk is a heap

Here is a 5-edge stalk on the right and a 5-coin Nim heap on the left. Chop an edge and the stalk drops everything above. Click a coin on the left and the heap loses every coin at or above the one you clicked. The two boards stay synchronized: whatever you do on one side, the matching move happens on the other.

Figure 1. Stalk-to-Nim correspondence. Taking coins from the heap is the same move as chopping the stalk at height . Both boards record exactly the same number of objects after every move. This is not an analogy — it is an identification.

Taking one coin from the heap and chopping the top edge of the stalk are the same action written two ways. Taking three coins is the same action as chopping the edge three from the top; everything above it falls with it, and the heap loses those three coins too. The rules of Nim and the rules of Hackenbush on a stalk generate exactly the same list of legal positions, and the transitions between positions match edge for edge.

That is the first stalk theorem, already. We will write it more carefully in a moment, but for now: the moves you can make from a stalk of height are exactly the moves you can make from a Nim heap of size , and the resulting positions line up through the map "how many objects are left."

Stalk theorem. A Green Hackenbush stalk of length is the same game as a Nim heap of size . A chop at height (counting from the ground) leaves a stalk of length , just as taking coins from a Nim heap of size leaves a heap of size . The two games are interchangeable.

Marienbad, as a garden

The second figure is the playable centerpiece. The starting position is the Marienbad garden: three green stalks of heights 3, 5, 7, rooted side by side in a shared ground line. You play against the computer, who treats the board exactly as if it were the Nim position and runs Bouton's strategy. You chop; the computer chops; whichever side removes the last edge wins.

Figure 2. Green Hackenbush on the Marienbad garden 3-5-7 against an optimal opponent. The XOR readout below the board shows the Nim value of the current position after each move — it is the same number Bouton's rule would compute, because a sum of stalks is a sum of heaps. Your job is to drive it to zero and leave it there.

Play a few rounds. Two things are worth watching. First: the XOR readout is the same number for the Hackenbush garden as it would be for a Nim position, because, by the stalk theorem applied to each stalk in the garden, they are the same Nim position. Second: the computer's move is always "chop some edge"; but which edge it chops is determined entirely by reducing each stalk to its length, running Bouton's rule on the three lengths, and translating the resulting Nim move back to a chop. The computer does not know Hackenbush. It knows Nim and it knows how to translate.

If you lose against the Marienbad start, you lost to an equivalent copy of the Nim game from explainer 1. If you win, the computer moved into a zero-XOR position and could not recover. Either way, what you are playing is Nim. The stalks are cosplay.

On the XOR readout. The panel under the board shows each stalk's length as a short row of bits, then the column-wise XOR. A zero XOR means the position is losing for whoever just moved. Bouton told us in explainer 1 that this number is the Grundy value of a Nim sum, and the next section will show it is also the Grundy value of the corresponding Hackenbush garden.

Forks, and why they still reduce

Stalks are the easy case. What happens when a Hackenbush position has a fork, a point where two edges branch off from a single vertex above the ground? A reasonable guess is that a branch of two side-stalks of lengths 2 and 3 is somehow "worth" . A better guess, which turns out to be correct, is that it is worth . The theorem that makes this work is called the colon principle.

State it as follows. Pick a vertex somewhere above the ground. Everything above is some subgraph ; what hangs off can be a single stalk or it can be a fork. The colon principle says: the branch above can be replaced by a single stalk of length , where is the Nim sum (XOR) of the lengths of the branches meeting at , and the game is unchanged.

The 2-2 case is worth looking at carefully. The plant has 1 stem edge plus 2 + 2 = 4 edges of side-branches, for five edges total; a naive count says five-edge positions should be first-player wins. But , so the entire branch above the fulcrum replaces a zero-length stalk, and the whole plant collapses to the single stem edge. That one edge is a first-player win, chop it and all four edges above fall as a consequence of gravity. The colon principle correctly predicts: the 2-2 plant is equivalent to a 1-edge stalk, and the winning move for the first player is to chop the stem.

The colon principle generalizes to full trees, not just single Y-forks. Any branch hanging off a fulcrum vertex contributes its own Grundy value, and the fulcrum edge plus that branch is equivalent to a stalk whose length is the Grundy value of the branch. Apply the principle recursively from the leaves inward and any green Hackenbush tree reduces to a single stalk, and therefore to a single Nim heap.

What "equivalent" means

We've said "the stalk and the Nim heap are the same game" several times now, and it is time to define what that means, because the rest of the series leans on it. The definition is due to Conway, and it does for games what equivalence of fractions does for numbers.

DefinitionGame equivalence

Two impartial games and are equivalent, written , if for every impartial game , the game sums and have the same winner under optimal play.

For impartial games this turns out to be the same as saying , because the Grundy value of the disjoint sum is the XOR of the pieces' Grundy values, and a position is losing exactly when its Grundy value is zero. You will see that XOR rule again on every page of this series.

Figure 5. Game equivalence, stated as a definition. The colon principle, the stalk theorem, and every reduction we have performed in this explainer are instances of this single relation. An impartial game is identified by its Grundy value; two positions with the same Grundy value play identically in every context.

The stalk theorem is an equivalence in this sense. A stalk of length added to any other impartial game produces the same winner as a Nim heap of size added to . The colon principle is an equivalence in this sense too: replacing a subtree by a stalk of the right length does not change the winner of any game that happens to contain that subtree. This is why the reduction steps in figure 4 are legitimate, each step replaces a piece of the tree by an equivalent piece, without changing who wins.

For impartial games, equivalence classes are labeled by a single non-negative integer: the Grundy value. We've seen a handful already. A Nim heap of size has Grundy value . A stalk of length has Grundy value . A two-branch fork whose branches have lengths and has Grundy value . A disjoint sum of games has Grundy value equal to the XOR of its pieces' Grundy values, Sprague and Grundy's theorem, which we will prove in full in explainer 4. Today, in Hackenbush, we are seeing it in a second example.

Takeaways

Three claims from this explainer carry forward.

  • Stalk theorem. A single Hackenbush stalk of length is the same game as a Nim heap of size . This is the simplest non-trivial instance of game equivalence.
  • Colon principle. In Green Hackenbush, a branch rooted at a fulcrum vertex can be replaced by a stalk whose length is the XOR of the branches meeting at the fulcrum. Apply recursively to reduce any tree to a Nim heap.
  • Equivalence. Two games are equivalent when their sum has Grundy value zero. For impartial games, equivalence classes are labeled by a single non-negative integer, the Grundy value, and the Grundy value of a disjoint sum is the XOR of the pieces.

Equivalence is the whole trick. Once we know that two games with identical Grundy value are interchangeable, we can reduce any impartial game we understand to any other. The next explainer turns this into a general computational tool: the Grundy value of any impartial game is applied to the set of Grundy values of its options, and every theorem in the rest of act I will be a corollary of that one formula.
