Dots and Boxes

Part 10 of 10. Two players take turns drawing one edge between adjacent dots. Close the fourth side of a box and you claim it and move again. Most boxes wins. The endgame is decided by counting chains.

Most people have played this on the back of a menu, and few notice that it has a sharp endgame theory: the unclaimed boxes organize themselves into chains, a parity rule decides who will be forced to open them, and a sacrifice called the double-cross lets the right player keep that advantage to the end. The playground tactic of taking every box you are offered is often exactly wrong.

Play first. The default board is the classic 4×4 grid of dots, which makes 3×3 = 9 boxes. You move first.

Against the greedy computer, watch for the point where every remaining segment gives away a box. From then on the game is about who is forced to open the next region, and how big it is.

Chains and loops

In the typical position with no safe move left, every unclaimed box has exactly two sides drawn. Each box then connects through its two open sides to a neighbor or to the edge of the board, so the unclaimed boxes split into paths (chains) and cycles (loops). Berlekamp calls a chain of three or more boxes long.

Short chains (1 or 2 boxes)
The opener of a 2-chain can draw the middle edge. The two boxes then have to be taken separately, so the taker cannot hand them back as a pair.
Long chains (3 or more)
However it is opened, the taker can capture all but two boxes and give the last two back with one stroke.
Loop
A closed cycle. The taker can keep control by capturing all but four and giving back two pairs.

Figure 2. The pieces of a frozen endgame. Solid lines are drawn, dashed lines are still open. Every box has exactly two sides drawn.

The cutoff at three follows from what the opener can do. A player forced to open a 2-chain draws the edge between its two boxes, and the taker gets two single boxes with nothing to decline. A chain of three or more cannot be opened that safely: whichever edge goes first, the taker can stop two boxes short and hand those two back.

The double-cross

Figure 3. The last two chains of a game, lengths 5 and 7, both open at the board edge. Your opponent has opened the 5-chain. Taking all five leaves you to open the 7-chain. Taking three and then drawing the far end edge leaves two boxes your opponent takes with a single stroke, a double-crossed move, after which they must open the 7-chain.

On one chain in isolation, taking everything is better. The sacrifice pays because the player who declines the last two boxes keeps the move order: the opponent has to take the pair and then open the next chain. The player with this control can repeat it on every long chain, paying two boxes per chain (four per loop) and taking the whole of the last one. Control is usually worth far more than those payments, though not always, as the last position in Figure 4 shows.

The long chain rule

So the fight before the freeze is over who will have control, and a counting argument says how many long chains each player wants.

Every turn ends with a move that completes no box, except the final turn, which ends the game. Call a move that completes two boxes at once a double-cross, and let be the number of them in a game. With edges and boxes, the moves that complete something number , so the game lasts

turns. The last step is Euler's formula for the grid (, counting the outside as a face), so edges minus boxes is always dots minus one. On the 4×4-dot board that is 24 − 9 = 15. Whoever plays the last turn is the player with control, since they take the final chain. The first player plays the odd-numbered turns, so the first player wants dots plus double-crosses to be odd.

In a simple endgame with control, every long chain except the last produces one double-cross (the handed-back pair), and every loop produces two, which leaves the parity alone. Substituting gives the rule in the form players use:

Long chain rule. The first player wants the number of dots plus the number of long chains to be even. The second player wants it odd. Loops do not count.

On the 4×4-dot board (16 dots) the first player wants an even number of long chains. On 5×5 dots (25) the first player wants an odd number, and on 6×6 dots (36) even again. Figure 4 checks the rule against exact play.

Position D is the rule's limit. The rule tells you who can get control. It does not promise that control is worth what it costs, and in D, with a 4-loop and a 2-chain beside a single 3-chain, the exact search says it is not.

Why it needs a book

Nim reduces to one XOR (). Dots and Boxes resists that because it is a scoring game with a move-again rule, so positions do not add up as independent games the way Sprague-Grundy or surreal values require. The theory that exists combines the parity fight above with a separate calculus for valuing chains and loops in the endgame.

Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays (Berlekamp, Conway and Guy, 1982) has a chapter on the game. Elwyn Berlekamp (1940–2019) later gave it a book of its own, The Dots and Boxes Game: Sophisticated Child's Play (A K Peters, 2000). Exact solutions by computer remain small: Joseph Barker and Richard Korf ("Solving Dots-And-Boxes", AAAI 2012) showed that the 4×5-box board is a tie with perfect play, the largest board solved at the time.
