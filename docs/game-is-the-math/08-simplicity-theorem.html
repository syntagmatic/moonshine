The Simplicity Theorem

Part 8 of 15. We have surreal numbers. We have partizan games. We do not yet have a way to compute the value of an arbitrary short game without unfolding the whole surreal construction. Conway's simplicity theorem closes the gap in one paragraph.

Three explainers ago we played Blue-Red Hackenbush and watched fractions appear. Two explainers ago we tried to derive the value of from first principles and found ourselves comparing surreal sums of empty sets. The construction is beautiful but it is not a computation: every simplification needs a fresh derivation. We need a tool that takes a partizan position and prints out its number, fast.

The tool is the simplicity theorem, and it really is one paragraph. For a short game , look at every Left option's value and every Right option's value. The game equals the simplest number — the one with the earliest birthday — strictly between every Left value and every Right value. That is the whole rule. The rest of this explainer is making the rule do the work it claims to.

We do not need a brand-new theory of the surreal numbers to use it. The values we encounter in short games are all dyadic rationals — fractions whose denominator is a power of two. So are the only numbers we will ever need to write down. Birthdays come for free: the integer has birthday ; the dyadic with odd has birthday . (We will rederive both of these from the construction in a moment, then stop worrying about them.)

Stops: the numerical envelope of a partizan game

Before stating the theorem, a warm-up. A partizan game tree has two kinds of edges: the moves Left can make (blue) and the moves Right can make (red). At the leaves, the game is over and someone has either won or there is a number sitting there. The interior nodes are choices. If both players play optimally toward different goals — Left wants the leaf number to be as large as possible, Right wants it as small as possible — what numerical outcome do we end up at?

The catch is that who moves first matters. If Left moves first, Left picks the move that, played out under optimal alternation, ends up at the largest number Left can force. We call that final number the Left stop of the game. Similarly the Right stop is what Right can guarantee if Right moves first. The two stops bracket the game's "numerical envelope" — the range of outcomes that perfect play can reach.

The simplicity theorem will turn out to compute a value that, for cold games, sits between the two stops. For hot games (where moving is a strict gain), it will fail loudly and we will know we need a richer theory. That is the topic of explainer 10. For now we work in the regime where Conway's rule produces a plain number, and we make sure that number is right.

The theorem

Here is the statement. Treat it as a definition of when the right-hand side is a number — we will spend the rest of the page convincing ourselves it is the right definition.

Conway's simplicity theorem. Let be a short partizan game in which every option's value is already known to be a (dyadic) number. Set and .

If , then , the simplest number strictly greater than and strictly less than . By "simplest" we mean the dyadic rational with the earliest birthday; if more than one shares the earliest birthday, the canonical procedure below picks one and the choice does not matter for the value of the game.

If , the game is not equal to any number, and a different theory (temperatures, switches, infinitesimals) takes over.

Two pieces deserve to be unpacked. First, "simplest number" — what is the algorithm? Second, why is the answer this and not something else?

Finding the simplest number in an open interval

"Earliest birthday" turns out to have a clean recipe. The integers come first (they are born on day for ), then the half-integers, then quarter-integers, then eighths, and so on. Within a day, what is "first" is the rational closest to zero. So a sensible search is:

  1. If falls strictly between and , return 0. (Day 0 wins immediately.)
  2. Otherwise, if there is an integer strictly between and , return the one with the smallest absolute value. (Day for some .)
  3. Otherwise, the entire open interval lives inside for some integer . Walk down the binary tree between and by halving — try the midpoint , then quarter-points, then eighths — and return the first dyadic the search lands inside.

That last step is binary search through the dyadics, with the tree of midpoints exactly matching the order in which dyadic rationals are born. The first one that fits the interval is, by construction, the one with the earliest birthday.

Why the simplest, and not something else?

The honest reason takes a careful look at the surreal-game equality, but the moral reason is short: any simpler number would have to be either itself, itself, or outside the interval — and none of those are options. Any more complex number would not be wrong, exactly, but it would not be the value either; the simpler one already is the game.

The full argument goes: if is the simplest number with , then we can show and hold simultaneously, so . Each direction uses the inductive fact that whenever Right (resp. Left) moves to a number outside the interval, Left (resp. Right) has a response back into the simpler region, because being earliest-birthday means everyone "older" than is on the wrong side. Conway's On Numbers and Games writes the proof out in full; we will trust the conclusion and use the algorithm.

Two things to notice about the calculator. First, only the largest Left option and the smallest Right option matter for the final value. We say those are the dominant options; the others are dominated and can be deleted from the game without changing its value. (Whoever owns a dominated option would never play it under any optimal line — choosing a worse move never improves your outcome in a cold game.) Second, the procedure does not try to "simplify" the game tree first; it walks straight to a number on the dyadic line. The simplification happens after the fact, when we look back and notice which options were dominated.

A worked example: 3/4 from the leaves up

The simplicity theorem really earns its keep when a game's options are themselves games whose values still have to be computed. Then the theorem is applied recursively: simplify the smallest sub-positions first, replace each sub-position by its computed number, and continue up the tree until the whole game collapses to a single value.

The starting position is a partizan tree four levels deep; the leaves are integers, the interior nodes are blue or red moves, and the goal is the value at the root. Each sub-tree is a short game whose options are already numbers (after we have processed everything beneath it), so the simplicity theorem applies directly.

Connection back to Blue-Red Hackenbush

Three explainers ago we played Blue-Red Hackenbush and started seeing values like , , and attached to small stalks. The book's "binary fraction rule" let us read those values directly off the stalk colors: count consecutive same-color edges from the ground up to set the integer part with its sign, then each edge of the opposite color halves the increment and flips its sign. It was a closed-form trick we accepted on faith.

The simplicity theorem proves that trick. Take any Blue-Red stalk, write down its game tree by considering Left's moves (chop a blue) and Right's moves (chop a red), apply the simplicity theorem at each level, and the value that pops out at the root will exactly match the binary-fraction reading.

This is the round trip we wanted. Blue-Red Hackenbush motivated the question of partizan game values; the surreal numbers gave us a language for them; the simplicity theorem turned the language into a calculation. From now on, any short game whose options reduce to numbers can be valued in time linear in the size of the tree, with arithmetic no harder than rational comparison.

Caveats and what comes next

The theorem is silent on hot games. Whenever , the open interval is empty and the simplicity theorem returns nothing. Such games are real combinatorial positions — they include every interesting partizan game where moving is a strict gain — but their values are not single numbers. They have a "mean" plus a "temperature," and the analysis is the subject of explainer 10. The simplicity theorem is the cold-game tool; the temperature theory is the hot-game tool, and together they cover the short partizan world.

"Short" matters. The simplicity theorem as stated covers short games — games with finitely many positions reachable in finitely many moves. Loopy games (positions that can recur forever) and infinite games (positions with countably many options) extend the theory in directions we will sketch in explainer 15.

What we have now is enough to handle every short cold partizan game we can write down. We can:

  • Compute the value of any short partizan game whose options are dyadic numbers, in linear time in the tree's size.
  • Recognize when a game is not a number (the simplicity theorem refuses; the game is hot).
  • Verify previously asserted Hackenbush values by computing them from scratch via the recursive game tree.
  • Compare two games by computing both values and comparing dyadics — the partial order on games becomes the total order on numbers, for cold games.

What comes next is Domineering, in explainer 9. Domineering is a partizan game played by placing dominoes on a grid — Left places horizontally, Right places vertically — and almost every Domineering position is a sum of small partizan games whose values we now know how to compute. The simplicity theorem becomes the daily tool; we use it without ceremony, pin the answer to each subgame, and add the answers up. By the end of explainer 9, the reader will be able to sit down with a half-finished Domineering board and compute, in their head, who is winning and by how much.
