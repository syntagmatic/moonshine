Blue-Red Hackenbush

Part 5 of 10. The first partizan game: Left chops blue, Right chops red, and a position can be worth half an edge.

Every game so far was impartial: both players had the same moves. Now edges come in two colors. Left may chop only blue edges, Right only red ones. Gravity still applies (whatever loses its path to the ground falls), and the player with no legal move still loses.

Once the moves differ, a Grundy number can no longer describe a position. Instead each position gets a signed game value. Positive means Left wins whoever moves first, negative means Right does, and zero means the second player wins. The first value that is not an integer is , and it really is half an edge: two copies of that position add up to one blue edge.

Mixing colors breaks this simple count, because one chop can delete edges of the other color: a blue edge under a red one is holding up a move that belongs to Right. Fractions come from that interaction.

Sums of stalks

The number after the equals sign comes from searching the whole two-board game tree, not from adding, and it always matches the sum of the two stalk values. In BR + BR (value 1) you win however the computer plays. In BBR + RRB (value 0) the first player loses, and here that is you.

BR + BR is worth 1, the same as one blue edge: in any larger sum you could swap the pair for a single blue edge and the winner would not change. Build BR on one side and RB on the other and the value drops to 0. RB is BR with its colors swapped, worth , and the two cancel.

The computer in Figures 1 and 4 plays exactly. It evaluates every position by recursion over the full game tree (with memoization), then moves to the option of least value; the hint does the same for Left with the greatest value. When values are numbers, moving to your best option wins whenever any move wins. This was checked against a plain win/loss search on every stalk and every sum of two stalks with up to four edges each.

Where the values live

Every value so far is a dyadic rational, a fraction whose denominator is a power of 2. For stalks, Berlekamp's rule reads the value straight off the colors: each edge in the bottom run of one color counts as a whole edge, and every edge above that run counts half as much as the edge below it, plus for blue and minus for red.
