Sums of Games

Part 11 of 15. The central operation of combinatorial game theory. Two boards on the table; the player to move picks one and plays in it. Everything we have built — Grundy values, surreal arithmetic, temperature — exists to compute one number for that combined board.

For ten explainers we have been working on single games, one board at a time. The whole point of combinatorial game theory is that that is not where games live. They live in piles. Two heaps of Nim, three Hackenbush stalks, a Domineering position next to a Go endgame on the same table — each turn, the player to move picks one component and plays a legal move in it; the others sit untouched. Whoever makes the last legal move (across all components) wins. That compound is the sum of the components, written , and it is the pattern almost every real combinatorial game eventually decomposes into.

This explainer collects what we already know about sums and consolidates it into one operator with one strategy rule. The work splits down a clean line. Impartial sums, where both players have the same moves in every component, are answered by the Sprague–Grundy theorem from Explainer 4: take the Grundy value of each component and XOR them. Partizan sums, where Left and Right may have different moves, split further into two regimes. Cold partizan games — the ones with surreal-number values — sum the way ordinary numbers do: just add them. Hot partizan games refuse to behave that way, and the strategy for summing them needs the temperature theory from Explainer 10. One operator, three behaviours, and one rule that ties them together.

We will work through all three regimes, then play the unified version: a sum of four components drawn from impartial Nim, cold Blue–Red Hackenbush, and hot Domineering, with a single optimal-play computer that knows about all of them. The centerpiece is the multi-game challenge in Figure 4 — that is where the three regimes have to coexist on one screen and the operator has to actually do its job.

The sum operator, on one screen

A formal definition first, so the figures have something to point at. Given games , the disjunctive sum is the game whose options are: every position you could reach by making a legal move in (leaving alone), together with every position you could reach by making a legal move in (leaving alone). The terminal position is reached when no legal move exists in any component; the player to move from there has lost. Sums are commutative and associative — the order in which you list the components does not matter, and you can always group them however you want — and the empty game (no components, no moves) is the additive identity .

That is the entire definition. There is no special "interaction" between components, no rule that crosses the boundary, no shared resources. The components are oblivious to each other; the only thing they share is the turn order. And yet — this is the surprise that all of CGT is built on — the strategic content of the sum is rarely the union of the strategic contents of the parts. The whole becomes more than the parts because the player to move gets to choose which part to play in. That choice is where the theory lives.

Impartial sums: XOR of Grundy values (recap)

Impartial games are the easy case. By the Sprague–Grundy theorem from Explainer 4, every impartial position is equivalent to a Nim heap whose size is its Grundy value . A sum of Nim heaps is itself a Nim position, and Bouton's theorem (Explainer 1) says the Nim value of a position is the bitwise XOR of the heap sizes. Chain the two together and the sum-of-impartial-games rule pops out: the Grundy value of a sum is the XOR of the component Grundy values.

That is the entire impartial story.

Three properties of the impartial sum that are about to break, partially, when we move to partizan games:

  • One number per component. The Grundy value summarises everything strategic about a component. Two positions with the same Grundy value are interchangeable in any sum.
  • The total is a fixed function of the parts. No matter which components you mix, the rule is XOR. Same operator every time.
  • Strategy is local arithmetic. To win, find any single-component move that brings the total XOR to zero. The choice of which component is unconstrained — you can usually pick the one whose moves you like best.

The last point is the one that fails hardest in the partizan world. For partizan hot games, you do not get to pick freely — you have to play in the hottest component, and the temperature theory from Explainer 10 is exactly the apparatus for deciding which one that is. We will get there. First the easy case.

Partizan cold sums: ordinary addition

For partizan games — Left and Right have different moves — there is no Grundy-value reduction. The strategic content of a position is no longer captured by a single non-negative integer; instead it is captured by a number on the surreal number line (Explainers 7–8) or, when the game refuses to be a number, by something more elaborate. We will not reconstruct the surreal number theory here. We need only one fact from it.

Cold-game addition

If and are numbers — equivalently, neither game has a move that strictly improves things for the player making it — then the value of the sum is the ordinary sum of the values: . Strategy: when it is your turn, play in any component that still has a move on your side; when the running value forces a loss, you have already lost.

"Number" here is the technical word; the informal word is cold. A cold game has the property that no move helps the player who makes it, in the sense that Left's options are all less-than-or-equal to the value, and Right's options are all greater-than-or-equal to it. There is no urgency: nobody benefits from moving first. That is the entire cold-game story. Sum is plus. Strategy is "if the value is in your favour, do not blunder it away; if it is against you, you have lost from the start." There is no temperature, no priority, no uncertainty about which component to play in. Play in any of them as long as you do not blunder away the value. This is what Conway means when he says the surreal numbers are the cold-game values — they live as game positions first and as numbers second, but once you have the number, the arithmetic is the same arithmetic you have known since grade school.

Two cold games, one rule. Sums of cold games are dull. The interesting case is what happens when the games are not cold.

Partizan hot sums: temperature decides

Some partizan games are not numbers. The simplest example is the Domineering position 2×2 — a two-by-two square of empty cells, where Left places vertical dominoes (covering two cells in the same column) and Right places horizontal dominoes (covering two cells in the same row). From the empty 2×2, Left has a single move (a vertical domino in either column, both equivalent by symmetry; the result is two empty 1×1 cells, in which neither player can move). Right has a single move with the same kind of result. Both options lead to the zero position. The value is , written as a Conway form — Left's options are and Right's options are . This is not a number on the surreal line. It is its own thing — the simplest infinitesimal game — and it confuses any attempt to use ordinary addition.

The clearest examples of hot games come from Domineering boards where Left and Right disagree about how good the position is. A 2×3 strip has different best moves for the two sides: Left's best leaves a position favouring Left, and Right's best leaves a position favouring Right. The Conway form looks like for some : Left thinks the position is worth a positive amount, Right thinks it is worth a negative amount, and the value sits between them with a temperature measuring how much each side wants to move first. Temperature is the central concept of Explainer 10. Here we will not reconstruct the theory; we will use one fact from it.

Hot sums: play in the hottest component

If a sum contains hot components, the optimal move is in the component with the highest temperature. Cold components and number-valued components contribute their value to the running total but should not be played in until all the hot components are gone. The intuition: every hot move shifts the running total by an amount equal to (roughly) the temperature, and you would rather grab a big shift now than leave it for the opponent.

What you were watching when you played the position above is temperature theory in action. Each component has a Conway form like , and the temperature measures how big the gap is between Left's and Right's preferred values. Hot moves narrow the gap by playing a move worth roughly one temperature unit; once all the hot moves are taken, the position cools and the running total settles. The reason "play in the hottest component" is the right rule is that any other rule lets the opponent grab the temperature you left lying around. Explainer 10 builds the thermograph machinery that makes the strategy precise; the figure is the operational version.

The two regimes are not separate operators. They are the same operator applied to games at different temperatures. Cold games have temperature zero (no urgency); hot games have positive temperature (someone wants to move first); at very low temperatures the hot rule degenerates to the cold rule because there is nothing to choose between components. The unified rule is "play in the component with the highest temperature, breaking ties by going for the largest immediate value swing." For impartial games the corresponding "temperature" is the indicator of nonzero Grundy value, and "play in the highest-temperature component" specialises to "play any zeroing move."

One strategic note. Because the cold components contribute integer values, you can read off whether the cold side is in your favour just by adding them up. If the cold total favours you, you can afford to lose a Hackenbush move late; if it favours the opponent, you need the hot Domineering and the impartial Nim to compensate. The unifying picture

What we have just played is the operator . The same operator. There is one disjunctive sum across CGT, and everything in the theory — Grundy values, surreal numbers, simplicity, temperature — exists to compute strategic invariants of that operator on a particular regime of games. The figure below is the picture.

impartial — Grundy values, XOR cold partizan — surreal numbers, + hot partizan — temperature, hottest first

Figure 5. The unifying picture. The disjunctive sum is the central operator of CGT. Three pieces of theoretical machinery — Grundy values for impartial games, surreal numbers for cold partizan games, temperature theory for hot partizan games — are the three different evaluators that turn a component into a strategic invariant. Once you have the invariants, the sum reduces to a single arithmetic in each regime: XOR for Grundy values, ordinary addition for surreal numbers, and "play in the hottest component" for temperature. The whole of CGT is what is needed to make those evaluators precise.

One operator, three evaluators, three arithmetics. That is the spine of the subject. From here, every later development in CGT is either an extension of the evaluator (atomic weights for nimber-laden hot games, Norton multiplication for repeated sums of the same component, the loopy values from Explainer 15) or a sharper analysis of the same arithmetic on a particular family of games (Go endgames, Domineering on larger boards, Dots-and-Boxes long-chain theory). The operator does not change.

Takeaways

Three claims that carry forward into Act III.

  • The disjunctive sum is the operator. Combinatorial games combine by sum, not by composition or interaction. The components do not see each other; only the turn order is shared. This is the formal sense in which a real game is "many small games at once."
  • Three regimes, three evaluators. Impartial sums use Grundy values and XOR. Cold partizan sums use surreal numbers and ordinary addition. Hot partizan sums use temperature and the rule "play in the hottest component." All three are special cases of a single optimal-play principle: among the moves that improve your position the most, pick one.
  • Local optimisation, global verdict. In every regime, the optimal play is decided by examining each component on its own and combining the verdicts. Even the hot rule is local — "what is the temperature of this component?" is a question about that component alone. The unifying picture is that local invariants and a sum are enough to determine the global winner.
