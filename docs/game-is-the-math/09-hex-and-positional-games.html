Hex and Positional Games

Part 9 of 10. Two players paint cells on a rhombus of hexagons, and whoever links their two sides wins. The two central theorems say a winner always exists and that the first player has a winning strategy, without saying what it is.

The rules: a rhombus of hexagonal cells, two players, Blue and Red. On each turn the player to move paints one empty cell their color. Blue wins with a connected chain of blue cells from the top side to the bottom side; Red wins with a chain from the left side to the right. There are no captures and no passes. Nothing here decomposes into a sum of games, yet two theorems describe it: Hex cannot end in a draw, and the first player can force a win. Play first; you are Blue and move first.

In vs.-computer mode Red is a one-move-lookahead heuristic, not a solver: for each empty cell it measures the shortest remaining border-to-border path for both sides (own stones free, empty cells cost 1) and picks the cell that most shortens its own path and lengthens yours, with a small pull toward the center. It does not know about bridges, so it can be beaten.

Good players build bridges: two stones diagonally apart with two empty cells between them. If the opponent takes one of the empty cells, you take the other, so the two stones are as good as connected. Play a few games and notice two things. Every game ends with a winner. And the first player seems to have the easier time.

Hex cannot draw

Tic-tac-toe between competent players is a draw, and chess and Go have draws built into their rules. In Hex, any full board has exactly one winner. Try to build one where nobody wins.

No-draw theorem. If every cell of a Hex board is Blue or Red, exactly one player has a winning chain.

The cleanest proof is David Gale's (1979). Surround the board with four extra strips: Blue strips along the top and bottom, Red strips along the left and right. Now look at the edges of the hexagon tiling that separate a Blue cell from a Red one. Every corner of the tiling has three hexagons meeting at it, so where a boundary edge ends, exactly one other boundary edge continues it: of the three cells at the corner, two share a color and one differs. The boundary edges therefore form paths with no branches. Start at a corner of the rhombus, where a Blue strip meets a Red strip, and follow the path. It cannot stop in the interior or revisit an edge, so it leaves the board at another corner. All along it, connected Blue cells sit on one side and connected Red cells on the other. Wherever it exits, one of those two sides has touched both of its own strips: either the Blue cells join top to bottom or the Red cells join left to right. At most one player can win because a Blue top-to-bottom chain and a Red left-to-right chain would have to cross, and on a hexagonal tiling two chains cannot cross without sharing a cell.

The argument leans on three cells meeting at every corner. On a square grid with four-way adjacency, four cells meet at a corner and the path can branch; exhaustive search finds 118 of the 512 fillings of a 3-by-3 square board have no winner. Gale also showed that the Hex theorem is equivalent to the Brouwer fixed-point theorem: either one can be derived from the other.

The first player wins

Strategy-stealing theorem (Nash). On an Hex board, the first player has a winning strategy.

Suppose instead the second player has a winning strategy . The first player makes any opening move , then pretends is not there and plays as if they were the second player. If ever says to play on the cell already holding , they play some other empty cell and treat that as the new spare stone.

An extra stone of your own color never hurts you in Hex: it can only add connections. So whenever would win on the imagined board, the first player wins on the real one. Now both players have a winning strategy, which is impossible. The assumption was false, so the second player has no winning strategy, and since the game cannot draw, the first player has one.

The proof names no move. Its opening is arbitrary only inside a proof by contradiction, and that does not mean every opening wins. Solving small boards exhaustively (a few seconds for 4-by-4) shows that on 4-by-4 only the four cells of the short diagonal win for the first player; the other twelve openings lose. Computers have gone further: all 8-by-8 openings were solved in 2009, and all 9-by-9 openings by Jakub Pawlewicz and Ryan Hayward in 2013, along with the center opening on 10-by-10. The 11-by-11 and larger boards played by humans remain unsolved.

Because the first player wins, serious Hex uses the swap rule: the first player places a stone, and the second player may either reply or take that stone as their own. A strong opening gets swapped and a weak one gets punished, so the first player looks for an opening close to the line between winning and losing.

Positional games

A positional game has a finite board, players who alternately claim unclaimed points, and a family of winning sets; the first player to own a whole winning set wins. Hex is one, with chains as winning sets. Tic-tac-toe is another, with the eight lines.

The stealing argument works in every positional game, because an extra claimed point never hurts. What it proves in general is weaker: the first player does not lose. It becomes "the first player wins" only when draws are impossible. Tic-tac-toe can draw, and it is a draw with best play. The game of Y, a triangular relative of Hex, cannot draw, so the first player wins there too. Havannah can end in a draw, so strategy stealing only shows the first player does not lose. In Go and other capture games an extra stone can hurt, and the argument does not apply at all.

The Hales-Jewett theorem (1963) gives another case. It implies that for any , the game of -in-a-row on a -dimensional cube with cells per side cannot end in a draw once is large enough, since every two-coloring of the full cube contains a one-color line. Strategy stealing then gives the first player a win, with no hint of how to find it.

Hex was invented twice. Piet Hein introduced it in the Danish newspaper Politiken in December 1942 under the name Polygon. John Nash, a graduate student at Princeton, found it independently around 1948, and the strategy-stealing proof is his. Parker Brothers sold the game as Hex from 1952, and Martin Gardner's Scientific American column of July 1957 brought it to a wide audience.
