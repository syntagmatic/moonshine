Misère Play

Part 4 of 10. Keep the moves, flip the win condition: whoever makes the last move loses. Nim survives with one endgame correction. Almost everything else breaks.

Every game so far has used the normal-play convention: whoever makes the last legal move wins. The Sprague-Grundy theorem rests on it, since a position with no moves is a loss for the player to move, and that base case is what makes mex and XOR work.

In misère play the player who makes the last move loses. The moves are unchanged; you are now trying to force your opponent to make the final one. For most games this destroys the algebra: Grundy values stop predicting outcomes and sums stop decomposing. Nim is the exception, and it is where we start.

The divergence comes late. While two or more heaps hold at least two objects the computers make identical moves, and they can only disagree once exactly one heap does.

In practice: play ordinary XOR-zero Nim while two or more heaps are big. When your move would leave a single big heap, shrink it to 0 or 1 so that an odd number of 1s remains. The proof follows the same line. A fat position with XOR zero has at least two big heaps, because a lone big heap cannot cancel against 1s, so every move from it leaves a fat position with nonzero XOR. From a fat position with nonzero XOR, the XOR-zero move works when two or more heaps are big; when only one is, shrinking it to leave an odd number of 1s works.

Where misère stops being clean

Nim needs one endgame patch. Most impartial games cannot be handled as normal play plus a correction.

The trouble is sums. Under normal play a position is summarized by its Grundy value, and the value of a sum is the XOR of the parts. Under misère play no single number can do that job, and Nim already shows why: (1, 1) and (2, 2) both have Grundy value 0, yet under misère (1, 1) is a win for the player to move and (2, 2) is a loss. Two positions with the same Grundy value can have different misère outcomes, so the Grundy value cannot decide who wins, and Sprague-Grundy has no misère analogue. The same failure carries over to Green Hackenbush, since two stalks are just two Nim heaps.

For most games the known approach is to work one rule set at a time. Thane Plambeck's misère quotients (introduced in 2005) group positions of a given game that behave identically in every misère sum; some games have small finite quotients and many do not. The classical treatment is the chapter of Winning Ways titled "Survival in the Lost World."
