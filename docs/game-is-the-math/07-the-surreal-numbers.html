The Surreal Numbers

Part 7 of 15. In the last explainer the value of a Blue-Red Hackenbush stalk turned into a fraction. The fraction was a hint. Conway noticed that the construction we used to build it generates a number system: the integers, the dyadic rationals, all the reals, and eventually all the ordinals (every number we have ever needed and a great many more) out of nothing but Left and Right play. They are called the surreals, and they are the most surprising numbers in mathematics.

By the end of Part 6 we had values for short Blue-Red Hackenbush positions. A single blue edge to the ground was worth ; a single red edge was worth ; a blue stacked on a red was worth . The values were doing what we wanted, adding correctly across components, telling us who would win, and they had crept beyond the integers. Half of an edge. Half of a move. The arithmetic worked, but where did the fractions come from?

Conway's answer is to take the construction seriously. We built a value out of two sets of options, one for Left and one for Right. The blue-on-red position had value : the brace says the position is something Left can move to from and Right can move to from, and "the simplest thing that fits" is . If we let go of the requirement that the things in the braces are "values of games" and let them just be other already-constructed objects, we get a recursive number system that needs nothing to start with, not the integers, not the rationals, not even the empty set as we usually use it. Just two boxes labeled Left and Right and a rule for what they make.

The numbers do not fill the line densely on any finite day, they fill it in stages of finer and finer dyadic resolution. After infinitely many days they have done it.

Why this matters for games

We have built a number system out of two empty boxes and a recursive rule. The next question is what this has to do with the games we have been playing.

The answer is one sentence: the value of a partizan combinatorial game is a surreal number whenever the game's options are themselves numbers. This is the simplicity theorem, and it is what Part 8 unfolds. Concretely, every Blue-Red Hackenbush position has a value that is a dyadic rational (every one) and the value is computed by writing out the bracket of Left options and Right options recursively until you bottom out at integers, then applying the simplicity rule from below. The chain "blue, red, red" you saw in Part 6 had value ; we can now derive that from first principles. (We will not derive it now. The derivation is long enough that it is the spine of Part 8.)

Coming up in Part 8. The simplicity theorem turns the bracket-then-name procedure into a real algorithm, with a formal definition of "simplest" and a proof that the algorithm terminates and gives the right answer. We use it to compute values of every short Blue-Red Hackenbush position and all the small partizan games, including a few games whose values are surreals you have not yet met (3/4, 5/8, the entire dyadic line), and a handful of games whose values are not numbers at all, only games. Those exceptional cases (the ones that are games but not numbers) will turn out to drive the second half of Act II.

For now it is enough to know that we have constructed the number line out of two empty sets, that the construction includes every real number eventually, and that combinatorial games are how it gets there.

Conway, the construction, and a novella

The surreals are John Conway's. The construction was published as a chapter of On Numbers and Games (Academic Press, 1976) and given a public-friendly treatment in Donald Knuth's mathematical novella Surreal Numbers (Addison–Wesley, 1974). We owe Conway both the construction and the name. The story is unusually well documented because Knuth wrote the novella before the book came out, and the priority of the ideas is Conway's, but the public exposure of "surreal numbers" is Knuth's coinage.

Conway's path to the surreals ran through Go. He was at Cambridge in the late 1960s, watching Go endgames with the British amateur Go community, and noticed that the additive structure of Go endgame positions, disconnected regions whose strategic values added like numbers, was demanding a number system that had not yet been invented. The integers and rationals were not enough. The reals were too much. Some of the values he was seeing behaved like fractions whose denominators were powers of 2, and a few were honest infinitesimals that no real number could match.

The construction was already finished in his head when Knuth visited Cambridge in 1972. Knuth was instantly captivated, and over a week in a hotel in Norway in 1973 he wrote the novella Surreal Numbers, which presents Conway's construction as a story of two students stranded on an island who reconstruct the surreals from a stone tablet bearing only the rule for and . Knuth invented the name surreal; Conway accepted it, having previously called them just numbers. The Academic Press book followed in 1976 and the term entered the mathematical lexicon for good.

On Numbers and Games is the canonical reference and remains in print (now from A K Peters / CRC). It is the book to read if you want every detail of the construction, including the transfinite case (day and beyond) and the bridge to game theory that we are partway through. The first three chapters cover the number construction; the rest of the book builds the partizan game theory of Winning Ways on top of it.

Takeaways

We have constructed the surreal numbers up to birthday 2 and seen how they extend, in principle, to every real number and beyond. The construction needs nothing as input, no integers, no rationals, no order. It returns a totally ordered field that contains the reals as a subfield and has room for infinitesimals and infinities besides.

Three things to carry forward:

  • Every number is a bracket. A surreal is determined by two sets of earlier surreals, satisfying Left < Right. The bracket form is the canonical representation; the dyadic name is a convenience. When we compute with games in Part 8, the bracket form is the form we will be working in.
  • Simplicity names the answer. When the bracket is legal, the value is the simplest (= earliest born) number strictly between max(Left) and min(Right). This rule is how Conway's construction connects to the integers and rationals we already had: the surrogate name is forced by the dominance of birthday over arithmetic.
  • Numbers are a special case of games. The arithmetic operations on surreals (comparison, addition, negation) are the disjunctive sum operations on partizan games specialized to brackets that obey Left < Right. Surreals are the games whose options are numbers. Other games (the ones that are games but not numbers) sit just outside the surreal hierarchy and form the rest of the partizan theory.
