Domineering

Part 9 of 15. A grid, two players, two kinds of domino. Left lays vertical, Right lays horizontal, last to place wins. Numbers are not enough to describe what is going on.

So far in this series our partizan games have all settled into orderly arithmetic. A Blue-Red Hackenbush stalk had a value, that value was a number, and the simplicity theorem from the previous chapter told us how to read it off. Sums worked the way they read on the page. The whole partizan world looked, mathematically, like a slightly weirder version of arithmetic.

Domineering breaks that.

The rules are about as simple as a grid game gets. There is a rectangular board of empty cells. Two players alternate. Left places a vertical 2×1 domino — that is, Left covers two empty cells stacked in the same column. Right places a horizontal 1×2 domino — two empty cells side by side in the same row. Once a cell is covered it is gone. When it is your turn and you cannot place your kind of domino anywhere on the board, you lose. (Normal play, exactly as in Nim.)

Read those two move-set lines again. Left and Right have different geometries. Left needs columns; Right needs rows. The very same square of empty cells looks different to the two players. That asymmetry is what is going to make the values stop being plain numbers.

If you played a few games you probably noticed that on the 4×4 you can sometimes win and sometimes the computer takes over the board completely. The losing games tend to look like this: you place your first vertical domino, then the computer places horizontal dominoes that cleanly partition what is left into rectangles where you have nothing to do, and you run out of legal moves while the computer still has plenty. The winning games look like the mirror image: you keep the geometry tilted in your favor, and at some point the computer is the one with no legal move.

The game is partizan. Left and Right move differently, so the question "is this position winning?" is not just "is the player to move winning?" — there are four sub-questions, one for each (player to move, who is asked). It turns out the cleanest way to answer all four at once is to give the position a game value — the same kind of object we built up in the simplicity-theorem explainer. We will see what those values look like for Domineering, and we will see why "value" cannot always be a single number.

The simplest positions are still numbers

The easiest Domineering positions to analyze are the rectangular ones where one of the two players has nothing to do at all.

Take a single column of empty cells: a board that is . Left can place a vertical domino if there are at least two cells in the column, and each such move shortens the column by 2. Right cannot move at all — Right needs two cells in a row, and there is only one cell in each row of a single-column board. So this game is "Left to play, repeatedly, until Left runs out." Strip values are easy because each strip is a pure one-sided game — there is only ever one player available to move. As soon as the board has both rows and columns wide enough for both players to fit a domino, both players can move and the value stops being one-sided. That is when "value" stops being a number and starts being something more interesting.

The 2×2 board: a position both players want

Look at an empty 2×2 board. Left has two legal moves — a vertical at column 0 and a vertical at column 1. Either move covers an entire column, leaving the other column as a single 2×1 strip with value . Right has two legal moves — a horizontal at row 0 and a horizontal at row 1. This game form is not a number. The simplicity theorem says we look for the simplest number strictly between the Left and Right options, where strictly means we need the Left option and the Right option . But the Left option here is greater than the Right option, not less. There is no number between them in the right direction. The whole simplicity recipe does not apply.

The position has a name: it is the switch . It is the canonical example of what combinatorial game theorists call a hot game. Both players are eager to move into it: Left wants to move because moving turns the position into +1 (a "bonus move" Left can later cash), and Right wants to move because moving turns the position into −1 (the same bonus, the other way). Whoever does not move first surrenders the bonus to whoever does. In a pure number game neither player gains by moving — moving just shrinks the game. In a hot game moving is profit, and the players race for it.

The game is the smallest hot game in Domineering. Bigger boards have hotter and more elaborate game forms — the standard 8×8 Domineering board, computed by Berlekamp and others in the 1990s, is famously complex and has been a benchmark for combinatorial game theory software ever since. But the 2×2 already shows the essential phenomenon: partizan games can have positions whose value is neither a number nor a sum of numbers, but a switch where both sides want to play first.

The hot/cold dichotomy. A game form is cold (a number, by the simplicity theorem) when , and hot (a switch, not a number) when . The boundary case is itself an integer. In a cold position neither player wants to move; in a hot position both do. Domineering is the simplest combinatorial game where hot positions arise routinely from natural play.

Sums of pieces

Mid-game Domineering boards almost always split into independent pieces. After a few moves, the empty cells form several connected regions, and within each region neither player's domino can cross to the next region — they are fully independent games glued together by addition.

For pure-number components, addition is ordinary arithmetic: +3 and −2 sum to +1, and both players know which way the position leans. For hot components, you cannot just "add" a switch to a number and get a number out — the sum stays hot. The interplay of cold and hot pieces is one of the central problems of combinatorial game theory, and it is the subject of the next chapter.

Two things to notice in this figure. First, the cold pieces add normally: the +2 column and the −2 row cancel exactly, contributing 0 to the total. Second, the hot piece does not add normally — it sits on top of the zero contributed by the cold pieces and gives the total a value of , the same switch we identified above. The total game's Left stop is +1 and Right stop is −1, even though everything else on the board has settled.

This is the picture you should keep when you read the next chapter: Domineering positions are sums of components, each component has its own value, the cold components reduce to numbers, and the hot components do not. The hot components have a notion of "how badly both players want to move" — the temperature — and there is a beautiful theory of how to play sums of hot games optimally by always moving in the hottest component first. That theory was developed by Conway, Berlekamp, and Hanner under the name thermography. We will build it next.

Why Domineering keeps mathematicians up at night

Hex (in the next act) is famous for being unsolvable in the sense that nobody can write down an explicit winning strategy. Domineering is famous in a different way: the values of small Domineering positions are computable, but they are wildly irregular. Berlekamp's group computed the value of empty Domineering boards for and the values are a complicated mix of switches, infinitesimals (the surreal-number relatives from earlier in the series), and ordinary numbers. The value of an empty 8×8 board is so intricate that its computation was a small landmark in computer-aided proof in the 1990s. Even today, empty Domineering boards for are unsolved.

So Domineering sits in a productive valley between the two failure modes of game analysis. It is too rich to have a closed-form theory like Nim, but small enough that a computer can do the tree search, and structured enough that a human can do the algebra by hand on small boards. That is exactly the kind of game where combinatorial game theory shines: rich enough to need the theory, structured enough that the theory works.

A historical note. Domineering was invented by Göran Andersson around 1973, and the name (and the analysis) was popularized by Conway in On Numbers and Games. It was later marketed under various names — Crosscram, Stop-Gate, "the dominoes-on-a-grid game" — and it has been studied for over fifty years without anyone finding a complete theory of optimal play on arbitrary boards. The theoretical breakthrough that did come, in the 1980s and 1990s, was thermography itself: a way to understand sums of hot games like Domineering positions without computing the entire game tree. That is what we will build next.

What we take with us

Domineering is the first game in this series whose values escape pure arithmetic.

  • Game forms can be hot. When the Left options of a position exceed the Right options (as numbers), the simplicity theorem does not apply, and the position is a switch — a game where both players race to move first.
  • Hot games are sums-friendly. A hot component can sit inside a sum with cold components. The cold parts behave like ordinary arithmetic; the hot parts contribute a "temperature" that does not collapse into a number. The total still has a meaningful value, and there are strategies for playing such sums well.
  • Small boards are tractable, big boards are not. A 4×4 Domineering board can be solved by exhaustive game-tree search; a 9×9 board is currently beyond reach. The exact spot where intractability sets in moves outward each decade as hardware and algorithms improve, but the underlying combinatorics keeps growing faster.
  • Hot positions motivate temperature. The fact that has Left stop +1 and Right stop −1 — and the fact that we want to write that as a single number telling us how hot the position is — is exactly the motivation for the next explainer.

The next chapter introduces temperature theory: a way of attaching a single non-negative number to any hot game form telling us how badly both players want to move there, plus the cooling operator that gradually freezes hot games into cold numbers we can finally read off. With temperature in hand, Domineering positions stop being a list of switches and start being a list of numbers-plus-temperatures — and sums of them become tractable in the same way Nim heaps were tractable in Act I.
