Hex and Positional Games

Part 12 of 15. Two players paint cells on a rhombus of hexagons. Whoever connects their two opposite sides wins. Hex is the game where the central theorems do not tell you what to do, they tell you that something exists, and refuse to say what.

Hex is the simplest game we will play in this series and the one whose theorems are the strangest. The rules: a rhombus of hexagonal cells, two players, Blue and Red. On each turn the player to move claims one empty cell by painting it their colour. Blue wins if a connected chain of blue cells stretches from the top side of the rhombus to the bottom side. Red wins if a connected chain of red cells stretches from the left side to the right side. There are no captures. There are no passes. Cells stay where they are put.

That is the entire ruleset. There is no hidden machinery, no Grundy value lurking in the binary expansion of a heap size, no decomposition into a sum of games. Hex is irreducible. And yet two of the most famous theorems in combinatorial game theory live here: Hex can never end in a draw, and the player who moves first always wins. The first is proved by a topology argument that needs no calculation. The second is proved by an argument so slippery it deserves its own warning label, it tells us a winning strategy exists without showing us a single move of it.

Play five or six full games. Try the small boards first, a 5-by-5 finishes in under a minute and forces every move to matter. Try playing in the centre. Try playing in a corner. Notice what the computer does when you play near its line of attack: a good Hex player builds two-cell bridges, two stones placed diagonally with two empty cells between them, where any attempt by the opponent to break the connection can be countered by playing the other empty cell. Bridges turn isolated stones into virtual edges. The shortest-path heuristic discovers them by accident.

Two things will happen as you play. First: every game will end with someone winning. There will not be a draw. There will not be a position in which both players have a chain and the game ties. There will not even be a position in which the board is full and neither player has a chain. We are going to prove that. Second: if you play sensibly and start near the middle of the board, you will start winning more often than the computer does. We are going to prove that too, but the proof will not say which first move to play, only that one of them works.

The first remarkable theorem: Hex cannot draw

In tic-tac-toe, two competent players will always draw. In Connect Four, perfect play yields a first-player win, but bad play between humans frequently produces a board jammed full of stones with no four in a row anywhere. In chess and Go, draws are part of the language of the game. Most board games admit a third outcome: nobody wins.

Hex does not. Whenever the board is filled completely with Blue and Red stones in any pattern at all, exactly one of the two players has a winning chain. Not at most one, exactly one. This is the first theorem of Hex, and it holds before any tournament rules or strategy enters the picture; it is a fact about the topology of the rhombic grid, not about the players.

Try to feel the necessity. A wider experiment: try to construct a fill, by hand, in which neither colour wins. If you ever achieve "no winner" with the board full, please tell us how, because the topological proof below says you cannot.

The proof has many flavours. The shortest is a planarity argument that treats the board as a topological gadget rather than a grid of cells.

No-draw theorem (Hein, c. 1942; Nash, 1948). If every cell of a Hex board is filled with either Blue or Red, then exactly one of the two players has a winning chain.

Sketch of the planar argument. Add four imaginary "outer" cells: a Blue cell labelled "top" lying above the top side of the rhombus, a Blue cell "bottom" below the bottom side, a Red cell "left" to the left of the left side, a Red cell "right" to the right of the right side. Each row-0 cell is adjacent to "top"; each row-(N−1) cell is adjacent to "bottom"; similarly for the Red outer cells. We get a slightly larger planar graph in which the question "does Blue have a winning chain on the original board" is equivalent to "are 'top' and 'bottom' in the same connected component of the Blue subgraph", and similarly for Red.

Suppose for contradiction that no Blue chain connects "top" to "bottom". Then in the Blue subgraph, "top" and "bottom" sit in different connected components. The Blue cells therefore split into two disjoint regions, one containing "top" and one containing "bottom". The boundary curve between these two regions (drawn in the plane) must therefore contain at least one arc that separates the upper and lower halves of the rhombus. That arc's two endpoints lie on the rhombus's left and right sides respectively, and along the arc the cells are necessarily Red (because they are not in either of Blue's connected components). The Red cells along the arc therefore form a connected chain from the left side to the right side, and Red wins. Symmetrically, if no Red chain connects "left" to "right", then Blue must have a chain connecting "top" to "bottom".

So at least one player always has a winning chain. The argument also shows at most one player can have one: if both had winning chains they would have to cross, and two paths of cells in a planar tiling where every interior vertex has degree three cannot cross without sharing a cell, which is impossible because Blue and Red are disjoint. Exactly one chain exists.

The proof is elementary in the sense that it uses no machinery beyond planarity, but it is genuinely topological, the argument depends on the fact that the rhombus has a well-defined boundary divided into two pairs of opposite sides, and that the cells tile the interior in a way where every interior vertex has degree three. None of this would work on a square grid without diagonal adjacency, and that is part of why Hex is the cleanest setting for a result of this shape.

There is a second proof, due to David Gale in 1979, that turns the no-draw theorem into a special case of the Brouwer fixed-point theorem. (And a third proof that runs the implication the other way: from the no-draw theorem one can give a combinatorial proof of Brouwer in two dimensions.) For our purposes, the planarity sketch above is enough, what we want next is the second remarkable theorem, the one about who wins.

The second remarkable theorem: the first player wins

Here is a question. In Nim, we proved that the position is winning for the player to move and we exhibited a specific opening move (XOR-balance the heaps). In Green Hackenbush we computed the Grundy value of the starting position and read off the winning move from that. In Domineering and Sums of Games we attached a numerical value to each component and decomposed the optimal play. Throughout the series, the proofs that "this position wins" have walked hand in hand with an algorithm for "here is the move that wins it."

For Hex on a symmetric board, we are about to prove the first player wins. Then we are going to fail to tell you what the first move should be. Not because the first move is unknown, for small boards (up to as of the early 2020s) the entire game tree has been solved by computer search and the winning openings catalogued, but because the proof itself never refers to any particular move. The proof is non-constructive. It tells us that a winning strategy exists without exhibiting one.

Strategy-stealing theorem (Nash, 1949). On a symmetric Hex board (any size with equal sides), the first player has a winning strategy under optimal play.

The proof goes as follows. Suppose for contradiction that the second player has a winning strategy . Then we are going to use against itself.

The first player makes any opening move at all, call it . Now the first player pretends, internally, that they are the second player on a board where was never played. Whenever it is the first player's turn, they consult to find what the (imagined) second player would do, and play that move. If ever asks the first player to play in the cell where already is, the first player simply plays any other empty move and continues the impersonation as if that were the new "extra" move.

The key fact is that an extra blue cell can never hurt Blue. If Blue has a winning chain on the board where they pretended was not there, then Blue still has a winning chain on the board where is there, adding a blue cell can only create new connections, never destroy any. So if would have won for the second player on the imagined board, the impersonation wins for the first player on the real board. We have constructed a winning strategy for the first player out of an alleged winning strategy for the second player.

But by the no-draw theorem (which we proved above), the game cannot be drawn, exactly one player wins. So either the first player has a winning strategy or the second player does, not both. We have just shown that if the second player has one, the first player has one too, which is impossible. The original assumption is false. The first player has a winning strategy.

It is worth pausing on what the proof does and does not give us. The proof says: a winning strategy exists. It does not say: here is the strategy. It does not even say: here is the first move. If you ask "what is the best opening move on a symmetric Hex board", the strategy-stealing argument refuses to answer, it merely promises that whichever move the first player picks, by following some sequence of replies, they can win. The proof is allergic to construction.

This is unusual in mathematics, but it is not unheard of. Existence proofs that do not produce examples have a long history: the proof that there exist transcendental numbers (Cantor's diagonal argument, before Hermite gave a specific transcendental), the proof that every continuous function on a closed interval attains its maximum (without telling you where), the proof that any finite group has a composition series. Hex is the cleanest game-theoretic example. Combined with the no-draw theorem, the strategy-stealing argument gives an absolutely-binding existence claim, there is a winning strategy for the first player, and you are entirely free to ignore the proof and try to find it.

Two observations make the slipperiness bite a little less. First, the strategy-stealing argument cannot run on every game. It uses the fact that adding an extra Blue stone is never bad for Blue, which is a specific feature of Hex, in Go an extra stone of your colour can be very bad indeed (it might fill in your own eye). Strategy stealing is not a generic gadget; it is a Hex-shaped one. Second, the argument cannot tell you which opening move wins because the opening move is genuinely unconstrained: from the proof's point of view, the first player can play anywhere and still win, because they have a "spare" stone to work with. The center is the strongest move in practice (and the first to be banned in tournaments under the swap rule), but the proof does not need it.

The swap rule and what tournament Hex looks like

Because the first player wins, competitive Hex uses a balancing convention called the swap rule. The first player plays an opening move; the second player either accepts that opening and plays on as the second player, or "swaps", claiming the played stone for themselves and now playing as if they were the first player from that position. The swap rule pushes the first player to choose an opening that is as close to balanced as possible: a strong move will be swapped (and now the second player has the strong position), and a weak move will be left alone (and the second player will win comfortably). The first player's best move under swap is the one for which the resulting position is most evenly balanced.

The swap rule does not contradict Nash's theorem. Nash's theorem says the first player wins under standard rules; under the swap rule, the players are effectively negotiating which side of the opening move to play, and the question of who wins becomes the question of whether any cell on the board produces an exactly-balanced game. On small boards (sizes up to 9 are solved as of the early 2020s) computer searches have catalogued every cell as winning for the player who has the stone in it, and no cell is exactly balanced, so under swap the first player chooses the opening that comes closest to balanced, and the second player swaps if and only if that opening is strictly winning. The mathematically interesting cells, in practice, are the ones near the long diagonal: they are weakest enough that the second player declines to swap, but strong enough that the first player can win with them.

None of this is in the original Nash argument. It is the kind of detail that emerges only when you sit down to play. If you went back to Figure 1 and took the small-board mode seriously, you have probably already noticed that opening near the corner is a nearly hopeless start.

Hex as a positional game

Hex belongs to a wider family. A positional game is one in which the board is a finite set of points, each player claims an unclaimed point on each turn, and the winning conditions are sets of points that one of the players must collect. Hex is a positional game where the winning sets are "chains from one side to the other"; tic-tac-toe is a positional game where the winning sets are the three-in-a-rows. Many puzzle and combinatorial games are positional.

The strategy-stealing argument works for any positional game in which (a) the game cannot be drawn and (b) an extra stone of your colour cannot be bad for you. Both conditions hold for Hex. The first condition fails for tic-tac-toe (which can draw), so strategy stealing alone does not tell you who wins tic-tac-toe; we need to do extra work, and we discover that with optimal play tic-tac-toe is in fact a draw. The second condition fails for Go, Othello, and most capture games, so strategy stealing does not apply there either.

Where it does apply, it is brutally efficient. Within the Hex family, the same argument can be ported to several variants, Y, Star, Havannah, and the more recent TwixT (with appropriate care), and each one inherits the "first player wins, but we cannot tell you how" verdict. The Hellinger-Hales-Jewett theorem and the Ramsey-theoretic existence results for general positional games descend from the same family; many of them are non-constructive in the same way.

None of this resolves the practical question of how to actually play. For that you have Figure 1 and the small-board game tree. If you played a few games and won a few, you are already doing what a hundred years of theory cannot do for you: producing winning moves on demand.

John Nash, Piet Hein, and Princeton common rooms

Hex was invented twice. The first inventor was the Danish polymath Piet Hein, who introduced the game in the December 1942 issue of the Copenhagen newspaper Politiken under the name Polygon. Hein also conjectured the no-draw property and, informally, the first-player win, though he did not have a proof of either at the time. Polygon was popular in Denmark during the war and circulated widely on hand-drawn boards.

The second inventor, in 1948, was the mathematician John Nash, then a graduate student at Princeton. Nash invented Hex independently (he was unaware of Hein's earlier game) and immediately gave the strategy-stealing proof of the first-player win. The game was known around Princeton's Fine Hall common room as "Nash" or "John", and the boards were laid out on the tile floor of the men's washroom (the floor was already a hexagonal tiling). David Gale, Martin Gardner, and others learned the game from Nash and spread it. Parker Brothers commercialised the game in 1952 under the name Hex, and Martin Gardner gave it a wide mathematical audience in his July 1957 Scientific American column, naming Hein as the original inventor and Nash as the rediscoverer.

Hein was a designer, poet, and engineer whose other inventions include the soma cube and the superellipse used in the layout of Stockholm's Sergels Torg. He introduced Polygon as a newspaper puzzle, accompanying it with the conjecture that the game cannot draw and a sketch of the planarity argument; the proof was made fully rigorous by later writers.

Nash's contribution was the strategy-stealing argument. The 1952 RAND report is the first written form of it in his hand; the proof had been folklore in the Princeton common room for several years before that. Nash's other contributions to game theory, the equilibrium concept that bears his name, on which his Nobel Prize in Economics was awarded, belong to a different branch of the subject (strategic games), but Hex is the place where his work intersects the combinatorial tradition that this series is about.

The connection to Go is partly historical and partly mathematical. Both games are about territory and connection on a board; both reward long-range vision; both reward thinking about which stones are almost connected. The Hex player's "bridge", two stones whose connection is unbreakable because either of two empty cells completes it, is a small-scale version of the Go player's miai, two equivalent moves of which the player can always answer one if the opponent takes the other. We will return to the miai idea in the next explainer, where Go endgames provide the only place in modern competitive board games where Conway-style game values are routinely computed in practice.

Takeaways

Hex's proofs stop telling you what to do. The no-draw theorem proves every full board has a winner without referring to any moves — it is purely topological. The strategy-stealing theorem proves the first player wins without naming a single opening or reply. Both are existence proofs without recipes.

Three things to carry forward:

  • Existence proofs versus constructive proofs. The strategy-stealing argument is the cleanest example we know of a non-constructive existence proof in game theory. The same shape recurs in tournament-style positional games, in Ramsey theory, in topology, in functional analysis. A proof that something exists is not the same as an algorithm for finding it, and learning to recognise the difference makes you a sharper reader of the rest of mathematics.
  • The "extra stone never hurts" lemma is the non-trivial part. The strategy-stealing argument hinges on the fact that an extra Blue stone cannot make Blue's position worse. That is a substantive claim about Hex's mechanics and it does not generalise to all games. When you read a strategy-stealing proof for a different game, the work is in checking the analogous monotonicity lemma, without it the argument falls apart.
  • Topology lives inside game theory. The no-draw theorem is the same theorem as the Brouwer fixed-point theorem in two dimensions. The Sprague-Grundy theory we built in earlier explainers is algebraic; the Hex theorems are topological. The next explainer (Go endgames) sits somewhere in between, with stones on a grid behaving like local games whose values can be added.

Explainer 13 takes the analytic machinery from the first eleven explainers (temperature, cooling, sums of games) and turns it loose on Go endgame positions, where it is actually used by professionals. Hex was the explainer about what the theory cannot do for you. Go endgames are where the theory does the most for the most people.
