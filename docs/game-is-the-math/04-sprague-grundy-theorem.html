The Sprague–Grundy Theorem

Part 4 of 15. Every impartial game is a Nim heap in disguise, and the disguise comes off via one operator: XOR. Play the Sum Challenge, watch three different games collapse into one number.

We are about to play a position that is three games at once: a Nim heap, a Hackenbush stalk, and a small subtraction game. One board, three component games, the reader moves in whichever one they like. No visible connection between them. And yet (if you know the trick from the last explainer) you can solve the whole thing by computing one number per component and XOR-ing them together. Win the XOR game, you win the real game.

That trick has a name. It is the Sprague–Grundy theorem: every impartial game, no matter how strangely drawn, is equivalent to a single Nim heap whose size is the game's Grundy value. Every. Impartial. Game. The theorem reduces combinatorial game theory for impartial games to Nim arithmetic, and it reduces sums of impartial games to XOR.

The question this explainer answers is: what happens when you play two impartial games side by side? A move is a move in exactly one of the component games; the other is untouched. You lose when you can't move in any component. This compound game is called the disjoint sum of the two games, and written . We will learn how to compute the Grundy value of a disjoint sum, and that rule will turn out to be the entire content of the Sprague–Grundy theorem in operational form.

Figure 2. The sum operator

Before we can prove anything about sums we have to agree on what a sum is. Here is the definition in one sentence: a position in is a pair , and a move in is a move in exactly one of the two components, leaving the other alone. The game ends when no move is possible in either component, which is the same as saying both components are at a terminal position.

This is not a hypothetical construction. Every time you have played Nim with three heaps, you were implicitly playing the sum of three one-heap games. A move in the three-heap game is a move in one heap, and the other two are left alone. The sum operator is already baked into how Nim works.

Once you have made a move or two, notice what the figure is not doing: it is not mixing tokens between the two components, and it is not creating new heaps in component B when you act on component A. A move leaves the other component exactly alone. That is the whole content of disjoint sum. Impartial games have a genuine operator, and that operator is commutative and associative (both sides of allow the same moves), and it has an identity: the empty game (no moves available) leaves any unchanged.

So impartial games under form a commutative monoid, the way integers under addition do. You will see in a moment that this monoid is more structured than that: it has a quotient that behaves like the natural numbers under XOR. The Grundy value is the quotient map.

Here is the proof sketch. "Sketch" because a fully rigorous account with all the equivalence bookkeeping takes a few pages, but the induction is short enough to fit in one paragraph.

Define the height of an impartial game position to be the length of the longest play sequence starting from . Terminal positions have height 0. Every non-terminal position has height equal to 1 plus the maximum height of its options. Because we assume the game is finite, every position has a well-defined finite height. Induction on height is exactly the right engine.

Position x has options with Grundy values {0, 1, 3}. By induction, each option is a Nim heap of the matching size. G(x) = \mathrm{mex}\{0,1,3\} = 2, and x plays exactly like a Nim heap of 2.

Figure 3. Proof by induction on game height. The base case handles terminals; the inductive step shows that a position whose options are equivalent to Nim heaps is itself equivalent to a Nim heap of size equal to the mex of the option values.

What does "equivalent" mean here? Two impartial games are equivalent if, for every third game C, the sum A + C and the sum B + C have the same winner under optimal play. The Sprague–Grundy theorem upgrades this to: A \equiv B iff G(A) = G(B), and equivalence is compatible with sums in the sense that A \equiv B forces A + C \equiv B + C. This is what lets you substitute a game for its Nim heap inside a larger sum without changing the strategic story.

The "reachability" caveat in the middle of that paragraph is the one subtlety to notice. The XOR tells you which target total you want (namely 0), but the move has to be legal in some component. Changing a Grundy value from to any value in is always legal (that is what "equivalent to a Nim heap of size " means), but you cannot move a Grundy value up. So look at the XOR of the total and each component Grundy value: you need at least one component where that XOR is strictly less than the current value.

Once you have won a few times, reshuffle the board and try a harder one. The computer always plays optimally from the Grundy values, so the only way to beat it is to play optimally too. If at any point you click a move that makes the total XOR non-zero, the computer will zero it back and push you toward the losing end.

What is the "strategy" you are actually using? It has three layers. First, which component to move in, pick any component where you can make the XOR drop to zero. Second, what target Grundy value to produce in that component, the XOR of the total with the component's current Grundy value. Third, which legal move realizes that target, this depends on the specific game. Nim heaps drop from k to any smaller value; Hackenbush stalks drop to the height of the cut edge; subtraction games drop by whatever you subtract. The theorem says "reduce to XOR", but the final step, realizing a target Grundy value as a move in the original game, is where the games still feel like themselves.

Figure 6. How this all started

Roland Sprague (German, 1894–1967) published the theorem in 1935 in Tôhoku Mathematical Journal, in a paper titled "Über mathematische Kampfspiele" ("On mathematical contest games"). He was a schoolteacher and amateur number theorist; the paper gives Nim-equivalence for impartial games in essentially the modern form.

Patrick Michael Grundy (British, 1917–1959) published the same theorem in 1939 in Eureka, the Cambridge undergraduate mathematics magazine, under the title "Mathematics and games". Grundy was 22 at the time and a Cambridge undergraduate; he had no knowledge of Sprague's work, which was in a German-language journal with limited circulation in wartime Britain.

Neither author was aware of the other, and neither received immediate recognition. The theorem entered the mainstream only in the 1950s through Richard Guy and Cedric Smith, and became the foundation of the whole subject when Berlekamp, Conway, and Guy wrote Winning Ways in 1982. Both names stuck; the single-barreled "Grundy value" and the double-barreled "Sprague–Grundy theorem" honor both discoverers, even though in strict priority order it should be Sprague's.

Figure 6. Two simultaneous discoveries, four years apart, across a language and a generation. Sprague (1935, Tôhoku) and Grundy (1939, Eureka). The combinatorial game theory that fills Winning Ways rests on this theorem, and the theorem rests on a short induction you just read.

Takeaways

Four claims that now carry forward into the rest of Act I and beyond.

  • Every impartial game is Nim. No exceptions. Every finite impartial game under normal play is equivalent to a Nim heap of some size; that size is the Grundy value computed by mex on options.
  • Sums are XOR. The Grundy value of a disjoint sum is the bitwise XOR of the component Grundy values. Multi-game positions collapse to a single number.
  • Losing iff zero. A position (single or compound) is losing for the player to move iff its Grundy value is zero. Winning move: drop the Grundy value to zero by making a legal change in some component.
  • Strategy has three layers. Pick the component to move in, pick the target Grundy value for that component, realize the target as a legal move in the component's own game. The first two layers are arithmetic; the third depends on the game's native rules.

Next we will see what happens when we break the "last move wins" convention. Misère play (last move loses) flips the theorem on its head and reveals that the tidy story of this explainer is fragile. The Grundy value, which determined everything under normal play, is almost useless under misère. The reader will spend explainer 5 appreciating how sharp the normal-play assumption really is.
