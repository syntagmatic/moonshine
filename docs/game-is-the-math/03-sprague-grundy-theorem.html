The Sprague-Grundy Theorem

Part 3 of 10. One number per position, computed by one rule, turns any impartial game into a Nim heap. Play three different games at once and the whole board collapses to an XOR.

At the bottom of this page is a board with three games on it: a two-heap Nim position, a Green Hackenbush stalk, and a pile of chips from which you may take one, two or three. On your turn you move in exactly one of them. Whoever makes the last move anywhere wins. Bouton's XOR rule from Part 1 handles the Nim part, but the chip pile has different moves. You need a number for each game that combines the way Nim heaps do. That number is the Grundy value, and it rests on a single operator.

The theorem

The disjoint sum of two games is the game in which a move is a move in exactly one component, and you lose when neither component has a move left. Nim with three heaps is the sum of three one-heap games. Two games A and B are equivalent, , if for every game C, the sums and have the same winner. Equivalent games can be swapped inside any sum without changing who wins.

The Sum Challenge

Here is the board from the opening, with one of five starting positions, each with a nonzero total. Type each component's Grundy value, then make the move that brings the total to 0 and play the game out. The computer moves to a total of 0 whenever it can, which by the theorem is optimal; when it cannot, it makes an arbitrary legal move, since from a zero position every move loses against correct play. One slip on your part and it takes the win.

The first two steps, picking the component and the target value, are the same arithmetic in every game. Only the last step, finding a legal move that reaches the target, depends on the rules. In Nim you pick a heap and XOR it with the total, the same way as in Part 1. A stalk is cut down to the target number of edges. From the chip pile you remove as many chips as the current value exceeds the target.
