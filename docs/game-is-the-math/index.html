The Game Is the Math

Fifteen-part interactive series on combinatorial game theory. Every explainer is a playable game. The theorems emerge from the play patterns.

You are going to learn combinatorial game theory by playing games. Not by reading about them. The subject — the mathematical study of games where both players have perfect information, there is no chance, and the last legal move decides the winner — is one of the most directly playable branches of mathematics. Conway, Berlekamp, and Guy's Winning Ways famously derived the entire theory by analyzing positions that anyone can reproduce on a napkin. This series follows the same pedagogical arc: you play the game first, notice a pattern in your losses, and then meet the theorem that explained what you already half-knew.

The centerpiece of every explainer is one playable game against an optimal computer opponent. You can win sometimes. You can also lose, and when you do, the explainer shows you the position you missed, the Grundy value that was against you, or the subtle misère endgame rule that flipped the position at the last move. The theory — Grundy values, the Sprague–Grundy theorem, surreal numbers, temperature — arrives as the explanation of things you have already seen in play, not as content to be learned first and applied second.

Three acts. Act I covers impartial games (both players have the same moves available). Act II extends the theory to partizan games (Left and Right have different moves), and introduces the surreal numbers. Act III looks at games in the wild — Hex, Go endgames, Dots and Boxes, loopy games — and the limits of what the theory can say.

Current state: All 15 explainers shipped across three acts. Library: lib/cgt-math.js (mex, xor, Grundy values) and lib/cgt-games.js (Nim engine with normal and misère conventions); Acts II and III explainers implement their game-specific engines inline. Test harness at lib/test.html.

Based on Berlekamp, Conway & Guy, Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays (A K Peters 2001–2004), Conway, On Numbers and Games (A K Peters 1976), and Siegel, Combinatorial Game Theory (AMS 2013). Part of the Moonshine explainer project.
