Domineering

Part 7 of 10. Left lays vertical dominoes, Right lays horizontal ones, and the last player to place wins. The smallest interesting board already has a value that is not a number.

The board is a rectangle of empty cells. Left covers two empty cells stacked in one column; Right covers two empty cells side by side in one row. Covered cells stay covered. If you cannot place your kind of domino on your turn, you lose. This is the convention of Winning Ways: Left is vertical, Right is horizontal.

The two players see the same empty square differently. Left needs columns, Right needs rows, and that asymmetry is what pushes the values past plain numbers.

The computer runs a memoized search of the entire game tree before each move, so whenever it has a winning move it finds one; when it has none it plays its first legal move. The 3×3 and 4×4 boards are wins for whoever moves first, so you can win them. The 3×4 board (3 rows, 4 columns) is a win for Right no matter who starts, so you cannot.

Losing games tend to look alike: the computer's horizontal dominoes cut the board into short, wide pieces where you have nothing to do. The game is partizan, so "who wins?" has two answers, one for each player moving first. A game value answers both at once.

The 2×2 board is a switch

On an empty 2×2 board, either Left move fills a column and leaves a 2×1 column worth . Either Right move fills a row and leaves a 1×2 row worth . After merging the duplicate options,

This is not a number. The simplicity rule of Part 6 picks a number strictly between the options, and that needs the Left option below the Right option. Here Left's option is above Right's, so there is nothing to pick. The position is the switch , the standard example of a hot game. Whoever moves first gains a free move. In a number, moving only uses up your own resources; in a switch, moving is profit, and both players race to do it.

Sums of pieces

After a few moves the empty cells split into regions no domino can bridge. Each region is its own game and the board is their sum. Numbers add as numbers. A number plus a switch is still hot.

The 4×1 column is worth +2 and the 1×4 row is worth −2, so they cancel, and the board is worth exactly the 2×2 switch. Whoever moves first takes the switch and wins by one move.

Small boards are computable, large ones are not

Figure 6. Canonical values of empty rectangles, rows by columns, computed by the in-page evaluator when you load this page. Plain cells are numbers. Shaded cells are hot: Left's stop is above Right's. Gray cells are not numbers but have equal stops, so they differ from a number by an infinitesimal. The 4×4 form is too long to print; both its stops are 0, yet it is a first-player win.

Even at this size the values are irregular: 3×3 is the same switch as 2×2, 2×3 is the lopsided switch , and 3×4 is the number . There is no formula for general rectangles.

Göran Andersson invented the game around 1973, and Martin Gardner published it in Scientific American in 1974 under the name Crosscram. For larger boards, only outcomes are known, from game-tree searches that stop once the winner is decided. Breuker, Uiterwijk and van den Herik showed in 2000 that 8×8 is a first-player win; 9×9 followed soon after, Nathan Bullock solved 10×10 in 2002, and Uiterwijk solved 11×11 in 2016. All of these are first-player wins; 5×5 is a second-player win. The exact value of the 8×8 board has never been computed.
