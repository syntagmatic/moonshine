Temperature and Cooling

Part 10 of 15. Some games are hot — both players are itching to move there. Some are cold — they have settled into a number nobody can change. The temperature of a game says how badly both sides want the next move in it, and the right way to play a sum of games is to play in the hottest one first. The whole theory is a thermometer.

Imagine you are playing two games at once. Game one is a Domineering corner where, whoever moves first, the score swings wildly in their favour. Game two is a settled patch of board worth exactly two points to Left and nothing anyone can do will change that. Where do you move? Game one, obviously. Game two has nothing left to fight over. Game one is still up for grabs.

Now imagine three live games, all hot, with different swings: 6, 4, and 2 points respectively. The biggest-swing game is the most urgent place to be. Same instinct.

This explainer turns "the most urgent place to be" into a number called the temperature of a game, and turns the instinct above into a strategy for sums of partizan games. Once you have the temperature, the rule for a sum is automatic: play in the hottest component first.

The story has two halves. The metric — temperature, stops, the picture called a thermograph. And the strategy — the play-the-hottest rule, demonstrated against a "naive" opponent who plays by other criteria. The bridge between them is an operation called cooling, named by analogy with cooling a metal: turn the heat down a little at a time and watch what stays hot and what freezes. The full theory was developed in Winning Ways by Berlekamp, Conway, and Guy and applied to real Go endgames by Berlekamp in the 1990s; we will meet that history at the end.

What "hot" means: a game both players want to move in

A game is hot when both Left and Right would profit from being the one to move in it. Concretely: if the value when Left moves first is better for Left than the value when Right moves first, then both players have a reason to move there. The game is contested.

The cleanest illustration is a switch: a game whose only options are a single number for Left and a single number for Right. In Conway notation a switch is , where a is what Left can move to and b is what Right can move to. When the switch is hot — Left wants the move because it lands at the higher value, Right wants the move because it lands at the lower value. When a = b the switch is just the number, and nothing is contested. The case a < b is cold in a different way — neither side wants to move voluntarily — and the simplification rules from explainer 8 reduce it to a number. The interesting case is the first one.

Temperature: half the swing

The temperature of a game measures how much both players want the next move in it. The natural unit is half the swing: if Left moving first lands at L(G) and Right moving first lands at R(G), then each player gains from being the mover. That gain is the temperature.

The mean is what you would expect on average from a fair coin deciding who moves; the temperature is half the spread around the mean.

Cold games — numbers — have temperature zero. Nothing to fight about means no temperature. A hot switch with a > b has temperature (a − b) / 2. The hotter the game, the more the players want the move and the larger the swing around the mean.

Two cases are worth flagging. When a = b the temperature is zero — a "switch" with the same option on both sides is just that number. When a < b we report the temperature as zero by convention; the game is equivalent to a number under the simplicity rules. The interesting case is a > b, where the temperature is positive and the switch is genuinely contested.

Cooling: turning the heat down

Where does the word temperature actually come from? Berlekamp, Conway, and Guy named it by analogy with thermodynamics. The analogy runs through an operation called cooling, which acts on a game the way lowering the ambient temperature acts on a hot piece of metal: the heat bleeds out of the components that are still hot and the cold ones are left alone.

Cooling is parameterised by a non-negative number t, also called the temperature of the cooling. Cooling a game by t means charging each player a tax of t per move. The new game is

"Tax of t per move" is the operative phrase. Moving is no longer free; you pay t from your side of the score sheet for the privilege. The hotter the game, the more you can afford to pay and still come out ahead. As t rises, hot components are taxed away to their means; cold components (numbers) are unaffected because there are no moves left to tax.

For a switch with a > b, cooling by t gives a smaller switch {a − t | b + t}. As t rises from 0 to (a − b) / 2 the upper option drops and the lower option rises, until at exactly t = (a − b) / 2 they meet at the mean. Any further cooling would push them past each other, and the game freezes into its mean. The temperature of a game is the smallest t that freezes it into a number.

The picture of this freezing process is called a thermograph. We plot the cooled game's value on the horizontal axis and the cooling temperature t on the vertical axis. For each t the cooled game is the interval [b + t, a − t], and we draw its two endpoints. As t rises the two walls slope inward at slope 1 until they meet at the mast position. From there a single vertical mast climbs upward — the game has frozen and no more cooling will change it.

Three things are visible in the thermograph that were invisible in the formulas. First, every switch has the same shape — a triangle resting on the value axis with a vertical mast above it; only the size and position of the triangle change. Second, the temperature is the height of the mast above the value axis. Third, the thermograph is the entire game stored as a picture: the foot of the blue wall is the Left stop L = a, the foot of the red wall is the Right stop R = b, the mast is at the mean, and the mast's height is the temperature.

For more complicated games the thermograph stops being a clean triangle. There can be steps — places where the wall jumps because a different option becomes optimal — and the mast can be off-centre. Berlekamp's thermographs for Go endgame positions look like zig-zag staircases. But the basic intuition is the one in this figure: a thermograph records how a game's value changes as you turn down its temperature.

Strategy in a sum: play the hottest first

Now we get to the payoff. Suppose you are playing a sum of partizan games — several games in parallel, with each move affecting exactly one component (the framework explainer 11 will formalise). The temperature gives you the strategy: play in the component with the highest temperature.

The reasoning is the swing argument from the opening, made precise. Each component has a "willingness to pay" equal to its temperature — that is the value of being the mover, measured in score units. A move in a hotter component is worth more than a move in a cooler one. If you take the cool move first, your opponent will take the hot one next and you have handed them the larger swing for free. Take the hottest live component first; only when all hot components are settled should you play in the lukewarm and cold ones.

Why does playing the hottest first work? A hand-wavy way to see it: a turn in a component with temperature t swings the score by t in your favour, compared to letting the opponent take it. If you and your opponent alternate, the total score is the sum of temperatures of components you took minus the sum of temperatures of components they took. To maximise that sum — given that you alternate — you want to be the one grabbing the biggest temperatures, which is the greedy "hottest first" rule.

This is hand-wavy because there are corrections. After your move a component's temperature might drop, and a previously cooler component might become the new hottest. The rule should be applied dynamically: at each turn pick the currently hottest live component. With that proviso it is correct for sums of switches and is a strong heuristic for general partizan sums. The exact theorem with bounds is in Winning Ways volume 1.

The strategy in one line. When playing a sum of partizan games, on each turn move in the component with the highest temperature. Cold components (numbers) can be left for last because they have nothing to contest. Hot components must be claimed in order of how hot they are — the hottest claim, then the next, then the next.

Berlekamp and the Go endgame

The most famous application of temperature theory is Elwyn Berlekamp's analysis of Go endgames. Go has, for centuries, been a game where the endgame — the phase after the territories are mostly settled and only a handful of "small" local fights remain — was the province of pure intuition. Professional Go players ranked sequences of endgame moves by feel, and the feel was good enough that the question "what is the right sequence?" was rarely asked precisely.

Berlekamp asked it precisely. In a 1994 paper with David Wolfe (Mathematical Go: Chilling Gets the Last Point) and a series of follow-ups, he showed that many local Go endgame positions are, in CGT terms, switches or sums of switches, and that the right way to play them is exactly the temperature-ordered strategy of Figure 4. He then went on to construct artificial Go positions where his algorithm identified the optimal move and a 9-dan professional did not.

Berlekamp was a professor of mathematics and electrical engineering at UC Berkeley, a co-author of Winning Ways for Your Mathematical Plays, and the central figure in applying combinatorial game theory to real games. His 1994 book with David Wolfe took the temperature framework that he, Conway, and Guy had developed in the 1970s and turned it on Go endgames — positions where the global game is large but locally decomposes into independent fights with computable temperatures.

The book introduced an operation called chilling (a small variant of cooling) that adapts the partizan theory to the specific arithmetic of Go score-keeping. With chilling, each local fight in an endgame has a temperature, and Berlekamp showed that playing the hottest fight first is optimal — and that this strategy can locate optimal endgame moves that even professional players miss. The book contained problem positions that were eventually used to test whether top human players could match the algorithm's recommendations. The algorithm won.

The wider lesson is the one in Figure 4 and one that holds across combinatorial game theory: a hard global game can be a sum of small, computable local games, and the right way to play the sum is to play the hottest local game first. Berlekamp's Go work is the most dramatic case in point, but it generalises. Several of the techniques in this explainer were named in the 1970s with Go in mind, even before the application was published.

What we take with us

The temperature of a game is a single non-negative number that measures how much both players want the next move in it. For a switch with a > b, the temperature is half the difference and the mean is half the sum. The strategy in a sum of partizan games is to play in the component with the highest temperature first, and to keep doing it until everything is cold. The thermograph is the picture of how a game's value changes as you cool it.

Three things to carry forward to the next explainers:

  • Hot vs cold is the partizan analogue of winning vs losing. Impartial games had a binary classification: win or lose for the player to move. Partizan games have a continuous one: how hot, how cold, what mean. The temperature is the partizan way of saying "this is contested" and the mean is the partizan way of saying "this is settled."
  • The hottest-first rule is greedy and approximately correct. Like greedy algorithms in optimization, "play the hottest live component" is not always optimal in pathological cases, but it is correct for sums of switches and is a strong heuristic for everything else. The exact theorems (with bounds on the suboptimality) are in Winning Ways, but for everyday play the greedy rule wins.
  • Cooling and temperature were invented by analogy with thermodynamics. The vocabulary — heat, temperature, mean, freezing — is not metaphor for the sake of metaphor. The mathematical operation of cooling a game by a tax behaves like physical cooling because it removes the components' freedom to move, leaving behind only their settled value. The thermograph is a phase diagram.

Explainer 11 takes the framework of "sums of games" — used informally throughout Acts I and II — and gives it the careful treatment it deserves. Sums are the operation that makes combinatorial game theory more than a list of curiosities about individual games; they are how games combine, and how the local theorems we have collected (Sprague–Grundy for impartial sums, temperature for partizan sums) become global strategies. The temperature rule is a special case of the general principle that the structure of a sum is determined by the structure of its summands.
