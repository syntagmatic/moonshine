Data and Statistics

Inference lives in several dialects across Moonshine. Frequentist point estimates in algorithms-ml, Bayesian posteriors in autoresearch, the Fisher metric and KL divergence in information-geometry, descriptive multivariate plots in parallel-coordinates and bioinformatics. This article lays those dialects out as a single poset and shows which series speaks which.

Every data-heavy series in the atlas confronts the same basic problem. A sample arrives; something has to be said about the process that produced it. The split between series is not what they do with the sample; it is which inferential language they default to. Parallel coordinates and bioinformatics stay close to the ground, with descriptive views that do not yet commit to a probability model. Algorithms-ml and decision-trees climb to point estimates, loss minimisation, bootstrap resamples. Autoresearch defaults to Bayesian thinking about evaluator noise and posterior belief. Information geometry collapses the whole stack onto a manifold and watches Fisher, KL, and natural gradient emerge as one object.

Three branches fan out of learn from data. The frequentist branch runs through MLE, confidence intervals, the bootstrap, hypothesis testing. The Bayesian branch runs through priors, posteriors, MCMC, credible intervals. The information-geometric branch pulls the other two together via the Fisher metric, KL divergence, and exponential families. Below both, a descriptive tier hosts the histogram / density / quantile / heatmap view that precedes any inference at all.

The shape of the Hasse matters as much as any one node. The frequentist and Bayesian branches meet at MLE / MAP, where the posterior with a flat prior collapses into a point estimate. The information-geometric branch quietly sits above both: Fisher as the Hessian of KL, the exponential family as the flat manifold where MLE becomes a moment-matching projection, the natural gradient as the coordinate-free version of steepest descent. Local KL equals Fisher is the single lemma that joins the two halves of the tree.

Figure 2 · Descriptive → inferential → causal

A ladder of commitment, each rung assumes more about the data-generating process
Reading top-down, each rung needs the rung above and commits to more. Descriptive summaries assume nothing. Inferential frameworks assume a probability model. Causal inference assumes a causal graph as well. The chips on each rung are the Moonshine explainers that live at that level of commitment — the lowest rungs are populated by visualisation-heavy series, the highest rungs almost entirely by decision-trees and autoresearch.

The climb from describe to infer to intervene is one of the few places where Moonshine's corpus lines up almost perfectly with a textbook ordering. The lowest rung is visualisation. The volcano plot, the MA plot, the parallel-coordinate view do not yet have a likelihood attached. The middle rungs are where probability models enter: gradient descent as MLE, Bayes' theorem, bootstrap resamples, MCMC. The top rung is causal: propensity scores, causal forests, uplift models in causal trees. The ladder is also how a project usually grows. You start by drawing the data, only then fit a model, and only then, rarely, argue that an edge in the model is causal.

Figure 3 · Pick a framework

Frequentist · Bayesian · information-geometric, concepts and series per framework
Click a column to highlight it. Each framework answers the same question — what is the parameter? — with a different object. Frequentist: a point estimate plus a procedure with coverage. Bayesian: a distribution over parameters updated by data. Information-geometric: a point on a manifold, with distance measured by KL. The footer shows which Moonshine series leans into the selected framework, and which explainer is the canonical entry.

The three frameworks are not mutually exclusive and any serious data series uses all three. But each series has a default dialect. Algorithms-ml is frequentist by reflex: gradient descent, cross-validation, k-nearest neighbours, PCA as an eigendecomposition of a covariance matrix. Autoresearch is Bayesian by construction: the evaluator is noisy, the scorecard is a posterior over quality, proposer strategies are updated as evidence arrives. Information-geometry is the unifier: it shows MLE as a Bregman projection, EM as alternating projection, the natural gradient as the Fisher-preconditioned update that makes the other two frameworks compatible. The cross-series overlap that drives the rest of this atlas is, in the data case, the overlap between these three dialects applied to the same underlying problem.

What this view buys

Data and statistics is the theme where Moonshine's series are most obviously talking past each other. A frequentist MLE, a Bayesian posterior mode, and a KL projection onto an exponential family are literally the same number. The Hasse shows the shared structure, the ladder shows the climb from description to causation, and the framework picker shows which series you should trust to speak which dialect well. If you want to learn inference end-to-end from Moonshine, read algorithms-ml for the frequentist basics, autoresearch for the Bayesian view of evaluation, and information geometry to see why the other two agree.
