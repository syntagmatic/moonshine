Spectral methods

Six moonshine series solve the same equation. Av = λv is the punchline of graph clustering, of PCA, of Fourier analysis, of normal-mode physics, of Koopman dynamics, of impedance spectroscopy, and of Hecke operator theory. This article is the Hasse diagram that fits over all of them.

The eigenproblem is a ladder, not a single rung. The operator at the top, Av = λv, forks by structure (symmetric vs. normal vs. non-normal vs. infinite-dimensional) and then forks again by domain, because the matrix A you care about depends on whether you are studying a graph, a covariance, a time-evolution, a Hessian, a crystal lattice, or a cell impedance. This article lays out the fork.

The first fork is structural. A symmetric (or Hermitian) operator has real eigenvalues and an orthogonal eigenbasis. Drop symmetry and you pick up either normal operators (still diagonalisable, possibly over the complex plane) or non-normal ones (Koopman, Frobenius-Perron, advection) where left and right eigenvectors disagree and pseudospectra matter. Drop finite-dimensionality and the spectrum splits into point, continuous, and residual parts, which is exactly the regime Schrödinger problems and Hecke operators live in.

The second fork is operator choice. A graph gives you the Laplacian L = D − A, and the smallest nontrivial eigenvector, the Fiedler vector, is the cut that bisects the graph most gently. A data matrix gives you the covariance, whose top eigenvectors are PCA axes and whose inverse square root is the whitening map. A shift-invariant operator gives you the DFT, because translations and the DFT share an eigenbasis. A nonlinear dynamical system gives you the Koopman operator, and its truncation to observables is DMD. A potential gives you the Hamiltonian, whose eigenstates are stationary modes. The fork is not arbitrary. It is structure all the way down.

The demo is deliberately narrow, because breadth is a property of the caption rather than the figure. Five tiny graphs, five spectra, one eigenvector drawn on top. But the badges tell the cross-series story. The path graph is the discrete analogue of the one-dimensional Schrödinger problem; its eigenvectors are sines. The cycle is the discrete Fourier transform in disguise; its eigenvectors are roots of unity. The two-cluster graph has its Fiedler vector split cleanly between the clusters, which is exactly spectral clustering. The star graph has a spectrum that algorithms-ml would recognise as the degree-distribution signature of a hub, and the complete graph collapses to a single repeated eigenvalue, the extremal case.

Figure 3 · Spectral recipe matrix

Six operators, six series, one matrix of applications

Each filled cell is an explainer (or a cluster of explainers) in that series where the row operator is the main object of study. The glyph is a short-hand for the specific flavour: F for Fiedler / spectral cut, P for principal components, D for DFT-diagonalisation, K for Koopman / DMD, H for Hamiltonian eigenstates, N for Nyquist / complex impedance, He for Hecke operator. The empty cells are not absences of mathematical content; they are absences from the current corpus.

Read the matrix two ways. Along a row, you see the operator stretched across domains: the Laplacian row lights up four different series because graph-cutting is a ubiquitous move. Along a column, you see a single series as a collection of spectral moves: algorithms-ml uses covariance (PCA), the Laplacian (attention-as-message-passing can be read spectrally), and the transfer operator (diffusion models). The payoff is that once you have internalised the ladder at the top of Figure 1, no domain feels surprising. You are always solving the same equation.

The remaining article in this series, geometry and manifolds, picks up from here. Every eigenbasis is also a choice of coordinates, and every choice of coordinates is a chart on some manifold. The Fisher information metric that information-geometry builds over a statistical family is exactly the kernel of the expected Hessian, which means natural gradient descent is an eigen-decomposition of the parameter-space Hessian. Spectral methods do not end at the spectrum; they flow into the geometry, which is the next poset over.
