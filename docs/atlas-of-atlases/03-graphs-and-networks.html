Graphs and Networks

A poset of vertex-and-edge structures — from bare graph to Cayley graph, persistence complex, quiver, neighbour graph — with leaves drawn from nine Moonshine series. The same concept lights up in tda, lattice-simulation, bioinformatics, and mathematical-diagrams simultaneously, and that coincidence is the figure.

A graph is a pair (V, E). Almost every refinement — directed, weighted, simplicial, labelled, Cayley, chordal, planar — is a graph plus extra structure, which is exactly what a taxonomy should encode. The figure below is that taxonomy. Eighteen concepts, ordered by generality. Hover any of them and its up-cone (more general versions) lights purple, its down-cone (specialisations and concrete instances) lights amber, and the side panel fills with explainer chips from whichever series ship a worked example.

Figure 1 · Concept taxonomy

Graph-theoretic concepts, partially ordered by generality
↑ more general — supergroups of the concept ↓ more specific — refinements and instances hover any node; side panel fills with explainer chips across series
Rank 0 at the top is the bare definition — a set of vertices and a set of edges. Rank grows downward as we add structure: direction, weight, a group action, a higher-dimensional face, a distance threshold, a probabilistic transition. Multi-series nodes are the load-bearing ones. Cayley graph appears in mathematical-diagrams, parallel-coordinates, and lattice-simulation at once. Simplicial complex appears in tda and emergence. DAG appears in decision-trees, algorithms-ml, and bioinformatics.

Two kinds of edges live in this diagram. The solid backbone is subtype: a tree is a special DAG, a DAG is a special directed graph, a directed graph is a graph with an asymmetric edge relation. The same logic threads Cayley graph → vertex-transitive graph → regular graph → graph. The other kind of edge is enrichment: a simplicial complex isn't exactly a subtype of graph, but it refines one by adding 2-faces and up. I treat both as downward edges because the information flow is the same — the specialisation carries the general structure as a forgetful footprint.

What the cross-series chips make visible is how lopsided the reuse is. The tree node shows up in five places at once: decision-trees uses it as a classifier, grateful-dead uses it as a setlist dendrogram, bioinformatics uses it as a phylogeny, algorithms-ml uses it as an ensemble member, lattice-simulation uses it as a spanning structure for an octree. The bipartite node is nearly as promiscuous — parallel-coordinates' two-axis duality, bioinformatics' gene-sample heatmaps, emergence's opinion dynamics. Meanwhile the quiver node has exactly one leaf, in mathematical-diagrams. That asymmetry is the geography of the atlas.

Figure 2 · Canonical drawings

One tiny drawing per concept, with its cross-series reference chips
The taxonomy gives generality ordering; the gallery gives visual identity. Every concept has a canonical drawing, and the chips underneath each tile name every explainer that leans on it. The gallery is deliberately small: the point is that six drawings you could sketch on a napkin — a tree, a DAG, a cycle, a bipartite graph, a simplicial complex, a Cayley graph — are the vocabulary out of which almost every diagram in the atlas is built.

There is a reason the canonical drawings are all simple. Most visual-analytic work in the Moonshine corpus is not about novel graph types — it is about overlaying extra structure onto one of these six skeletons. The Fiedler partition in lattice-simulation and bioinformatics is a two-colouring of an undirected graph. The MFCC cluster tree of Grateful Dead shows is a tree with timbral colour painted on each leaf. The Rips complex is a simplicial complex parameterised by a distance. The cellular automaton transition diagram is a Cayley graph of a shift semigroup. Learning the six shapes well is most of the work.

Figure 3 · Pair intersection

Pick two series; see which graph concepts they share
Two series placed on left and right. Each graph concept is drawn at a radial angle; its three arcs report how many explainers from series A, series B, and both use it. The middle column is the intersection — concepts shared between the two series. The default pairing, topological-data-analysis × bioinformatics, shows the shared spine: simplicial complex, tree, bipartite, neighbour graph.

The intersection view answers a question the taxonomy can't: for any two series, how much of their graph-theoretic vocabulary is the same? Moving the pickers around reveals structure. Decision-trees × algorithms-ml shares trees, DAGs, and Markov chains. Grateful-dead × bioinformatics shares segue graphs, dendrograms, and co-occurrence matrices — all instances of weighted graphs and hierarchical trees. Parallel-coordinates × mathematical-diagrams shares the Cayley graph. Lattice-simulation × topological-data-analysis shares the neighbour graph, the simplicial complex, and, through graph automorphisms, the Cayley graph. The empty cells are informative too: the Monster never meets a decision tree.
