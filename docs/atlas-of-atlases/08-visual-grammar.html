Visual Grammar

The chart types and interactive idioms themselves form a poset. Mark-level primitives at the top, full interactive experiences at the bottom, layout patterns in between. This is the self-referential closing view: the grammar of the figure you are looking at is on the figure.

Read enough of Moonshine in a row and the visualisations start to rhyme. The Hasse diagrams that organise the subgroup lattice of D4, the ADE classification, and the lattice of type refinements are the same chart type three times over. The force-directed layouts in emergence, tda, and bioinformatics are the same relaxation running on three different adjacency structures. The parallel-axes figure gives its name to a whole series and still shows up in information-geometry and bioinformatics whenever there are more than three dimensions worth looking at. The grammar is finite; it just gets applied a lot.

Hover or click an idiom above. The chips list the Moonshine series that actually use it.
Rank 0 is mark-level: point, line, area, path, rectangle, glyph, text. Rank 1 is visual channels: position, colour, size, shape, opacity. Rank 2 is the layouts these primitives compose into. Rank 3 is the interactive experiences a reader actually meets. The edge from point upward to scatter is the same composition as rect upward to heatmap; the chart type is not a primitive, it is a choice about how to spend the primitives.

The Hasse layout is itself a layout idiom, and it sits on the figure at rank 2. Every article in this atlas-of-atlases series uses it. The semantic is that edges go upward toward more general / more primitive, downward toward more specific / more composed, and a hover paints both cones at once. The chart type is doing the explaining, not sitting underneath it. That is the strongest argument I know for letting the grammar show.

Move from the top of the Hasse diagram to the bottom and you are walking the same path that builds every Moonshine visualisation. A point is nothing on its own. Give it an x and y channel and you have a scatter. Replace point with rect and quantise the channels and you have a heatmap. Keep the rect but stack in one dimension and you have a histogram or a ridge plot. Make the cells conditional on a category and you have small multiples. Swap linear axes for radial and you have a chord or a radial Hasse. None of this is specific to Moonshine; what is specific is how often the same choices reappear. Force-directed layouts live in at least four series. Parallel coordinates gives its name to one series and recurs in three more. Hasse diagrams show up in six.

The article is arguing about the grammar it is written in.

There is a mild embarrassment to writing an article about visual grammar in visual grammar. If I described force-directed layouts without showing one, I would be asking you to take the argument on faith. If I showed a force-directed layout without saying which series use it, I would be back in the trap this atlas tries to escape, where a chart type is never connected to the places it lives. So the gallery above shows each idiom and names the series it comes from, with live chips you can click. The chart type is the example of itself.

Rows are idioms, columns are all 21 Moonshine series. A filled cell means the series uses the idiom somewhere; a darker cell means the idiom is part of that series’ spine rather than a one-off. The densest rows — scatter, line, small-multiples, KaTeX, brush, hover — are the grammar nearly every series speaks. The sparser rows — radial, chord, choropleth, field-visualisation — are where each series finds its local dialect.

A few things are worth pointing out in that matrix. The top few rows (scatter, line, small-multiples, annotation, hover, brush) are close to universal; if a Moonshine series cannot make a scatter and annotate it, it does not exist yet. The Hasse-diagram row is denser than you might expect: six of the 21 series reach for it, not counting this one. The parallel-coordinates row lights up four columns: parallel-coordinates itself, bioinformatics, information-geometry, exceptional-atlas. The force-directed row lights up emergence, tda, bioinformatics, algorithms-ml, grateful-dead. And then there are the idioms that live in one column and nowhere else — field-visualisation in SPH, choropleth in japan-earthquakes — the fingerprints of those individual series.

This is the last article in the atlas-of-atlases. The previous seven each took a piece of mathematical or computational machinery — groups, graphs, spectra, manifolds, dynamics, data, inference — and showed how it sprawled across the corpus. This one takes the visual machinery and does the same thing. The punchline is modest: there are maybe thirty idioms and twenty-one series, and most of the reason Moonshine feels coherent is that the grammar is shared. The Hasse on Figure 1 is the same Hasse you have seen in seven previous articles and in six other series. That is not redundancy. That is the grammar working.
