Dynamics and time

Six or seven Moonshine series spend most of their time simulating something. Under the hood they are all instances of the same small zoo of time-evolution formalisms. This article lays that zoo out as a single Hasse diagram, sorts it into a deterministic-vs-stochastic quadrant, then lets you pick a formalism and see which explainers actually use it.

An ODE, a Markov chain, a cellular automaton and an agent-based model all solve the same underlying problem: given the state of a system at time t, what is its state at time t + dt? The answers differ on two axes. Is the update rule deterministic or does it sample from a distribution? Is time advanced in discrete ticks or as a continuous flow? That two-axis split, plus the question of whether state lives on a lattice or on the real line, is enough to explain why the same half-dozen formalisms keep reappearing across the Moonshine corpus.

From dynamical system at the top down to specific integrators
↑ more general, the formalism this one specialises ↓ more specific, concrete integrators or schemes that instantiate it hover a node for its series badges and example explainers
The root dynamical system splits on two independent binaries — deterministic vs stochastic, continuous-time vs discrete-time — and those mix further with continuous-state vs discrete-state. Specific workhorses (leapfrog, Metropolis, lattice Boltzmann, random walk) sit at the bottom. Hover any node to see which Moonshine explainers instantiate it.

The poset is not strict, most nodes have two or three parents, because every real formalism sits at an intersection of binaries. Metropolis-Hastings is discrete-time and stochastic and on a discrete state space; leapfrog is discrete-time and deterministic and on a continuous state space; a Langevin SDE is continuous-time, stochastic, continuous-state. What the diagram gives you is a way to compare them: move up for generality, move down for a runnable scheme. The up-cone of lattice Boltzmann passes through discrete-velocity PDE, cellular automaton and kinetic equation before it reaches dynamical system. That is exactly the ancestry the lattice-Boltzmann explainer traces.

Three families dominate the landscape. The ODE family flows deterministic states on continuous time: the RC-circuit model of a lithium cell, the Lotka-Volterra equations, any strange attractor. The Markov family takes discrete jumps between states under a transition kernel: a Markov chain, Metropolis on the Ising model, the Nagel-Schreckenberg traffic rule. The agent / CA family is a special case: identical local rules applied in parallel on a lattice or graph, which is how cellular automata, flocking, SPH particles and Boltzmann lattices are all put together. The Hasse makes the inheritance explicit.

Each chip is an actual Moonshine explainer, placed in the cell whose formalism it implements. A series can legitimately live in several cells — emergence has ODE explainers, CA explainers and Langevin explainers all at once, and those chips land in three different quadrants. The lower-right cell is by far the densest: sampling problems are the bread and butter of computational work, and Metropolis, random walks and agent-based models all flow into it.

The split is unforgiving. An explainer either admits noise or it does not; it either advances in ticks or in dt. The bottom-right is the noisy discrete quadrant (Metropolis-Hastings, Ising, Nagel-Schreckenberg, opinion dynamics, percolation) and it is where most of the lattice-simulation series and half of emergence live. The top-left, the ODE/PDE quadrant, is narrower but has the lithium cell, predator-prey, reaction-diffusion, vortex streets and the full Hamiltonian flow chapter of lattice-simulation. The top-right holds the symplectic integrators and classical CAs: the same update, applied forever, with no dice. The bottom-left (SDE, CTMC, Langevin) is where the Brownian ratchet and Langevin dynamics sit, and where earthquake inter-arrival times look a lot like a continuous-time Markov point process.

Six families that cover almost everything simulated in the corpus. The view on the right lists every series that has at least one explainer in the selected family, grouped by series, with the specific explainers linked. When you click ODE and see both the lithium-ion RC model and Lotka-Volterra appear, that is a cross-series overlap by formalism rather than by subject matter.

The six wedges are not a partition of the Hasse, they are the six formalisms that actually carry weight across the corpus. ODE and SDE are the two continuous-time families. Markov chain and Metropolis/MCMC are the two discrete-time stochastic families, separated because the second has a detailed-balance invariant. Cellular automaton and agent-based are the two lattice / particle families; the first has a fixed grid and the second does not. Between these six, the emergence catalogue, the lattice-simulation series, the lithium-ion cell and the Japan earthquakes point-process story are all covered.

What the three figures share is the claim that time-evolution formalism is a first-class dimension of the Moonshine corpus. It is not a tag we happened to apply; it is the shape of a non-trivial chunk of what the series actually are. A reader who walks through the corpus looking for SDEs can thread Langevin dynamics in emergence, the diffusion interpretation of gradient descent in algorithms-ml, and the anomaly-detection model in Japan earthquakes into a single reading arc. A reader who walks through looking for CAs can thread Langton's ant, Lenia, Conway's Life, lattice Boltzmann and Ising into another. The Hasse, the quadrant and the picker are three ways of saying the same thing: pick a clock, and the corpus reorganises.
