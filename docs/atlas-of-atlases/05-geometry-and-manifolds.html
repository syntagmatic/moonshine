Geometry and Manifolds

Five series quietly live on manifolds. Information geometry on the Fisher-Rao half-plane, modular forms on the hyperbolic disk, parallel coordinates on projective duality, exceptional-atlas on root-lattice spheres, type-systems on the A₂ plane. This article lays the geometric-space concepts they share as one Hasse diagram, then slices them by curvature and by manifold.

A Riemannian manifold is three things welded together: a set of points, a notion of closeness, and a way to measure length along curves. Swap the metric and the same point set becomes a different space. The flat torus and the Clifford torus are the same underlying quotient ℝ² / ℤ² carrying two different metrics; the Poincaré disk and the open unit ball carry the same topology under two radically different rulers. The Moonshine corpus keeps coming back to the same half-dozen curved spaces because statistics, number theory, and crystallography all point to them from different directions.

From topological space down to concrete moduli, ordered by "more structure added"
↑ more general, drop structure, move up ↓ more specific, add structure, move down hover a node for explainer badges across the corpus
Rank 0 is the bare topology. Each rank downward adds structure: a metric, then smoothness, then a Riemannian metric, then a connection or a transitive group action, then an explicit example. Every leaf lights up with badges from whichever series actually use it. The Poincaré half-plane node has modular-forms and information-geometry badges side by side — that is the punchline of this whole map.

Read the diagram top-to-bottom and you are climbing down a ladder of commitments. At the top, all you know is which sequences converge. By rank 2 you have a differentiable manifold, and the category changes: you can now take derivatives, speak of the tangent bundle, ask for a connection. Layer a Riemannian metric on top and distance becomes intrinsic rather than ambient. Demand that the manifold's isometry group act transitively and you land in a homogeneous space; demand also that the stabiliser fixes a symmetric decomposition and you have a symmetric space, the cleanest playground in all of Riemannian geometry. Almost every specific example the Moonshine corpus leans on is symmetric.

Where the badges pile up is where the overlap is real. The Poincaré half-plane appears as the Fisher-Rao manifold of univariate Gaussians in information-geometry and as the modular group's home in modular-forms. The two-torus ℝ²/ℤ² is the periodic-boundary box in lattice-simulation, the A₂ root lattice quotient in type-systems and exceptional-atlas, and the moduli space of flat metrics in modular-forms. Projective space is the natural home of duality in parallel-coordinates. A single node with three badges is five paragraphs of cross-series commentary compressed into a pixel.

Curvature is a single number (for these symmetric examples, anyway), and the sign is the best short identity card a manifold has. Negative-curvature spaces are where Fisher information, SL₂(ℤ) tilings, and the j-function all live. Flat spaces are where crystallography, lattice-Boltzmann, wallpaper groups, and parallel coordinates all live. Positive-curvature spaces are where Stiefel manifolds, the Bloch sphere, and Fisher-for-categoricals live. The Lorentzian regime sits off to the side — it is not Riemannian, the signature is wrong, but several series brush against it.

Curvature is the crudest invariant that still divides the map sharply. Two manifolds with the same sign of sectional curvature will feel alike; two with opposite signs will not. Hyperbolic space is the one where geodesics diverge exponentially and the boundary at infinity is a circle you can actually see (the Poincaré disk makes this visible). Flat space is where Pythagoras works unmodified. Spherical space is where geodesics reconverge and antipodes exist. The boundaries between the three are not soft; they show up in the signs of sums of angles in a triangle, in whether the exponential map is a diffeomorphism, in whether a random walk is recurrent.

The up-cone lists everything the chosen concept inherits from: a Poincaré-disk inherits from hyperbolic plane, which inherits from symmetric space, which inherits from Riemannian manifold, and so on up to topological space. The down-cone lists the concrete specialisations below it. The explainer list is the payoff — every place across the Moonshine corpus where that concept shows up doing real work, regardless of which series it belongs to.

Start with Poincaré half-plane. Its up-cone climbs through hyperbolic plane, symmetric space, Riemannian manifold, smooth manifold, topological space. Its down-cone is nearly empty, since the half-plane is already very concrete. But its explainer badge is crowded: info-geom's Fisher metric, modular-forms' SL₂(ℤ) action and fundamental domain, and the Weil-Petersson frontier article that ties them together. That is the payoff. Pick flat torus instead and the badge list splits across lattice-simulation, modular-forms' moduli-of-elliptic-curves, and exceptional-atlas' root-lattice quotients. The Hasse diagram told you these concepts are neighbours. The picker tells you who uses them.

What this view buys

The geometry theme looks, at first glance, like it belongs only to the explicitly geometric series. It does not. Every time a series uses a distribution family with a natural divergence, it is implicitly living on a Riemannian manifold. Every time it uses periodic boundaries, a lattice quotient, or a projective duality, it has picked a specific homogeneous space. Every time it talks about curvature, convergence rates, or symmetry reduction, it is pulling from the same small library the figures above lay out. Five series sit squarely inside this theme; half a dozen more touch it without announcing that they do.
