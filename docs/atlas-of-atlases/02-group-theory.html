Group Theory Across the Atlas

One Hasse diagram of group-theory concepts from abelian up through the Monster. Every node carries a badge-list of the Moonshine explainers that use it, drawn from six series. The same subgroup-poset trope you have seen five times in lattice simulation, now read across the atlas.

Groups are the atlas' most-shared machinery. The symmetric group lives in lattice simulation as a permutation of sites, in noether as the action whose invariants a Lagrangian must respect, in modular forms as the Weyl group of SL(2,Z), in mathematical diagrams as the row stabilizer of a Young tableau. The Monster lives only in modular forms, but the ladder that reaches it (abelian, Lie, exceptional, simple, sporadic) climbs through every other series first. This article is that ladder.

Rank 0 sits at the top: the bare category of groups. Rank grows downward through abelian / non-abelian, then branches: the finite-discrete spine (cyclic, symmetric, dihedral, wallpaper, point, crystallographic, Monster) runs left; the continuous spine (Lie, matrix Lie, exceptional) runs right; root-system / Weyl-group / lattice specialisations meet in the middle and flow down into E8, the Leech lattice, and the exceptional atlas. A concept is more specific than another when every instance of it is also an instance of the other.

The two spines merge precisely where the Moonshine series themselves merge. The Weyl group of a Lie group is a finite reflection group, so it lives under both "Lie group" and "finite group" in the poset. Every crystallographic lattice is a Z-module, so it lives under both "abelian group" and "lattice". E8 is a Lie group, a lattice, a root system, and a finite-index subgroup's quotient all at once, so it has four parents. The figure makes those multiple parentages explicit, and the hover shows you in real time which series treat that concept as a figure-one primitive versus as a downstream consequence.

The concept count (17) is deliberately small. A Hasse diagram this compact can be read at a glance, and every node has at least one explainer badge from at least two of the six relevant series. A bigger concept list would hide that overlap behind clutter. If you want the denser version, the spine view in Figure 3 lets you drill into any single concept and see its full explainer list across all six series at once.

The dihedral group D4 of eight elements is the smallest non-abelian group whose subgroup lattice isn't a chain. Wallpaper group p4m has a D4 point group at each lattice site. The Noether-style symmetry argument for a square plate's conserved quantities uses exactly this lattice. The root-system article in Type Systems builds B2, whose Weyl group is D4. Hover a node to see which of those stories makes that subgroup load-bearing.

Picking D4 instead of the main Hasse for Figure 2 is deliberate. The main Hasse classifies groups by kind; this lattice classifies the elements inside one group. Both are posets, but the questions they answer are orthogonal. A wallpaper-group article needs the first poset to say "p4m is a crystallographic group"; it needs the second poset to say "at each fixed point, the stabiliser is the full D4, and its mirror subgroup is where the Z2 reduction lives". The atlas uses both, constantly.

The subgroup lattice of D4 is also a useful calibration. If you can read the index-2 chain from D4 down through Z2×Z2 to the trivial group, you can read the analogous chain from the Monster down through its maximal subgroups, because Hasse diagrams scale the same way. The only thing that grows is the height. The subgroup lattice of the Monster has height on the order of 102 and width on the order of 108, which is why no one draws it.

Figure 3 · Concept spine picker

Pick a concept; see its two cones and the full badge-list across the six target series
The up-cone is the set of more-general concepts that contain the chosen one. The down-cone is the set of more-specific ones it contains. Below the cones, the series-badge row collects every explainer (across all six series) that uses the chosen concept. A concept can have a big cone and no badges, or a tiny cone and many badges; the ratio tells you whether the Moonshine corpus has explored that piece of machinery at the level of generality it deserves.

The spine view reads the same DAG as Figure 1, but swaps the visual question. Figure 1 asks "where does this concept sit in the global poset"; Figure 3 asks "for this one concept, which explainers instantiate it, and at what level". The Monster has a big up-cone and exactly two badges, both in Modular Forms. The abelian group has a tiny up-cone, the whole down-cone, and badges everywhere. The symmetric group sits in the middle and has the densest badge-row of any concept in the figure, which is roughly right.

If you want a single takeaway from this article: group theory in the Moonshine atlas is not a tower of increasing abstraction, it is a poset. The abelian group is at the top and the Monster is at the bottom, but most explainers live somewhere in the middle, where a Weyl group is at the same time a finite reflection group, a point group, and a permutation group, and the context decides which hat it wears. The figures here let you switch hats by hovering.
