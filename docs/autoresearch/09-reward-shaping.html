Reward Shaping

Designing objective functions for the autoresearch iterate loop

The composite score is a scalar pretending to be a vector.

Four dimensions in (visual_critic 0.30, encoding_integrity 0.25, cognitive_load 0.25, stress_test 0.20), one number out. The system checks delta vs. -0.3 and decides keep or discard. Simple, legible, lossy.

Visual_critic 6 → 8 while stress_test 8 → 5 produces a composite delta near zero. The system keeps it. A human reviewer would have questions.

The problem with a single number

Fixed weights encode a permanent opinion about which dimensions matter most. But which dimension matters depends on where the block stands. A block at 4 visual / 9 stress should weight visual much more heavily than 0.30 vs 0.20.

Worse, the composite invites dimension collapse: the optimiser learns to pump the high-weighted dimension at others' expense. Visual +1 / encoding -0.5 still shows a positive composite delta. Over many rounds, the block looks good on one axis and progressively worse on the rest. 

current weights:
  0.30 x visual_critic       # highest weight, most pumpable
  0.25 x encoding_integrity
  0.25 x cognitive_load
  0.20 x stress_test         # lowest weight, most neglectable

Three alternatives

Three alternatives, each solving a different failure mode.

1. Pareto dominance

Treat the score as a vector. An experiment is Pareto-dominant if it improves at least one dimension without worsening any. Improvements with tradeoffs sit on a different point of the frontier rather than dominating.

Under Pareto selection, the system keeps an experiment only if every dimension is at least as good and at least one is strictly better. The human sees the frontier and picks which tradeoffs to accept.

2. Adaptive weighting

Keep the composite, but let the weights shift. The intuition: weight each dimension inversely to its current score. A dimension scoring 4/10 gets more weight than one scoring 9/10. The system automatically focuses on the weakest link. 

adaptive_weight(dim) = base_weight(dim) * (10 - score(dim)) / normalizer

At visual 4 / stress 9, the adaptive weights are 0.42 for visual and 0.08 for stress. As visual climbs, its weight drops and the next-weakest dimension takes priority — a soft curriculum.

Formula: weight_i = (10 - score_i) / sum(10 - score_j). Scores [4, 9, 7, 6] have gaps [6, 1, 3, 4], normaliser 14, weights [0.43, 0.07, 0.21, 0.29]. The system can't over-optimise a strong dimension because the formula won't let it.

Edge case: all dimensions at 10 makes the normaliser zero. Fallback to uniform weights when the sum of gaps falls below epsilon.

3. Threshold constraints

Hard floors: stress_test ≥ 6, encoding_integrity ≥ 5. Any drop below the floor discards the experiment, regardless of gains elsewhere. Above the floors, optimise freely.

This separates acceptable from optimal. Floors prevent catastrophic regression; aggressive optimisation works above them.

Curriculum: weakest-first scheduling

Curriculum is adaptive weighting taken further: focus on one dimension at a time. Identify the weakest, optimise for it specifically, advance when it crosses a threshold.

Each iteration has a single objective. The decision function is simple: did the target dimension improve without the others collapsing?

Downside: scheduling. Threshold values, advancement rules, oscillation when A and B are anti-correlated — all hyperparameters that need tuning.

LOC as a proxy metric

LOC and quality overlap without coinciding. A 200-line visualisation can be worse than a 300-line one if those extra hundred lines are interaction code, accessible labels, or responsive layout. The LOC gate can't tell removed dead code from removed necessary complexity.

Every metric is a proxy. Stress_test 8 means the evaluator found no failures under the conditions it checked, not that the block is robust. The composite is a proxy of proxies.

Asymmetric regression penalties

Uniform -0.3 treats all regressions equally. But a 1-point drop in visual_critic is immediately visible; in stress_test, only under edge cases; in encoding_integrity, often a missing label that 80% of viewers won't notice.

An asymmetric penalty schedule would weight regressions differently: 

regression_penalty:
  visual_critic:       -0.15  # tight: regression is obvious
  encoding_integrity:  -0.25  # moderate
  cognitive_load:      -0.25  # moderate
  stress_test:         -0.40  # loose: less visible, noisier signal

More tolerance for noisy dimensions; tighter gates where small drops are perceptible.

Try the simulator. Composite, Pareto, and adaptive each accept different sets of experiments.

Tradeoffs in every direction. Pareto optimization is slower to converge. It keeps more experiments because partial improvements are not rejected, but it also means less aggressive pruning. Adaptive weights can oscillate: improving the weakest dimension shifts the weights, making a different dimension weakest, pulling the optimizer back. Curriculum scheduling adds hyperparameters (thresholds, ordering rules) that themselves need tuning. There is no free lunch here. Each strategy trades one failure mode for another.

What changes in practice

The composite works — it has shipped 107 blocks. But it's a blunt instrument. The alternatives here cut more precisely at the cost of more setup.

At thousands of iterations per block the composite would be fine; noise averages out. The loop runs 5–15 iterations per block, where one bad keep (dimension collapse) or bad discard (Pareto improvement rejected on a dip) shapes the whole trajectory.

Layered: threshold constraints as hard gates, adaptive weighting as the optimisation signal, Pareto tracking as diagnostic. Three strategies covering the composite's blind spots, not one replacement.

Validating a new reward function

Designing a better reward is one problem; proving it's better is harder. A/B comparison runs into circularity: the composite is the metric you're trying to improve on.

Protocol: 10 blocks of comparable starting quality, both reward functions with proposer prompt, temperature, and iteration budget held constant. Compare on composite (old metric), dimension-floor compliance (structural), and human preference (ground truth).

Human judgment is cleanest. Show final artifacts to a reviewer blind to which reward produced them. 7-of-10 wins is evidence; 5-of-10 is noise. Expensive but sidesteps circularity.

Dimension-floor compliance is the cheap proxy. Pareto should produce fewer 8/4 imbalances than the composite. 9/10 blocks with all dimensions above 6 vs. 6/10 is a structural improvement even at similar composite scores.

The cost of knowing

Each experiment is ~$0.10. 10 blocks × 10 experiments × 2 arms = 200 experiments, $20 per A/B comparison. Cheap by ML research standards, expensive for "test a one-line change to keep/discard logic."

The iterate loop has high variance — a single lucky proposer output can dominate a trajectory. Five blocks per arm makes one outlier a 20% shift. Ten is the minimum for a claim worth acting on; twenty would be better at $40.

Compromise: 3-block pilot, full 10-block comparison if ambiguous. Not rigorous sequential testing, but catches obvious failures before committing the full budget.

What the A/B test actually measures

The A/B comparison reveals something beyond which reward function is "better." It reveals the shape of the optimization trajectory. Two reward functions that reach the same final quality via different paths produce different intermediate artifacts, different failure modes, and different demands on the iteration budget. A composite-driven trajectory might hit a high score quickly but bounce around as dimension collapse pulls it back. A Pareto-driven trajectory might climb slowly but monotonically, with fewer discards and less wasted budget.

This is why the trajectory plot matters. Comparing only final scores loses the process information. A reward function that reaches 7.5 in 8 experiments is more valuable than one that reaches 7.6 in 14, because the 6 extra experiments cost time and money. The right metric is not peak quality but quality per experiment.

Both panels start from the same block. The left trajectory uses composite threshold scoring: a single weighted sum with a -0.3 tolerance gate. The right trajectory uses Pareto dominance: no dimension may regress, at least one must improve. Composite scoring is aggressive. It discards more experiments, converges faster, but leaves dimension imbalances unchecked. Pareto scoring is conservative. It accepts more experiments (because partial improvements count), but each accepted experiment makes smaller jumps. The cumulative cost tells you what that caution costs in dollars.

Regenerate the comparison a few times. The pattern is consistent: the composite arm takes bigger jumps but occasionally regresses on one dimension while climbing on another. The Pareto arm moves in smaller increments but almost never loses ground. Over 10 experiments, the composite arm spends less total (fewer experiments pass the gate, so the proposer gets called less often in a real budget-limited loop) while the Pareto arm spends more but produces more balanced final scores.

Amber risks

Every alternative reward function trades one failure mode for another. The composite's failure mode is well-understood: dimension collapse, silent tradeoffs, insensitivity to the block's current weaknesses. The alternatives introduce failure modes that are less familiar and harder to detect.

Pareto dominance is slow

Pareto selection keeps any experiment that improves at least one dimension without worsening any other. In principle this sounds conservative. In practice it means more experiments pass the gate. More accepted experiments means less aggressive pruning. The optimizer explores a wider region of the quality space but takes longer to converge. On a 10-experiment budget, the Pareto arm may still be climbing when the composite arm has already plateaued. If the iteration budget is tight (and at $0.10 per experiment, it usually is), the slower convergence translates directly into lower final quality or higher cost.

  • Accept rates under Pareto are typically 50-70%, vs. 30-45% under composite threshold.
  • Convergence takes 2-4 more experiments to reach the same composite score.
  • The Pareto arm produces more balanced scores but may not reach the same peak on any single dimension.

Adaptive weights can oscillate

The adaptive weighting formula recalculates weights after every accepted experiment. This creates a feedback loop. Suppose visual_critic is at 4 and stress_test is at 8. The formula concentrates weight on visual. The next experiment improves visual to 6. Now the gap between visual and stress has narrowed. The formula shifts weight toward whatever dimension is now weakest. If encoding_integrity was quietly sitting at 5.5, it becomes the new target. The next experiment pushes encoding up. But that experiment might slightly degrade visual (because the proposer was not optimizing for visual anymore). Visual drops to 5.5, and the formula swings weight back.

This oscillation is not catastrophic. The block still improves on average because each step is a net positive. But the trajectory zig-zags between dimensions rather than climbing steadily on all of them. In a 10-experiment budget, 2-3 experiments may be spent correcting regressions caused by the previous experiment's weight shift. That is 20-30% of the budget lost to oscillation overhead.

A damping factor helps: instead of recalculating weights from scratch each step, blend the new weights with the previous ones. weight_new = 0.7 * weight_calculated + 0.3 * weight_previous. This smooths the transitions but introduces another hyperparameter (the blend rate) that itself needs tuning.

Curriculum scheduling adds hyperparameters

Curriculum learning sounds elegant: focus on the weakest dimension, improve it past a threshold, move to the next. But "threshold" and "which dimension is next" and "what happens when two dimensions are tied" are all decisions that need values. Each value is a hyperparameter. The original composite has four weights and one tolerance. A full curriculum schedule has four thresholds, a tie-breaking rule, a fallback strategy for when no dimension is below threshold, and a transition policy (hard switch vs. gradual blend). That is at least eight hyperparameters, each of which interacts with the others.

Worse, the optimal curriculum depends on the starting block. A block that starts at [4, 4, 4, 4] benefits from focusing on any dimension. A block at [8, 4, 7, 6] needs the curriculum to target the clear weakness. A block at [7, 6, 6, 7] has no clear weakness and the curriculum has to pick one arbitrarily. A single fixed curriculum cannot serve all three cases well. The reward function now needs a meta-policy for selecting curricula, which is the reward shaping problem restated at a higher level.

Validation costs compound

Each of these alternatives needs its own A/B validation. Pareto vs. composite: $20. Adaptive vs. composite: $20. Curriculum vs. adaptive: $20. Testing a damping factor at three settings: $60. The menu of possible reward functions grows faster than the budget for testing them. Without discipline, reward shaping becomes its own infinite iterate loop: changing the objective function, testing it, finding an edge case, changing it again. The composite's greatest virtue may be that it is boring enough to leave alone.
