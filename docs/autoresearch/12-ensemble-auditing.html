Ensemble Auditing

Using multiple auditors to produce scores you can trust

The auditor scores 7.2. Yesterday it scored 6.8 on the same block. Same prompt, same model, different seed — the auditor disagreeing with itself.

Ten runs give you a distribution with standard deviation ~0.25 on a 10-point scale, so any single evaluation can land in a range of ~1.0 around the true quality. The noise usually washes out; near a decision boundary, it becomes the decision. 

block "radial-tree-nav"
  evaluation 1:  composite 7.2   (keep, above -0.3 threshold)
  evaluation 2:  composite 6.8   (discard, below -0.3 threshold)

  same block, different luck

The regression threshold is -0.3. With measurement noise of ±0.5, a block that genuinely improved by 0.1 can be discarded if the second score lands low and the first landed high.

The problem isn't that scores are wrong. A single sample from a noisy distribution gets treated as a precise measurement. A composite of 7.2 means the block is probably between 6.7 and 7.7.

The fix: ask more than once

Run the evaluation three times and take the median. {6.8, 7.2, 7.0} → 7.0. The variance of the median shrinks roughly with ensemble size, taking effective noise from 0.5 to ~0.3. Enough to stop most spurious flips.

Same-model multiple-runs costs 3× with no new infrastructure. It catches random variance but not systematic bias. If Claude underscores radial layouts because its training data had few of them, three Claude evaluations converge on the same wrong answer.

Four strategies

Strategy What varies Catches Cost
Same model, N runs Random seed Random variance Nx base
Different models Model weights Model-specific bias Nx base (varies by model)
Different prompts Audit focus Dimension blindness Nx base
Calibrated ensemble All of the above + weights Everything above Nx base + calibration data

Different models sends the same block to Claude, Gemini, and GPT as independent auditors. Agreement is high confidence; disagreement above a point is genuine ambiguity.

Different prompts keep the same model but change the audit's focus — one auditor on color and typography, one on layout and whitespace, one on interaction responsiveness. Forcing a model to spend tokens on color catches problems the generalist glosses over.

Calibrated ensemble tracks each auditor's historical accuracy against ground truth and weights accordingly. Requires labeled examples to build the calibration table.

The simulator

Single auditor jumps around; ensemble median is stable.

Decision divergence

Noise in scores becomes noise in keep/discard decisions. 20 simulated experiments, each with a true quality delta. Single auditor adds noise; ensemble of three adds less. Decisions flip when the noisy score crosses -0.3 in a different direction than the true delta.

Disagreement as signal

{7.5, 7.3, 7.1} is clearly a 7.3. {8.2, 6.1, 7.4} has a 2.1-point spread — not noise, ambiguity.

High disagreement means one of: the block does something unusual that one model appreciates and another penalises; a real flaw only one auditor notices; or the auditors are weighting dimensions differently.

Auto-deciding is risky in any of these cases. A heuristic: spread above 1.0 → flag for human review. The human reads the notes, looks at the screenshot, and judges with the ambiguity in view.

Disagreement is the ensemble's most valuable output. Agreement gives you a confident number; disagreement gives you a question.

The cost-accuracy tradeoff

Ensemble cost scales linearly. $0.03 × 3 = $0.09 per experiment. $9 over 100 experiments instead of $3.

A false discard throws away an improvement; a false keep accepts a regression. Both cost downstream iterations. If wrong decisions cost ~2 extra iterations at $0.15 each, preventing one saves $0.30. An ensemble that prevents one wrong decision per 10 experiments pays for itself.

About 15% of evaluations sit within 0.3 of the threshold. Those are the experiments where ensemble auditing changes the outcome.

Calibrating individual auditors

A raw 7.0 from Claude, Gemini, and GPT mean different things. Each model maps its assessment onto the 1–10 scale differently — some compress into 6–8, some use the full range, some grade lenient, some stingy. Averaged raw, the lenient model dominates.

Fix: a calibration curve. Score 20 ground-truth blocks (humans + each model), plot model score against human score. Perfect calibration is the diagonal; deviations above are over-scoring, below under-scoring, with slope ≠ 1 a stretched/compressed scale.

Per-model linear regression gives slope and intercept. Adjusted score = slope × raw + intercept. After adjustment, all models speak the same language.

Different models excel at different dimensions. Claude tends to catch visual layout and typography. Gemini handles encoding integrity and SVG structure. GPT is often most rigorous on stress testing. Tendencies, not rules, and they shift across model versions.

Per-dimension weights exploit this: Claude 50% on visual_critic but 20% on stress_test, GPT the reverse. The calibration data dictates the weights — whichever model's adjusted scores correlate tightest with human scores gets the largest weight for that dimension. 

calibration results (20 ground-truth blocks):

model         dimension           slope   intercept   R²
Claude        visual_critic       0.91    +0.42       0.87
Claude        stress_test         0.74    +1.85       0.61
Gemini        visual_critic       0.82    +1.35       0.72
Gemini        encoding_integrity  0.95    +0.18       0.91
GPT           stress_test         0.88    +0.73       0.84
GPT           visual_critic       0.78    +1.60       0.68

R² drives weighting. Claude's 0.87 on visual_critic explains 87% of the variance in human scores; GPT's 0.68 explains less.

A steeper-than-diagonal trend line means the model compresses, assigning narrow scores even when humans see wide spread. Shallow exaggerates differences. Parallel-but-shifted is a constant offset. All correctable with the linear adjustment, given enough ground-truth.

Calibration turns raw scores into comparable measurements. Without it, ensembling is arithmetic on scales that don't share meaning.

Risks of calibration and ensembling

Ground-truth data is expensive and subjective. Two designers rating the same block on visual_critic can differ by a full point. You're calibrating against a noisy reference. Twenty blocks is the minimum; 50+ for stable coefficients. At 15 minutes per block per evaluator, a single calibration round is 12+ hours of human labor.

Calibration drifts when models update. A calibration built on Claude Opus 4 doesn't hold for the next release. Spot-check ground-truth blocks after every model update; re-run full calibration if the residuals jump.

Committee conservatism penalises novelty. A novel radial force-directed layout that works might confuse two of three auditors. The median drags toward the confused majority. Mitigations: treat high disagreement as a flag for human review rather than a penalty, or include a "novelty bonus" dimension that at least one auditor rewards.

Cost compounds. 3 models is 3× base. Calibration adds a layer (ground truth, regression, drift recalibration). Operations add another (drift monitoring, confident-wrong-answer debugging, disagreement triage). Total is closer to 5–8× a single auditor. Start with same-model median and upgrade when systematic bias is costing decisions.

When to use it

Ensemble auditing is not always worth the overhead. Use it when: the regression threshold is tight (below 0.5), you are iterating on blocks near the quality boundary, or you are running a long optimization where cumulative wrong decisions compound. Skip it when: blocks are clearly high- or low-quality, the cost per evaluation is high relative to your budget, or you are in an early exploration phase where precision matters less than coverage.

The simplest starting point is three runs of the same model with median aggregation. It costs 3x, requires zero new infrastructure, and handles the most common failure mode. Upgrade to multi-model or multi-prompt ensembles only when you have evidence of systematic bias in the single-model approach.
