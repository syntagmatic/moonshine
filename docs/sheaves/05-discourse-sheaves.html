Opinion dynamics on discourse sheaves

What people think and what they say live in different spaces. A sheaf keeps both, and heat flow can move either one: opinions toward public agreement, a whole club toward its two stubborn leaders, or the words themselves, until someone learns to say what their neighbour wants to hear.

The classical models of opinion dynamics give every person a number, or a vector of numbers on a fixed list of topics, and let each person drift toward the average of their neighbours. The graph Laplacian does the averaging and the group converges to a consensus. Jakob Hansen and Robert Ghrist noticed that the sheaf Laplacian from the previous essay runs the same dynamics on a much richer model, and that the richer model separates two things the classical one conflates: what a person believes, and what they say.

Their discourse sheaf puts a person's private opinions in the vertex stalk, a vector space whose basis is the topics that person has views on. Two people who talk share a discourse space on their edge, whose basis is whatever they discuss, and it need not be any of the topics either of them holds views on. The restriction map is how person turns private opinions into a public stance in that conversation. It can exaggerate, understate, mix several principles into one policy, stay silent on a topic, or flip a sign and lie, and it can do each of these differently with each neighbour. A 0-cochain is a distribution of private opinions, and the coboundary

is the difference between what and say. The Laplacian measures discord, and a global section is a distribution of opinions in which every pair of neighbours says the same thing. That does not mean neighbours hold the same opinions. In Hansen and Ghrist's words, it means “their expressions of personally held opinions have the appearance of agreement.” The constant sheaf, where everyone has views on the same topics and says exactly what they think, is the classical model.

This page builds three of the paper's dynamics. Opinions can flow while everyone keeps their way of speaking. Some people can refuse to move while the rest flow, which is persuasion. Or opinions can stay put while the restriction maps flow, which is how the model learns to lie.

With one policy each, every person slides along a line parallel to their own arrow, since that is the only direction they ever express. The stances converge to a common value on every edge, and the private opinions end up spread along the perpendicular directions, which no one hears. The space of global sections is six-dimensional. Each person contributes the one direction they never express, five in all, and one more is the value everyone ends up saying.

Choose everyone discusses topic 1 and every map is the same arrow, so on topic 1 the sheaf is the constant sheaf and the group converges to the mean, the classical result. Topic 2 does not move at all. Polite company is the paper's Example 4.2 spread over five people. C and D reverse their arrows toward A, B and E, while speaking plainly to each other. Every cycle crosses from one camp to the other an even number of times, so the sheaf is balanced in the sense of the signed graphs of the last essay. From any start the flow ends in public harmony, with C and D privately holding the opposite of what they tell A, B and E. That polarized state is a global section, so it is stable. One lie on a loop reverses a single arrow, E's toward A. Now the stances around the cycle A–C–D–E pick up a sign, the only stance every edge can agree on is zero, and the flow ends with everyone saying nothing while holding whatever private opinion the perpendicular direction allows.

Persuasion by the stubborn

Suppose some people will not move. Hansen and Ghrist hold the opinions of a set of stubborn agents fixed and let everyone else flow (their eq. 5.1). The system converges exponentially to the harmonic extension of the stubborn opinions, the distribution that minimizes total discord with held fixed on , and if there are several such, to the one nearest the start (Theorem 5.1). The extension is unique exactly when no nonzero global section vanishes on , which is the relative cohomology . The same condition makes the network stabilizable by anyone who controls (Theorem 6.1). For the constant sheaf on a connected graph a single stubborn person is enough (their Example 6.3), and a person who is only partly reluctant is covered by the same theorem, by giving them a stubborn copy of themselves to talk to (their Section 7).

A real network where two people refused to move is Wayne Zachary's university karate club, 34 members that split in two after a dispute between the instructor, Mr Hi, and the club's administration. Zachary recorded which pairs of members interacted, 78 pairs in all, and which of the two new clubs each member joined. The flow settles on a harmonic extension, which on the constant sheaf means every free member ends at the average of their friends. Thirty-three of the 34 members land on the side of the club they actually joined. The exception is member 9, who had friends in both camps and ends slightly on the officers' side, at −0.19, but joined Mr Hi's club. Member 3 is nearly undecided at +0.02. Nobody ends more extreme than the two leaders, since an average can never exceed the largest thing being averaged. The discord never reaches zero. With two stubborn people disagreeing and a connected network between them, some friendships always carry a difference of opinion, and the harmonic extension is the arrangement with the least total.

Released from the leaders' harmonic extension, that mean is −0.02: the extension is almost perfectly balanced between the two camps. Then turn on polite lies across the split. This changes the sheaf rather than the opinions. On each of the 11 friendships that cross between the two eventual clubs, the officers' side now reverses its restriction map, so the edge asks for opposite opinions. Every cycle crosses the split an even number of times, so this sheaf is balanced, and the two stubborn leaders are now in agreement as far as the sheaf is concerned. The flow ends with every member of Mr Hi's club at , every officer at , and zero discord on every edge: complete polarization held together by politeness, the paper's Example 4.2 at the scale of a real club.

Learning to lie

Hansen and Ghrist remark that the heat equation is perhaps not how real people engage in discourse. A person facing disagreement may keep their opinion and change how they express it. Their Section 8 holds the opinions fixed and lets each person adjust their own restriction maps to remove the part of their expression that disagrees with a neighbour:

This is a diffusion on the maps, and each edge evolves on its own. Their Theorem 8.1 shows that the sheaf converges to the nearest sheaf, in the Frobenius norm on restriction maps, for which the fixed opinions are a global section. The projection has a closed form. The person with the stronger opinion changes their expression the most.

The conserved quantities say who can lie. They do not say who will. The joint flow is not linear, and it can approach its limit slowly. What the model does not say

None of this is an empirical claim about people, and the paper does not make one. It is a language in which such claims can be stated precisely: who is allowed to translate, what counts as agreement, which directions of opinion are ever heard. Then linear algebra answers questions about the stated model. Stubbornness and control reduce to computations with , its kernel, and the kernel's restriction to a subset of people. The same paper goes on to nonlinear Laplacians that reproduce bounded-confidence models, where people ignore neighbours whose stated opinions are too far from their own, and to antagonistic edges, where two people push their stated opinions apart. With a signed Laplacian that is no longer positive semidefinite, those dynamics can be unstable, and for the constant sheaf they are whenever the hostile edges form a cutset (their Proposition 12.1). Figures 1 and 2 changed opinions and Figure 3 changed the maps to reduce discord. The next essay learns restriction maps again, for a different purpose: a graph neural network that fits its maps to a classification task, so that diffusion can keep neighbouring nodes apart instead of pulling them together.
