Germs and analytic continuation

The sheaf the finite version was copying. A germ of a holomorphic function is a convergent power series, the germs over the plane form a space of their own, and carrying one germ round a branch point traces out a Riemann surface inside that space.

Every sheaf in this series has lived on a graph, where a sheaf is a list of matrices and every question about it is a rank computation. The word and the axioms come from somewhere else. The model example is the holomorphic functions on open subsets of the complex plane, and the most useful picture of it, a space whose points are germs of functions, is older than sheaves: it is Weierstrass's construction of a many-valued function by analytic continuation, recast as topology. This essay sets the two side by side, shows where the cellular definition of the first essay comes from, and shows what it gave up.

Both conditions hold because being holomorphic is a local property: a function defined piece by piece, with the pieces agreeing where they meet, is complex differentiable at each point because it is near each point. Change the property and the axiom can fail. Bounded holomorphic functions form a presheaf that satisfies identity and fails gluing, since is bounded on each disk and the disks cover the plane. Constant functions fail gluing on a disconnected open set; the sheaf they generate is the locally constant functions, one constant per component, which is the constant sheaf of the first essay, where sections of the constant sheaf on a graph were one number per connected component.

Germs

For holomorphic functions a germ is the same thing as a convergent power series: a function holomorphic near equals its Taylor series on the largest disk about inside its domain, and a convergent series defines a function on its disk. So , and a germ carries much more than a value. By the identity theorem, the germ at one point determines the function on any connected open set where it extends. That rigidity is special. For smooth functions a germ is not its Taylor series, and two different germs can come from functions that agree on an open set touching the point.

The gluing axiom, on a cover

Now go back to the gluing axiom with a cover of the punctured plane near the unit circle by a ring of disks, each missing 0, consecutive ones overlapping, the others disjoint. Instead of itself, take the solutions of . On a disk, or on any connected open set that does not go round 0, they are the multiples of one branch of , a line. On an overlap of two disks, the branches chosen on the two sides are either equal or negatives of each other. So the solutions form a sheaf of lines, and the gluing axiom for this cover is a finite linear problem: one number per disk, one equation per overlap. Its matrix is the coboundary of a cellular sheaf on the nerve of the cover, a cycle.

What the cellular definition kept

The first essay defined a sheaf on a graph by stalks on vertices and edges and maps from vertex to edge, with global sections the kernel of . That essay also noted that with the Alexandrov topology, whose open sets are the up-closed sets of cells, a cellular sheaf is a sheaf in the sense above. Here is the match in detail. One cover is enough because every point of the Alexandrov topology has a smallest open neighbourhood. The colimit that defines a stalk then stops at that neighbourhood, so the stalk at is just , and a sheaf is determined by its values on the stars and the maps between them. It is also why the arrows point from vertex to edge: the star of an edge sits inside the star of each of its vertices, so the map is restriction to a smaller open set.

In the plane no point has a smallest neighbourhood, which is why a stalk has to be a colimit, why its elements are germs, and why a germ remembers a whole power series where a cellular stalk remembers a vector. The rest carries over. Continuing a germ through a chain of disks is propagating a section through the nerve, vertex to vertex, as in the cut-open hexagon of the first essay; the monodromy of the continuation is the holonomy of the cellular sheaf; and the étalé space, the answer to "what are all the germs and which are near which", is what the Riemann surface was all along.

Where the idea came from

Jean Leray was a prisoner of war from 1940 to 1945 in Oflag XVII-A, an officers' camp in Austria, where he ran the camp university and turned from fluid mechanics to algebraic topology, so that his expertise would be of no use to the German war effort. The course he taught there, published in 1945, already contains the constant-coefficient ancestor of the theorem used above: a compact Hausdorff space covered by finitely many closed sets that, with all their finite intersections, have the cohomology of a point (Leray's word is simple) has the cohomology of the cover's nerve (Théorème 12 and Remarque 3). The word faisceau first appears in a note to the Comptes rendus in 1946, and there a sheaf assigns a module to every closed set, with maps to smaller closed sets. The same note announces, in one sentence and without proof, that a space with a cover whose supports are simple relative to the sheaf has the same homology as the cover. The version with a proof is in Leray's 1950 memoir (Théorème 49.2), still for closed, locally finite covers.

The étalé space arrived with Henri Cartan's seminar, which redid the theory with open sets. In the exposé of 9 April 1951 Cartan defines a sheaf as a space with a local homeomorphism onto the base, each fibre a module, and credits this "topological" form of the definition to Michel Lazard. Two pages later he takes the holomorphic functions on a complex manifold, identifies the stalk with the convergent power series, and remarks that a connected component of the sheaf "is nothing other than an analytic function on its whole domain of existence (an unramified Riemann surface)", and that the topology is Hausdorff by the principle of analytic continuation. That is this essay's Figure 2, in two sentences. The exposé names neither Weierstrass nor Weyl, whose 1913 book built the Riemann surface from function elements; that lineage is the standard account, not something these sources say.

This series took one path through the subject and left others alone. Cosheaves reverse every arrow, putting extension maps from small sets to large ones, and carry homology where sheaves carry cohomology; Curry's thesis develops the cellular versions of both side by side. Persistent homology can be phrased as sheaves or cosheaves on the real line, or on posets of parameters, and Kashiwara and Schapira connect it to microlocal sheaf theory. In quantum foundations, Abramsky and Brandenburger read contextuality as a family of local probability tables, one for each set of jointly measurable observables, that agree on overlaps and admit no global section. Each of these begins where the essays began, with local data and rules for how neighbours must agree.
