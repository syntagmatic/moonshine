Stalks and restriction maps

A sheaf on a graph is a vector space on every vertex and edge and a matrix on every place where they meet. Its global sections are the kernel of one bigger matrix, and a loop can hide a twist that no local check will find.

Suppose each vertex of a graph holds some data, and each edge holds a rule for how the data at its two ends must relate. The data at a vertex might be a number, a direction, or a full state vector; the rule on an edge might ask two numbers to be equal, two directions to differ by a fixed rotation, or two state vectors to agree in one coordinate and ignore the rest. A sheaf is the bookkeeping for this situation, and it is simple enough to write down in one line of linear algebra.

A cellular sheaf on a graph assigns a vector space to every vertex and to every edge, the stalks, and a linear map

for every vertex of every edge , the restriction maps. The edge stalk is where the two endpoints are compared. A global section is a choice of at every vertex such that both ends of every edge restrict to the same element of the edge stalk:

Take every stalk to be and every map to be the identity, and a section is a function that is constant along every edge: one number per connected component. This is the constant sheaf, and every other sheaf on this page is a variation on it.

Each edge on its own is satisfiable, and so is every set of edges that stops short of a full loop. What cannot be satisfied is the loop.

There the maps are rotations, which preserve length, and the spectrum depends on the holonomy alone.

The same thing as a functor

For the reader who prefers categories: the vertices and edges of a graph form a poset, with when is an end of . A cellular sheaf is a functor from that poset to vector spaces. On a graph there are no chains of length two, so functoriality says nothing; on a complex with 2-cells it forces . Give the poset the Alexandrov topology, whose open sets are the up-closed sets. The smallest open set containing a vertex is its open star, the vertex together with its edges, and the open star of an edge is the edge alone. Since the star of an edge sits inside the star of each of its vertices, the map from vertex to edge is restriction to a smaller open set, which is where the name comes from. With that topology a cellular sheaf is an honest sheaf and is its value on the open star, a fact that Curry's thesis sets out in detail, crediting the combinatorial definition to Shepard.
