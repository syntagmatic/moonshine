Sensor fusion and the consistency radius

Overlapping sensors never quite agree. A sheaf says what agreement would mean, the consistency radius says how far the readings are from it, and the edges that carry the disagreement point at the sensor that caused it.

A sensor reports on a piece of the world, and sensors overlap. A radar and a camera watch the same harbour mouth; two thermometers a few metres apart sit in the same air. Where they overlap they ought to agree, and they never do exactly. Michael Robinson's argument is that a sheaf is the right bookkeeping for this. The stalk over a sensor is the space its readings live in, the restriction maps say what each reading implies about each overlap, and the global sections are the states of the world that every sensor could have seen at once. Real readings are almost never a section. The questions are how far they are from one, which section to report, and, when one sensor is broken, which one.

The first essay's coboundary already measures failure to be a section: is the gap on the edge . Robinson's measure is built from the same gaps, with two differences that matter in practice. It is a worst case rather than a sum, in the units the sensors report, and it allows data on the overlaps as well as on the sensors.

Assignments and the consistency radius

A global section puts a value on every vertex and every edge, each edge's value being the restriction of each endpoint's. An assignment drops the constraint: an arbitrary in each vertex stalk and an arbitrary in each edge stalk. Edge values are not a technicality, since two sensors can report jointly on their overlap and a fusion centre can write down what it believes about it. Each incidence then has a critical threshold, the distance between the edge's value and what the vertex says the edge should hold, and the consistency radius is the largest of them:

This is Robinson's definition read on a graph. He states it for a sheaf of pseudometric spaces on any topological space, first as the smallest for which every restriction between nested open sets holds to within (2017, Def. 20), later as the supremum over all such pairs (2020, Def. 7). On a graph with the Alexandrov topology, the open star of a vertex carries that vertex's stalk, an edge is open on its own, and the nested pairs of stars are exactly the incidences. An assignment that also chose values on unions of stars would have more pairs to compare, and Robinson notes that its radius can then come out larger (2020, Sec. 9). Everything on this page lives on cells. With metric stalks, radius zero means every restriction holds exactly, so the assignment is a global section.

Often only the sensors report and the edges are blank. The natural completion (2020, Def. 16) picks the edge values that make the radius as small as possible. Knock the camera and its three edges light up together, while the gaps between the radar and the two lines stay small. Knock P instead and exactly two edges light, R–P and C–P, and P is the only vertex they share. That is fault localization, and it works here because every sensor overlaps with at least two others. A sensor with a single overlap could not be told apart from its partner, since one lit edge implicates both of its ends equally. Meanwhile least squares does what it always does with an outlier. The fused point moves toward the knocked sensor, by a share set by how many readings outvote it, and in a real deployment there is no cross to compare against. The radius is what warns you, before any ground truth arrives, that the fused answer is being pulled.

A lab full of thermometers

Between 28 February and 5 April 2004, 54 wireless sensors (motes) in the Intel Berkeley Research Lab reported temperature, humidity, light and battery voltage about every 31 seconds. The data is public, and some of the motes fail during the deployment. Figure 2 uses the three days from Sunday 7 March to Tuesday 9 March 2004, reduced to each mote's median temperature in each half hour. Three motes sent nothing in that window. Of the remaining 51 motes' 7344 half-hourly readings, 126 are missing.

The sheaf is the simplest model of a room there is: the constant sheaf on the graph joining each mote to its Delaunay neighbours no more than 8 m away, 106 edges in all. It says that neighbouring motes read the same air. That is wrong about the lab in a known way. A room has gradients from windows, machines and people, so even perfect sensors give a nonzero radius, and the radius measures model error and sensor error together. The missing readings make this a partial assignment. The page fills each gap by harmonic extension, the least-squares completion from the motes that did report, so the radius shown is an upper bound for the radius Robinson assigns to the partial assignment, which uses the best completion (2020, Def. 16).

In the small hours of Sunday the lab is nearly uniform and the radius sits between about 0.6 and 0.8 °C: no two neighbours differ by more than about a degree and a half. On Monday morning it rises to about 4 °C, and twenty or more edges pass 1 °C, spread across the room rather than gathered around one mote. That is the model's error, the gradient the constant sheaf pretends is not there, and it sets how large a fault has to be before it can be seen.

Mote 18 is the exception. At 03:00 on Monday it already owns the worst edge, to its neighbour 14, at a time when the lab is quiet and the rest of the graph is below 0.7 °C. By Monday afternoon it reads several degrees above its neighbours and climbing, and at midnight it reports 64 °C. From 01:30 to 05:00 on Tuesday it sends nothing at all, along with seven or eight other motes. The page fills its value from its neighbours, so for those four hours the red line drops back to the room's level: that drop is the fill, not a repair, and the hatched band on the strip marks it. From 05:30 it reports about 122 °C in every half hour to the end of the window. Through all of this the slate line, the radius with mote 18 withheld and the gaps filled without it, stays with the room's own gradient. The mote is localized by the same argument as the camera in Figure 1: every lit edge through that part of the graph ends at 18, and each of its neighbours has other edges that stay dark. The slate line is a check rather than a search. The lit edges name the suspect, and withholding it confirms that the rest of the room agrees.

The nearest global section of the constant sheaf is one temperature for the whole lab, the mean of the readings. When mote 18 is stuck at 122 °C it drags that mean up by about two degrees, which is least squares' usual weakness. The radius reports half the largest gap, 50 °C, while the fused value moves by the fault divided by the number of motes.

A consistency radius can only see errors that are large compared with the error of the model; on a quiet Sunday night, with near 0.7 °C, that means a little over two degrees to be likely and about three to be certain.
