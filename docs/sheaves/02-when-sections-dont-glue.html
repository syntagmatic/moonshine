When local sections don't glue

H¹ is the set of edge discrepancies that no vertex assignment can explain. Computed once from the coboundary and once from a cover, it comes out the same, and when the restriction maps are invertible it only ever lives on loops.

The first essay turned a sheaf on a graph into one linear map, the coboundary , with . Its kernel is the space of global sections. This essay is about the other end of the same map: which elements of it reaches.

A nonzero class is a pattern of edge discrepancies that no choice of vertex data can account for. With the stalks' inner products, splits orthogonally as , so each class has one representative orthogonal to everything explainable. It is what least squares leaves over. Pick to minimise , and the residual lands in . It is zero exactly when was explainable, and its size is how far is from being so.

For the constant sheaf this is ordinary graph cohomology. Edge values are explainable when they are differences of a potential, which happens when their signed sum around every cycle vanishes. has one dimension per independent cycle, and the cohomology series starts from exactly this picture. What changes with a general sheaf is that the edges transform the data on its way round.

On the constant hexagon the six arrows are one arrow. Any assignment of potentials adds up to zero around the loop, so the loop's total is the one thing no assignment can touch. The class is that total divided by , every edge moves it at the same rate, and least squares spreads it evenly, which is why the residual is the same on every edge. A change that some assignment explains moves the class nowhere, which is what it means for to be a quotient. Add a chord and the class needs two coordinates. The three edges on each side of the chord share an arrow, and the chord's arrow lies along the difference of the two loops, since it runs forward round one and backward round the other.

Now twist the hexagon. The same six edges, one of them with map , and suddenly every prescription is explained. Going round, a potential no longer has to come back to itself: it comes back negated, and the equation always has a solution. The twist that destroyed the global section in the first essay also destroys the obstruction. That is forced. It is the untwisted loop ADEF. The arrows of AB, BC and CD vanish: a change on any of them is explained by values on the twisted loop ABCD, with E and F copying D.

Twists only show up around loops, which is why the tree never leaves a residual. Choose a root and walk outward. Each edge leads to a vertex not yet assigned, and if that vertex's restriction map onto the edge is surjective, some value there produces the prescribed discrepancy exactly. Gluing on a cover

The coboundary is one way to compute . The way sheaves were first handled, and the way they are handled on spaces with no cells at all, is to cover the space by open sets, compute sections on each, and ask whether they agree where they overlap. On a graph the open sets are those of the Alexandrov topology: a set of cells is open when it contains every edge touching each of its vertices. An arc of the hexagon, the open stars of a run of consecutive vertices, is open, and so is a single edge on its own. Sections over an open set are assignments to all its cells that satisfy every restriction inside , a vector space .

Given a cover and a local section on each piece, the sheaf axiom says they glue to a global section exactly when every pair agrees on its overlap. That is the kernel of the first map of the Čech complex,

With the constant sheaf and three arcs, "Make them agree" always finds a common value, and the nerve is a triangle, a combinatorial circle. The Čech complex is with of rank two, which leaves one dimension in and one in , the same loop the coboundary found. With the twist, the only family that glues is zero. A local section on the arc containing the edge flips sign across it, and no choice of three constants can satisfy all three overlaps. That is the twisted hexagon's missing global section, now seen as a failure to glue.

Two arcs cover the hexagon too, but they overlap in two separate places, near A and near D. The nerve is a single segment with nothing circular about it, and the loop has moved into the overlap: its sections are , one number per component, and the Čech complex still has . The wide cover has a nonempty triple overlap (the three edges BC, DE and FA lie in all three arcs), so is no longer zero, and the answer is still the same. Every cover in the menu except one gives the cellular answer, for both sheaves.

The exception is the cover by one piece, the whole hexagon. Its Čech complex is in degree zero and nothing else, so it cannot see any , and for the constant sheaf it misses the loop. The piece itself is not simple enough. The theorem behind the agreement, usually credited to Leray, needs every piece and every intersection to have no cohomology of its own above degree zero, and an arc has none, while the whole hexagon carries the very class being computed. For the twisted sheaf the one-piece cover does give the right answer, and for the reason the theorem gives: the twisted hexagon has no of its own, so the single piece already satisfies the hypothesis. The Mayer–Vietoris essay in the cohomology series does the two-piece case of this bookkeeping for constant coefficients.

Both computations say the same thing about where obstructions live. A tree, or any piece of a graph that contains no loop, can always be dealt with one edge at a time. What cannot be dealt with locally is a loop whose data comes back changed, and H¹ is the name for the ways that can happen. Sensor fusion, the subject of the next essay, is where this stops being a yes-or-no question: real readings never quite glue, and the useful quantity is how far they are from gluing.
