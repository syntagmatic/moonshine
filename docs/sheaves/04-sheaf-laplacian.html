The sheaf Laplacian

A sheaf defines an energy, the total disagreement across its edges. Heat flow on that energy finds the global sections, and the spectrum tells you how hard they are to find.

A cellular sheaf on a graph puts a vector space on every vertex and every edge. It also puts a linear map on each place where a vertex meets an edge, taking the vertex's data to the edge's. Call the spaces stalks and the maps restriction maps, written . An assignment picks a vector in each vertex stalk. Friends and enemies

Start with the smallest change that matters. Keep every stalk one-dimensional, but let a restriction map be as well as . A edge asks its endpoints to be equal. A edge asks them to be opposite. That is a signed graph, the model social psychologists use for friendship and enmity. A global section assigns each person a number so that friends match and enemies are exact opposites, which amounts to splitting the group into two camps (up to scale).

Such a split exists exactly when the graph is balanced: when every cycle has an even number of enemy edges. That is Harary's theorem, and in sheaf language it is a statement about loops. Going around a cycle multiplies the restriction maps together. An odd number of s leaves a sign flip that no assignment on the cycle can absorb.

Twisting the stalks

With two-dimensional stalks, the restriction maps can be rotations. Put an arrow at every vertex of a hexagon and ask each edge to carry the arrow at one end to the arrow at the other by a fixed rotation. A global section is a choice of arrows that satisfies every edge at once. Follow the rules around the loop and you come back rotated by the product of all the edge rotations, the holonomy . A consistent arrow has to be fixed by that rotation, which happens only when . Then is the whole plane, two-dimensional, since any starting arrow at one vertex propagates. Otherwise it is zero.

Figure 2. A rotation sheaf on a hexagon. The arrows are the assignment, and the faint green arrows are the global section the flow is heading for. At θ = 0 the arrows swing into line and the curve in the plot (below on a phone) levels off at the length of that section. Move θ off zero and the green arrows vanish: the curve bends into a straight line on the log scale and settles onto the dashed prediction, decay at the rate of the smallest eigenvalue. The arrows are redrawn larger as they shrink (the factor in the corner), so they stay visible and settle into the slowest-decaying pattern, all one length. Drag an arrow tip (or focus it and use the arrow keys) to change the start. Spreading the twist over every edge changes the pattern the arrows settle into but not how fast it decays.

At every vertex a twisted hexagon looks exactly like the untwisted one: one plane per stalk, one invertible map per edge. The difference only shows up around the loop, and heat flow finds it. Spread the twist evenly and every edge looks like a small, harmless rotation. The arrows still drain away, because what matters is the holonomy, not any single edge. This is the finite version of a flat connection, and the Laplacian here is what Singer and Wu call a connection Laplacian.

Double the length of the cycle and the same twist costs a quarter of the energy, so heat flow needs four times as long to notice it. Locally the sheaf is trivial, and the only evidence against a global section is spread thinly around the whole loop. A local check cannot see it. That is what cohomology is for, and here the spectrum shows the same obstruction as a small number rather than a yes or no.

Agreement from pairwise measurements

Twisted loops can look like a toy. They are also a working method, because many measurement problems produce a rotation sheaf whose maps you measured rather than chose. Suppose forty objects each have an unknown orientation , and the only thing you can measure is the relative angle between nearby pairs. Singer called this angular synchronization. Put each measurement on its edge as a restriction map. With exact measurements, the true orientations are a global section: every loop has zero holonomy, because the differences telescope. The answer is , up to one global rotation that no relative measurement can see.

Real measurements are noisy, so no loop closes exactly and . The Laplacian still has a best effort: its bottom eigenvectors are the unit-length assignment with the least total disagreement, and reading an angle off each vertex's two coordinates gives an estimate. Singer's spectral method takes the top eigenvector of the matrix of measured phases . The sheaf Laplacian subtracts that matrix from the vertex degrees, so on a graph where every vertex has the same number of neighbours the two methods agree exactly, and elsewhere they are close relatives.

Figure 4. Spectral synchronization on simulated data. Each point is joined to its seven nearest neighbours. An edge carries the true relative angle plus Gaussian noise, or, if it is corrupted, an angle drawn uniformly at random. Thin slate rays, drawn a little longer than the arrows, are the hidden truth and green arrows are the estimate after the best global rotation. The histogram (below on a phone) counts errors. At the defaults most errors are under 20° and none reaches 45°. Push the corrupted fraction past about half and the histogram spreads toward the flat profile of a random guess.

Noise and corruption fail differently. Gaussian noise on every edge blurs the answer smoothly, because each vertex averages the evidence from its neighbours and from their neighbours in turn. Corrupted edges are wrong by arbitrary amounts, and the energy is a sum of squares, so one bad edge can pull harder than several good ones. The spectral method holds until the corrupted edges start to outvote the true ones, and then the bottom eigenvector stops describing the truth at all. Where that happens depends on how much evidence each vertex gets. Singer's analysis puts every pair in contact, and on that complete graph he recovers 400 angles with 90% of the measurements replaced by random ones. Here each object hears from about nine neighbours, and the estimate gives out far sooner. The readout shows how close the call is through the relative gap , where is the bottom eigenvalue and the next distinct one: how much less disagreement the best assignment carries than the runner-up, as a fraction of the runner-up's. Averaged over thirty layouts at the default noise, it is 0.86 with no corrupted edges, 0.32 at the default 20%, and 0.14 at half, while the median error climbs from 3° through 10° to 32°. When it is small, quite different assignments are almost equally good, and the estimate is a guess among them. The raw gap is a poor guide, because corruption lifts both eigenvalues together; over the same layouts it only drifts from 0.64 to 0.41. Even the relative gap shows a trend rather than a threshold. On the default layout it falls to 0.04 at 50% corrupted and climbs back to 0.19 at 60%, where the estimate has already failed.

Nothing on this page needed the stalks to be the same kind of object, or the maps to be invertible. Heat flow still lands on the nearest global section when the stalks have different dimensions and the maps are projections. That is the setting for the next applications: people whose private opinions and public statements live in different spaces, and graph neural networks whose restriction maps are learned rather than measured.
