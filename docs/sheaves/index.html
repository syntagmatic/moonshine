Sheaves

Local data, rules for how neighbours must agree, and what it takes for local answers to glue into a global one. Computed live on graphs, then put to work.

A sheaf attaches data to the pieces of a space, along with maps that say how the data on overlapping pieces must relate. A global section is an assignment that satisfies every one of those local rules at once. Sheaf cohomology names the reason no such assignment exists, when none does. The idea came out of algebraic topology and complex analysis, but its finite version is just linear algebra. A cellular sheaf on a graph is a collection of matrices, its global sections are the kernel of one of them, and its Laplacian turns agreement into an energy that heat flow can minimise.

These essays are written for readers who already know some topology and linear algebra. They work with cellular sheaves first, because there every number in a figure can be computed in the page, and they spend most of their time on the places sheaves are used: fusing sensor readings that disagree, consensus and opinion dynamics, recovering orientations from noisy pairwise measurements, and graph neural networks that keep neighbouring features apart. The last essay goes back to where the idea came from, the sheaf of holomorphic germs. Where an argument is plain cohomology, the essays point to the cohomology series rather than repeat it.
