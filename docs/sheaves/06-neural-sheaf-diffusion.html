Neural sheaf diffusion

A graph neural network averages each node with its neighbours, which erases the classes on graphs where neighbours tend to differ. Give the diffusion restriction maps and it can push neighbours apart instead. Let a network learn those maps on a real benchmark, and the most useful thing it finds to do is cut the links.

A graph convolutional network updates the features on each node from the features of its neighbours. Bodnar, Di Giovanni, Chamberlain, Liò and Bronstein start from the observation that the standard update, , is one Euler step of heat diffusion with a weight matrix and a nonlinearity attached. Here is the normalized graph Laplacian and holds one row of features per node. Heat diffusion makes neighbours alike. That helps on homophilic graphs, where linked nodes tend to share a label, and it hurts on heterophilic ones, where they tend not to. Run it long enough and every graph suffers, since the features converge to the kernel of the Laplacian, which on a connected graph remembers one number per feature channel. This is oversmoothing.

Heat diffusion flattens

For the graph Laplacian the kernel of on a connected graph is spanned by , the square roots of the degrees. So every feature channel ends as a multiple of one fixed vector, . Whatever the features were, the limit can separate two classes only if the degrees happen to sort them. Positive edge weights move the degrees around, and their Proposition 8 uses weights to separate two classes when every node of one class has a neighbour in its own class. Their Proposition 9 shows that nothing of this kind can work for a bipartite graph whose two sides are the two classes and have equal size. With symmetric weights each edge adds the same amount to the weighted degree of its two ends, one in each class, so the sums of over the two classes are equal, and one class cannot lie entirely below the other.

The degrees of the two classes interleave, so the best threshold, which sorts 16 of the 20 nodes at the start, sorts only 12 in the limit. The transport across each edge, , is , so a neighbour's feature arrives negated and the flow pushes the two ends of every edge toward opposite values.

Proposition 10 says this works on every connected graph with two classes, with any positive and any mixture of within-class and between-class edges: transport inside a class and across. In the language of the signed graphs of essay 4, that sheaf is balanced, since every cycle crosses between the classes an even number of times, and its one global section is the polarized one. Bodnar et al. offer it as a sheaf-theoretic reason for the negative edge weights that heterophilic GNNs had already been using. On the bipartite graph of Figure 1 there is more to see: the sign-flip sheaf is isomorphic to the constant sheaf. Negate the coordinate at every class-B vertex and every map becomes on both ends of each edge, which is the constant sheaf up to a sign per edge (row), and does not see the sign of a row. The two Laplacians have the same spectrum, and the readout's smallest nonzero eigenvalue does not change when you switch. The sheaf flow is the plain flow run on the features with class B's negated. That is exactly why the sheaf needs to know the classes to help. Choosing the right sheaf is most of the classification problem.

Learning the sheaf

Everything so far used a sheaf built from the labels, which is exactly what a classifier does not have. The model Bodnar et al. propose learns the restriction maps from the features. Each map is a function of the two endpoint features, , and it has to depend on the order of its arguments, or it could not learn asymmetric transport. They normalize by rather than , as GCN does, for numerical stability. The paper's three models learn diagonal maps, orthogonal maps, or general ones.

The graphs are three of their benchmarks, the WebKB graphs Texas, Cornell and Wisconsin: web pages from university computer science departments, linked by hyperlinks, each page described by which of 1703 words it contains and labelled with one of five classes. The page uses the files and the ten fixed train, validation and test splits distributed with Geom-GCN (Pei et al.), as the paper does. The splits hold 48%, 32% and 20% of the nodes. Texas has 183 pages, and 279 links once direction and 16 self-links are dropped, and only 17 of those 279 links join pages of the same class. The largest class has 101 pages.

What so few same-class links do to diffusion shows up in the spectrum. Take the label signal to be the class indicators, one vector per class with its mean removed, and expand it in the eigenvectors of the normalized Laplacian. An eigenvector with small eigenvalue changes little across a link; one near the top of the spectrum changes sign across most of them. A diffusion step multiplies the component at by , so steps leave the fraction of the signal's energy, where is the share on the -th eigenvector.

On these graphs the labels are high-frequency, and diffusion that smooths throws them away. The sign-flip sheaf moves them down: with the model's normalization the share of Texas's label energy below rises from 56.8% to 78.3%, and two steps keep 30.9%. With five classes that sheaf is not balanced (the smallest eigenvalue is 0.039, so some cycle crosses an odd number of flipped links) and nothing reaches the kernel. A sheaf that moves the labels to where diffusion keeps them is what the learned restriction maps are for, and the model has to find one from the words.

On Texas, plain diffusion scores 60.3%, barely above the 58.9% of always guessing the largest class, while the features alone reach 83.0%. The links hurt, as Figure 3 suggests they would: after two steps each page's words are mixed with those of neighbours that mostly belong to other classes. The learned scalar maps recover almost all of it, 81.6%. The rotations land in between, at 70.0%. Cornell and Wisconsin give the same order: features alone 73.8% and 84.9%, plain diffusion 46.5% and 61.6%, scalar maps 74.3% and 83.9%, rotations 56.8% and 72.9%.

The scalar maps never turn negative. They start near 0.8, drift for a few dozen epochs, and then collapse to zero on every edge, within- and between-class alike. On the first Texas split this happens between epochs 25 and 75, and validation accuracy climbs from 53% to 75% as it does. The same collapse happens on the first split of Cornell and of Wisconsin, and it still happens with weight decay switched off, so it is learned, not imposed by the regularizer. A zero map is a restriction map like any other (the palette of essay 1 had one), and a sheaf with every map zero has : every cochain is a global section, , and diffusion does nothing. The best sheaf this model finds for these graphs disconnects them, and the model becomes the features-only model with extra parameters.

Rotations cannot do that, since an orthogonal map has norm 1. What they learn instead is to rotate more across class boundaries. On the first Texas split, after 300 epochs, the median between-class angle is 50° against 13° for within-class edges in the first layer, and 103° against 54° in the second, which is the behaviour the paper reports for its synthetic three-class experiment (their Appendix F). With only 17 within-class edges that comparison is thin, and it does not rescue the model: the rotations still score well below ignoring the graph. Proposition 10's sheaf needs the classes to build, and the model can only guess transport from the words at the two ends of a link. On these three graphs, the learned sheaf gets the most out of the links by cutting them.

What the theory does and does not promise

The results of Section 3 are about the limit of diffusion with a sheaf chosen knowing the answer: which hypothesis classes contain a sheaf whose harmonic space separates the classes. They say that sign flips suffice for two classes, that one-dimensional stalks cannot handle three, that orthogonal maps on planes can, and that symmetric weights cannot even handle two on a balanced bipartite graph. Those statements are exact, and Figures 1 and 2 are small instances of them. What they do not say is that a network will find such a sheaf from features, or that finding one beats not diffusing at all. On the WebKB graphs, a small model with one hand-set configuration found that the best transport was none. The last essay leaves graphs behind and goes back to where sheaves came from: holomorphic functions, their germs, and analytic continuation around a branch point.
