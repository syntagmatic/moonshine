The Sky From Where You Stand

The naked-eye sky as a problem in spherical geometry and clocks.

Nearly everything astronomy knows about the universe beyond the Solar System arrived as light at one point: wherever a telescope, or an eye, happened to be. That light carries four things we can measure. It comes from a direction. It has a brightness. It has a colour, or more finely a spectrum. And it changes with time. This series pulls one thing after another out of that light, each resting on the one before. This article takes direction and time. The second turns angles and brightness into distance, the third reads the physics of stars from their colour and brightness once their distances are known, and the fourth finds planets around them from small dips and wobbles in their light.

Direction and time can be measured with nothing but eyes and a clock, and they go a long way. Every figure below works with angles on the sky and the times at which things happen, and not one of them needs to know how far away anything is. That is both the power of this geometry and, as the end of the article shows, its limit.

The celestial sphere

The stars are so far away that even Earth's yearly trip around the Sun, 300 million kilometres across, shifts the nearest of them by less than an arcsecond either way. We can't see how far away any of them is, so each star might as well be a point on a sphere around us, of any radius we like. A star's place on that sphere takes two angles, the way a city's place on Earth takes latitude and longitude. Declination δ is celestial latitude, measured from the celestial equator, the projection of Earth's equator onto the sky. Right ascension α is celestial longitude, measured east along the equator from the point where the Sun crosses it in March, and it is usually given in hours, 24 to a full turn. These coordinates belong to the stars, not to us, so a catalogue can list them once for everyone.

What you see depends on where you stand and when. From latitude φ the celestial pole stands φ degrees above your horizon (the north pole above the northern horizon, or for southern latitudes the south pole above the southern one), and the whole sphere turns about it once a day. To find a star in your own sky, its altitude a above the horizon and its azimuth A measured from north through east, you need a single clock: the local sidereal time θ, which is the right ascension crossing your meridian, the north-south line through the zenith. A star stays up all night if its whole daily circle clears the horizon, which happens when δ > 90° − φ. At the North Pole every star north of the celestial equator is circumpolar and none of the others ever rise. At the equator no star is circumpolar, and every star spends half of each turn above the horizon.

The sky turns once in a sidereal day of 23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds, not in 24 hours. The difference is the Sun's own motion. While Earth turns once, it also moves about 1/365 of the way round its orbit, so the Sun slides about a degree east against the stars, and Earth has to turn that extra degree, four minutes' worth, to bring the Sun back to the meridian. Clocks keep solar time. So at the same clock time each night the stars stand a degree further west, and each star rises four minutes earlier than the night before. Hold the clock still in figure 1 and drag the date: a month turns the sky by 30°, two hours of clock time, which is why the winter constellations belong to winter.

The pole itself moves. Earth's equator bulges, and the Sun and Moon pull on the bulge, trying to bring it into line with their own orbits. A spinning top answers a sideways pull by swinging its axis around, and Earth's axis swings around the pole of the ecliptic by about 50 arcseconds a year, a full circle in about 26,000 years. This is the precession of the equinoxes. Right ascension and declination are measured from the equator and the March equinox, so every star's coordinates slowly change, and a catalogue has to say which year's equator it means (J2000 for the one used here). Drag the year. Today the pole is 0.7° from Polaris, and it will be closest, 0.46°, around AD 2100. When the Great Pyramid was built, around 2600 BC, the pole was about a degree from Thuban in Draco, which it had passed within a tenth of a degree around 2800 BC. By AD 13,600 it swings to within 6° of Vega, which will make a bright but not very accurate pole star.

The precession is the long-term model of Vondrák, Capitaine and Wallace (2011), built to stay accurate over long time spans, where a constant precession rate drifts off. Over the slider's range it also lets the tilt of Earth's axis drift between 22.6° and 24.2°. Each star also drifts with its own proper motion, applied here in a straight line: over the slider's 26,000 years Arcturus crosses 16° of sky, more than thirty times the Moon's width.

Sundials and clocks

A sundial reads apparent solar time, the hour angle of the real Sun. It doesn't keep a steady rate, so clocks keep mean solar time instead: the hour angle of an imaginary Sun that moves along the celestial equator at a perfectly uniform rate. The gap between the two is the equation of time. It has two sign conventions. Here it is sundial minus clock, positive when the sundial is ahead, which is the right ascension of the mean Sun minus that of the true Sun:

The two brackets are two separate causes, and they simply add. The first is the eccentricity of Earth's orbit. Earth moves fastest at perihelion in early January, so the Sun's true longitude λ runs ahead of the uniform mean longitude L for half the year and behind it for the other half, by an amount Kepler's equation gives. The second is the tilt. Even a Sun that moved uniformly along the ecliptic would not move uniformly in right ascension, because the ecliptic is tilted against the equator. Near the equinoxes part of its motion goes north or south and is lost to right ascension. Near the solstices all of it goes east, and it goes east at high declination, where the hour circles crowd together, so it counts for extra. The first cause repeats once a year and the second twice.

Photograph the Sun at the same clock time every day for a year and it traces a figure eight on the sky, the analemma: up and down with the Sun's declination, east and west with the equation of time.

With Earth's values the eccentricity term swings about 7.7 minutes each way over a year and the tilt term about 9.9 minutes each way twice a year; they reinforce in November and partly cancel in May. Set the eccentricity to zero and only the tilt term remains: the eight becomes symmetric, both lobes the same size, because that term repeats every half year. Now restore the eccentricity and drag the tilt down. The tilt term shrinks with the square of the tilt, faster than the declination, which shrinks only in proportion, so the eccentricity term takes over and the eight opens into an ellipse. The Sun's east-west offset is plotted in minutes of time; one minute is a quarter of a degree, so the real analemma is much thinner than drawn. The model is a Kepler orbit with Earth's perihelion 283° from the March equinox and leaves out the small wobbles the Moon and planets add.

Retrograde motion

The planets wander against the stars, mostly eastward, the same way as the Sun and Moon. But every so often a planet slows, stops, runs backwards for weeks, stops again and resumes. In a Sun-centred picture it is what overtaking looks like from Earth. The direction to Mars is the line from Earth to Mars, and when Earth, on the faster inside track, passes Mars, that line swings backwards against the distant stars, the way a slower car on a motorway seems to slide backwards against the hills while you pass it.

The figure computes both planets' positions from JPL's approximate orbital elements, solving Kepler's equation for each, and the direction to the planet as seen from Earth is the direction of the difference between the two position vectors. The lower chart repeats the calculation for all seven planets over fifty years. Every spell lasts longer the farther out the planet is; beyond Mars, the farther the planet, the less ground it loses.

The outer planets' pattern follows from the geometry. A distant planet hardly moves in a year, so its retrograde spell lasts most of the time Earth spends on the near side of its orbit, which approaches half a year as the planet gets farther away; Neptune spends 158 days of every year going backwards. But from a distant planet Earth's orbit looks small, so the backwards swing it causes shrinks, down to 3° for Neptune. Mars sits at the other extreme: it spends only about 72 days in retrograde, but backs up by 15°.

Why eclipses repeat

A solar eclipse needs a new Moon, and there is one every synodic month, 29.53 days. Most new Moons pass the Sun by, because the Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the ecliptic, so the Moon crosses the Sun's path only at two points, the nodes, and only a new Moon near a node can cover the Sun. The Moon returns to the same node every draconic month of 27.21 days, shorter than its 27.32-day orbit around the stars because the nodes themselves slide backwards round the ecliptic once every 18.6 years. And the Moon's distance, which decides whether a central eclipse is total or annular, cycles with the anomalistic month of 27.55 days, from one perigee to the next.

So an eclipse repeats with the same geometry when three clocks come round together. They almost do, after 223 synodic months:

That interval is the saros, 18 years and 11 days (or 10, or 12, depending on leap days) and about 8 hours. In those 8 hours Earth turns about a third of the way round, so the next eclipse in the family falls about 120° of longitude further west.

At 358, the inex, the Moon is at the opposite node; that is why neighbouring saros series alternate between the two nodes. At 669, three saroses, even the day dial comes back round.

The saros isn't exact, and the small mismatch is what makes an eclipse family finite. After each saros the new Moon sits about half a degree further along its orbit from the node than the time before. An eclipse is possible only while the new Moon falls inside a window around the node, and the canon's complete families average 74 eclipses, so that window is about 74 steps of 0.48°, some 35°, wide. A family is born with a small partial eclipse at one pole of the Earth, drifts across the globe a little each time as the Moon's shadow crosses Earth slightly further north or south, and dies at the other pole after some 70 to 86 eclipses and 12 to 15 centuries. New families start all the time, so about 40 run at once.

What angles can't tell us

Look back at the five figures and none of them uses a distance. The globe in figure 1 has an arbitrary radius. The equation of time needs the shape of Earth's orbit, not its size. Retrograde motion depends on the ratios of the orbits' sizes, not the sizes themselves, and the saros is a statement about ratios of periods. The celestial sphere has no radius; we chose one for convenience. The one place where geometry reaches out into the third dimension is the one exception to the stars' fixed directions: the tiny back-and-forth shift of the nearest stars as Earth carries us round the Sun. That parallax is where the next article begins.
