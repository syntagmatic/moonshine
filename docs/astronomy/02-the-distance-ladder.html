The Distance Ladder

How a telescope that measures only angles and brightness reaches billions of light years, one calibrated rung at a time.

Parallax

Earth carries us around a circle 300 million kilometres across every year, so we look at the sky from a different place in January than in July. A nearby star seen from two ends of that circle lies in slightly different directions, and it shifts against the much more distant stars behind it. The parallax ϖ is the angle subtended at the star by the radius of Earth's orbit, one astronomical unit. For a star at distance d the angle is tiny, so ϖ = 1 AU / d in radians, and astronomers pick the unit of distance that makes this as simple as possible. One parsec is the distance at which the parallax is one arcsecond, 206,265 AU or about 3.26 light years, and then

No star is close enough for its parallax to reach one arcsecond. The nearest ones have parallaxes of a few hundred milliarcseconds, the angle a coin subtends from ten kilometres away.

The star doesn't just rock back and forth. It traces Earth's orbit as the star would see it, shrunk to the size of the parallax. A star near the pole of the ecliptic, the plane of Earth's orbit, sees the orbit face on and traces a circle. A star in the ecliptic sees it edge on and slides back and forth along a line. Most stars sit in between and trace an ellipse whose long axis is always ϖ and whose short axis is ϖ times the sine of the star's ecliptic latitude. On top of that, every star drifts steadily across the sky with its own motion through the Galaxy, its proper motion, so the real path is a loop pulled into a wave. Gaia fits exactly this model to each star: two numbers for its position, two for its proper motion and one for its parallax.

That fast motion is what first drew astronomers to it, and in 1838 Friedrich Bessel published its parallax, 0.31″ against Gaia's 0.29″, the first reliable distance to any star beyond the Sun. Positions, parallaxes and proper motions are Gaia DR3 values at the catalogue's reference epoch, 2016.0; Earth's position comes from a low-precision solar ephemeris.

These are some of the easiest parallaxes in the sky: tens to hundreds of milliarcseconds, each measured by Gaia to a tenth of a milliarcsecond or better, so their distances are good to a part in a thousand. A star a hundred times further away has a parallax a hundred times smaller, but Gaia's errors don't shrink with it, and for fainter stars they grow. Most of the stars in Gaia's catalogue are far enough and faint enough that the error is a large fraction of the parallax.

Why you can't just invert a parallax

A parallax measurement is a number with an error bar, and the error is close to Gaussian: the measured ϖ scatters symmetrically around the true one. Distance is 1/ϖ, which is not symmetric at all. A parallax measured 30% too small gives a distance 43% too large, while one measured 30% too large gives a distance only 23% too small. As the error grows, some measurements land near zero, where 1/ϖ runs off to enormous distances, and some land below zero, where it gives no distance at all. A negative parallax is not a mistake in the catalogue. It is what a small parallax plus a larger error looks like.

The cleanest way to see this is a group of stars that we know share one distance. The open cluster M67 is such a group: over a thousand stars born together and still moving through space together. The ones used here lie within 7 pc of its centre on the sky, under 1% of its distance, so for this purpose they all sit at one distance. That shared motion lets us pick its members out of the Gaia catalogue without looking at their parallaxes at all, just by their positions and proper motions, so their parallax errors are left whole. The bright members have parallaxes good to about 2%, and together they put the cluster at 835 pc. The faint members are at the same distance, but their parallaxes are far less precise.

Slide from precise parallaxes to poor ones and the histogram changes shape. At a few percent it is a narrow spike at the cluster. By 30% it has a long tail toward large distances, and the average of the inverted parallaxes lands well beyond the cluster, even though nothing about these stars is wrong. The computed curve follows the tail, so the skew is arithmetic, not bad data. Among the least precise stars a few parallaxes come out negative.

The cure is to stop asking what distance the parallax implies and ask instead which distances are consistent with it. Bayes' theorem makes that precise. The likelihood is the probability of the measured parallax if the star were at distance r, a Gaussian in ϖ centred on 1/r. The prior says which distances are plausible before we look. A simple choice counts the volume in each shell, which grows as r², and lets the density of stars fall off exponentially with a length scale L, so that distant stars aren't favoured without limit:

For a star with a 2% parallax the likelihood is a narrow spike, and whatever prior you choose, the posterior sits right on it: the answer is 1/ϖ. At 30% the likelihood is broad and lopsided, and the prior starts to decide. With L = 1000 pc the prior expects most stars to be about 2 kpc away and drags every posterior outward, so across the group the posterior medians land further from the cluster than the naive distances do. Shrink L to about 200 pc and they centre on it. Neither is an error in the arithmetic. The prior is a claim about the population the star was drawn from, and a member of a cluster at 835 pc is not a typical star in that direction. The large Gaia distance catalogues build their prior from a three-dimensional model of the Galaxy, direction by direction, and some also use each star's colour and brightness, which is the diagram in the next section put to work as a prior.

What the posterior always gets right is the shape of our ignorance. It is never negative or infinite, it widens as the parallax gets worse, and for the worst parallaxes and the negative ones it hands back little more than the prior. A poor parallax doesn't give a distance; it rules some distances out.

A cluster offers a better way out, because its stars share one distance. Average the parallaxes, each weighted by the inverse square of its error, and you have one precise parallax, and only then invert it. Averaging the inverted parallaxes instead would carry the skew of every faint star into the answer. The next rung of the ladder has this problem on a much larger scale: the stars that set its calibration are few and far, so their parallaxes are exactly the kind that can't just be inverted.

Brightness, corrected for distance

Astronomers measure brightness in magnitudes, and the scale runs backwards. A smaller number means a brighter star, and five magnitudes is a factor of exactly 100 in the light received, so one magnitude is a factor of about 2.512. A star's colour is measured the same way, as the difference between its magnitudes in two filters. Gaia records every star in one broad band, G, running from 330 to 1,030 nm, the whole visible range and into the near infrared, and in a blue part and a red part of it, BP (up to about 670 nm) and RP (from about 620 nm). A red star is fainter in BP than in RP, so its BP−RP is large; a blue star's is small or negative.

A star's apparent magnitude mixes two things we would like to keep apart: how much light the star gives off, and how far away it is. Parallax separates them. Define the absolute magnitude M as the magnitude the star would have if it sat at 10 parsecs. The left panel already looks like a blurred copy of the right one, and that is itself the work of parallax. These stars were chosen because their parallaxes put them within 100 pc, so they sit between 10 and 100 pc away and their apparent magnitudes are pushed faint by between 0 and 5 magnitudes. Pick Sun-like dwarfs and the readout shows the smear directly: the same stars spread over more magnitudes in G than in MG. The smear is lopsided, because volume grows as the cube of distance: 87% of these stars are beyond 50 pc. A sample taken without parallax, from 10 pc out to 10 kpc, would spread each sequence over 15 magnitudes and wash it out entirely.

At a parallax 10 times its error, every absolute magnitude is good to about a fifth of a magnitude, which is the cut the Gaia team used for their own HR diagrams. Loosen it and the diagram does more than blur. A new population appears, a haze below the red end of the main sequence, filling the space toward the white dwarfs. Most of these aren't nearby stars at all. They are distant, faint sources whose astrometric solution went wrong, usually because a close pair of stars was resolved on some of Gaia's scans and not on others, which is most likely in crowded fields. Their parallaxes came out large by accident, and the formula faithfully turned that accident into a faint absolute magnitude. The Gaia Catalogue of Nearby Stars, which weeds these out with a trained classifier, keeps under 1% of the haze stars whose parallax is less than 10 times its error, and only 7% of those between 10 and 20. Raise the cut to 20 and most of the haze goes, and nine in ten of the stars left there are in the catalogue. The RUWE box applies a different test, rejecting stars whose motion on the sky fits a single-star model poorly, which also removes many genuine unresolved binaries.

Two corrections are left out on purpose. Gaia's parallaxes carry a zero-point bias of a few tens of microarcseconds; every star here has a parallax above 10 mas, so correcting it would move an absolute magnitude by about a hundredth of a magnitude at most. Dust would dim and redden the stars, but the Sun sits inside the Local Bubble, a region so empty of dust that reddening is almost negligible within about 60 pc, and we ignore it out to 100.

Leavitt's law

Parallax runs out quickly. The M67 figure showed Gaia's parallaxes turning to noise for faint stars less than a kiloparsec away, and the Large Magellanic Cloud, the Milky Way's largest satellite galaxy, is fifty times further. To go on we need a standard candle: a kind of star whose luminosity we can learn without knowing its distance. Then its apparent magnitude m and absolute magnitude M give the distance through the distance modulus, the same inverse-square law as before read the other way:

Classical Cepheids are that kind of star. They are young supergiants, thousands of times as luminous as the Sun, that swell and shrink with a period of days to months, and their brightness rises and falls with them. In 1908 Henrietta Leavitt, cataloguing variable stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud from photographic plates at Harvard, noticed that the brighter ones had the longer periods, and in 1912 she showed the trend for 25 of them. Every star in the cloud is at nearly the same distance from us, so the brighter apparent magnitude meant a greater luminosity. If the period sets the luminosity, a Cepheid's period, which is easy to measure at any distance, gives its absolute magnitude.

First the period. A survey doesn't watch one star continuously. It returns to each field every few nights, around weather and the Moon, so a light curve is a few hundred measurements scattered over years. To find the period, fold the measurements: plot each one against its phase, the fraction of a cycle since some start time, for a trial period. At the wrong period the phases are scrambled and the fold is a cloud. At the right one every cycle lands on top of the last.

Data: OGLE-IV I-band photometry (Soszyński et al. 2015).

The fold snaps into place only in a narrow window, and that is why Cepheid periods are known so well: over six years a 10-day Cepheid completes more than two hundred cycles, and a period wrong by a part in a thousand would slide the last cycle a fifth of the way out of phase with the first. Folded, the light curve has the Cepheid's signature shape, a quick rise to maximum and a slower fall.

The relation is there in every band, but its tightness is not. In V the Cepheids of one period scatter by about 0.2 magnitudes around the line, in I by 0.15. Part of that is dust, which dims and reddens each star by a different amount depending on what lies in front of it. Dust dims a star in I by a fixed multiple of the amount it reddens its V−I colour, about 1.55 for ordinary interstellar dust, so the combination

called a Wesenheit magnitude, comes out the same with or without dust in the way. In WI the LMC scatter falls to under 0.08 magnitudes, about half of what it is in I, and under 4% in distance for a single star.

The Small Magellanic Cloud's Cepheids follow the same law, displaced downward: they are further away. The displacement is a difference of distance moduli, so it gives the ratio of the two distances directly, with no calibration at all. The SMC's scatter is twice the LMC's, largely because the SMC is stretched along our line of sight: eclipsing binaries in it lie at distances up to 10 kpc apart. Its Cepheids are also poorer in metals, and metal-poor Cepheids are slightly fainter, by roughly 0.2 magnitudes for a factor of ten in metal content, which is a likely reason the Cepheid distance to the SMC comes out a little longer than the one from eclipsing binaries.

That comparison needs one more number: the distance to the LMC itself. The PL relation gives only relative distances; its zero point must come from a galaxy whose distance is known some other way. For the LMC it comes from twenty eclipsing binaries, pairs of stars whose sizes follow from their eclipses and orbital velocities and whose surface brightness follows from their colour, which together give a distance accurate to one percent: 49.59 kpc. It is one of three anchors used by the SH0ES team, whose Hubble constant appears in the next section; the others are Milky Way Cepheids with Gaia parallaxes and the galaxy NGC 4258, whose distance comes from masers in its nucleus. With an anchor, a single Cepheid anywhere gives a distance.

Figure 6. A galaxy's distance from its Cepheids. Each Cepheid's distance modulus is its apparent magnitude minus the LMC relation of figure 5 at its period, plus the LMC's own modulus, 18.477. The curves flatten. Averaging more Cepheids beats down the scatter of the relation, fastest in WI, but it can't touch the anchor, and every Cepheid distance in every galaxy inherits the LMC's 1.1%. The whole ladder works this way. Each rung is calibrated on the one below, and its error floor is the error of that calibration, carried upward unchanged. Cepheids can be picked out in galaxies a few tens of megaparsecs away, far enough to reach galaxies that have hosted a particular kind of exploding star, and that is the next rung.

Supernovae and the Hubble constant

Cepheids can be picked out several tens of megaparsecs away and no further. The next candle is a star at the moment it destroys itself. A Type Ia supernova is the thermonuclear explosion of a white dwarf, and for a few weeks it can rival the light of its whole galaxy. At its peak it reaches an absolute magnitude of about −19.3 in the B band, some billions of times the Sun, which makes it visible across most of the observable universe: the most distant supernova in the sample below is at a redshift of 2.26.

They are nearly standard, but not quite. Measured at the same distance, their peak magnitudes scatter by about a quarter of a magnitude, 12% in distance. Two regularities take most of that out. Supernovae whose light curves rise and fall more slowly are brighter, a relation Mark Phillips quantified in 1993, and redder supernovae are fainter, partly from dust and partly intrinsically. The stretch correction alone takes the scatter from about 0.26 magnitudes to 0.22, the colour correction alone to 0.17, and the two together to about 0.13, which is 6% in distance for a single supernova, nearly as good as a Cepheid, at thousands of times the distance of the Magellanic Clouds.

What the supernovae measure, far enough out, is the expansion of the universe. Distant galaxies recede with a velocity proportional to their distance, v = H0d, and the velocity shows up as a redshift z. For small z, v ≈ cz. The Hubble constant H0 sets the scale, and the size and age of the universe along with it. The supernova magnitudes and redshifts alone give relative distances, and so the slope of the Hubble diagram only up to the unknown absolute magnitude MB. That magnitude comes from the supernovae that went off in galaxies close enough to have Cepheid distances, and those Cepheids get their zero point from the anchors of the last section. This is the ladder assembled: parallax and eclipsing binaries calibrate Cepheids, Cepheids calibrate supernovae, and supernovae reach the Hubble flow.

At redshifts up to 0.15 the expansion's acceleration already matters slightly, and the fit uses the standard expansion of the luminosity distance to second order in z, with the deceleration parameter q0 = −0.55 and jerk j0 = 1 of a flat universe with 30% matter:

With the SH0ES Cepheid distances as published, the page finds H0 close to their 73. The slider is the ladder's error propagation made visible. Every Cepheid distance shares the same zero point, so a shift of 0.1 magnitudes in it, a 5% change in every Cepheid distance, moves H0 by 5% as well. Nothing downstream can average it away.

The cosmic microwave background gives a different route to H0 that uses no ladder at all: the early universe's physics sets a length, the standard model of cosmology carries it forward to today, and the result is 67.4. To reach that value from the ladder, every Cepheid distance would have to be about 9% too short, a shift of about 0.18 magnitudes (the "Match Planck" button), several times the anchors' quoted uncertainty. This is the Hubble tension. It has survived years of checks, including JWST observations of Cepheids that rule out crowding as the cause, and a 2025 community analysis combining many distance indicators found 73.50 ± 0.81, seven standard deviations from the value the cosmic microwave background implies. It isn't settled either: the Chicago-Carnegie program, calibrating supernovae with red giants instead of Cepheids, finds values near 70, and the question of whether the tension is a measurement problem or new physics is still open.

Read left to right, the chart is the article. A parallax is a triangle, and needs nothing else. The Magellanic Clouds are too far for useful parallaxes, so the Cepheid rung stands on eclipsing binaries there, and on parallaxes of Cepheids in our own Galaxy. The supernova rung stands on the few dozen galaxies near enough for Cepheids that happened to host a supernova. And the Hubble constant stands on all of it, which is why an error in the distances to the anchors turns up, undiminished, in a number that describes the whole universe.
