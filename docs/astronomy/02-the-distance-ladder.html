The Distance Ladder

How a telescope that measures only angles and brightness reaches billions of light years, one calibrated rung at a time.

Parallax

Earth carries us around a circle 300 million kilometres across every year, so we look at the sky from a different place in January than in July. A nearby star seen from two ends of that circle lies in slightly different directions, and it shifts against the much more distant stars behind it. The parallax ϖ is the angle subtended at the star by the radius of Earth's orbit, one astronomical unit. For a star at distance d the angle is tiny, so ϖ = 1 AU / d in radians, and astronomers pick the unit of distance that makes this as simple as possible. One parsec is the distance at which the parallax is one arcsecond, 206,265 AU or about 3.26 light years, and then

No star is close enough for its parallax to reach one arcsecond. The nearest ones have parallaxes of a few hundred milliarcseconds, the angle a coin subtends from ten kilometres away.

The star doesn't just rock back and forth. It traces Earth's orbit as the star would see it, shrunk to the size of the parallax. A star near the pole of the ecliptic, the plane of Earth's orbit, sees the orbit face on and traces a circle. A star in the ecliptic sees it edge on and slides back and forth along a line. Most stars sit in between and trace an ellipse whose long axis is always ϖ and whose short axis is ϖ times the sine of the star's ecliptic latitude. On top of that, every star drifts steadily across the sky with its own motion through the Galaxy, its proper motion, so the real path is a loop pulled into a wave. Gaia fits exactly this model to each star: two numbers for its position, two for its proper motion and one for its parallax.

Positions, parallaxes and proper motions are Gaia DR3 values at the catalogue's reference epoch, 2016.0; Earth's position comes from a low-precision solar ephemeris.

These are some of the easiest parallaxes in the sky: tens to hundreds of milliarcseconds, each measured by Gaia to a tenth of a milliarcsecond or better, so their distances are good to a part in a thousand. A star a hundred times further away has a parallax a hundred times smaller, but Gaia's errors don't shrink with it, and for fainter stars they grow. Most of the stars in Gaia's catalogue are far enough and faint enough that the error is a large fraction of the parallax.

Why you can't just invert a parallax

A parallax measurement is a number with an error bar, and the error is close to Gaussian: the measured ϖ scatters symmetrically around the true one. Distance is 1/ϖ, which is not symmetric at all. A parallax measured 30% too small gives a distance 43% too large, while one measured 30% too large gives a distance only 23% too small. As the error grows, some measurements land near zero, where 1/ϖ runs off to enormous distances, and some land below zero, where it gives no distance at all. A negative parallax is not a mistake in the catalogue. It is what a small parallax plus a larger error looks like.

The cleanest way to see this is a group of stars that we know share one distance. The open cluster M67 is such a group: over a thousand stars born together and still moving through space together. The ones used here lie within 7 pc of its centre on the sky, under 1% of its distance, so for this purpose they all sit at one distance. That shared motion lets us pick its members out of the Gaia catalogue without looking at their parallaxes at all, just by their positions and proper motions, so their parallax errors are left whole. The bright members have parallaxes good to about 2%, and together they put the cluster at 835 pc. The faint members are at the same distance, but their parallaxes are far less precise.

Slide from precise parallaxes to poor ones and the histogram changes shape. At a few percent it is a narrow spike at the cluster. By 30% it has a long tail toward large distances, and the average of the inverted parallaxes lands well beyond the cluster, even though nothing about these stars is wrong. The computed curve follows the tail, so the skew is arithmetic, not bad data. Among the least precise stars a few parallaxes come out negative.

The cure is to stop asking what distance the parallax implies and ask instead which distances are consistent with it. Bayes' theorem makes that precise. The likelihood is the probability of the measured parallax if the star were at distance r, a Gaussian in ϖ centred on 1/r. The prior says which distances are plausible before we look. A simple choice counts the volume in each shell, which grows as r², and lets the density of stars fall off exponentially with a length scale L, so that distant stars aren't favoured without limit:

For a star with a 2% parallax the likelihood is a narrow spike, and whatever prior you choose, the posterior sits right on it: the answer is 1/ϖ. At 30% the likelihood is broad and lopsided, and the prior starts to decide. With L = 1000 pc the prior expects most stars to be about 2 kpc away and drags every posterior outward, so across the group the posterior medians land further from the cluster than the naive distances do. Shrink L to about 200 pc and they centre on it. Neither is an error in the arithmetic. The prior is a claim about the population the star was drawn from, and a member of a cluster at 835 pc is not a typical star in that direction. The large Gaia distance catalogues build their prior from a three-dimensional model of the Galaxy, direction by direction, and some also use each star's colour and brightness, which is the diagram in the next section put to work as a prior.

What the posterior always gets right is the shape of our ignorance. It is never negative or infinite, it widens as the parallax gets worse, and for the worst parallaxes and the negative ones it hands back little more than the prior. A poor parallax doesn't give a distance; it rules some distances out.

A cluster offers a better way out, because its stars share one distance. Average the parallaxes, each weighted by the inverse square of its error, and you have one precise parallax, and only then invert it. Averaging the inverted parallaxes instead would carry the skew of every faint star into the answer. The next rung of the ladder has this problem on a much larger scale: the stars that set its calibration are few and far, so their parallaxes are exactly the kind that can't just be inverted.

Brightness, corrected for distance

Astronomers measure brightness in magnitudes, and the scale runs backwards. A smaller number means a brighter star, and five magnitudes is a factor of exactly 100 in the light received, so one magnitude is a factor of about 2.512. A star's colour is measured the same way, as the difference between its magnitudes in two filters. Gaia records every star in a broad visible band, G, and in a blue half and a red half of it, BP and RP. A red star is fainter in BP than in RP, so its BP−RP is large; a blue star's is small or negative.

A star's apparent magnitude mixes two things we would like to keep apart: how much light the star gives off, and how far away it is. Parallax separates them. Define the absolute magnitude M as the magnitude the star would have if it sat at 10 parsecs. The right panel sorts itself into a few populations. Most stars lie on the main sequence, the diagonal band from hot, bright stars at the upper left to cool, faint red dwarfs at the lower right. A thin scatter of giants sits above its middle: stars redder than the Sun that are nonetheless about 40 times brighter than the Sun-like dwarfs, so they must be much larger. Far below the main sequence runs a second, narrower sequence of white dwarfs. At a BP−RP of 0.3 they are about 11 magnitudes, some twenty-five thousand times, fainter than main-sequence stars of the same colour, and since the same colour means roughly the same surface temperature, they must be tiny.

The left panel already looks like a blurred copy of the right one, and that is itself the work of parallax. These stars were chosen because their parallaxes put them within 100 pc, so they sit between 10 and 100 pc away and their apparent magnitudes are pushed faint by between 0 and 5 magnitudes. Pick Sun-like dwarfs and the readout shows the smear directly: the same stars spread over more magnitudes in G than in MG. The smear is lopsided, because volume grows as the cube of distance: 87% of these stars are beyond 50 pc. A sample taken without parallax, from 10 pc out to 10 kpc, would spread each sequence over 15 magnitudes and wash it out entirely.

At a parallax 10 times its error, every absolute magnitude is good to about a fifth of a magnitude, which is the cut the Gaia team used for their own HR diagrams. Loosen it and the diagram does more than blur. A new population appears, a haze below the red end of the main sequence, filling the space toward the white dwarfs. Most of these aren't nearby stars at all. They are distant, faint sources whose astrometric solution went wrong, usually because a close pair of stars was resolved on some of Gaia's scans and not on others, which is most likely in crowded fields. Their parallaxes came out large by accident, and the formula faithfully turned that accident into a faint absolute magnitude. The Gaia Catalogue of Nearby Stars, which weeds these out with a trained classifier, keeps under 1% of the haze stars whose parallax is less than 10 times its error, and only 7% of those between 10 and 20. Raise the cut to 20 and most of the haze goes, and nine in ten of the stars left there are in the catalogue. The RUWE box applies a different test, rejecting stars whose motion on the sky fits a single-star model poorly, which also removes many genuine unresolved binaries.

Two corrections are left out on purpose. Gaia's parallaxes carry a zero-point bias of a few tens of microarcseconds; every star here has a parallax above 10 mas, so correcting it would move an absolute magnitude by about a hundredth of a magnitude at most. Dust would dim and redden the stars, but the Sun sits inside the Local Bubble, a region so empty of dust that reddening is almost negligible within about 60 pc, and we ignore it out to 100.
