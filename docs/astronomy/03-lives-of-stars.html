Lives of Stars

With distances in hand, the HR diagram becomes physics: balls of gas holding themselves up, burning fuel at a rate their mass decides.

In the last article, parallax turned the apparent brightness of Gaia stars within 100 parsecs into luminosity, and on a Hertzsprung-Russell diagram they sorted themselves into a long diagonal main sequence, a scatter of giants above it and a thin line of white dwarfs far below. That diagram is a measurement. What follows is about why it looks the way it does.

Two numbers place a star on the diagram: its luminosity L, and its surface temperature T, which its colour tells us. A star radiates roughly like a blackbody, so each square metre of its surface gives off σT4, and the two numbers together fix its radius, L = 4πR2σT4. That already explains the giants: redder than the Sun and still dozens of times brighter, they must be many times larger. It doesn't explain why stars of one temperature should mostly have one luminosity, which is what a main sequence is. For that we need what's inside.

The answer, worked out over the first half of the twentieth century, is that a star on the main sequence is fixed almost completely by its mass. Its mass sets how hard gravity squeezes it, the squeeze sets how hot its centre gets, and its mass and structure set how fast radiation leaks out, which is its luminosity. The burning at the centre settles at whatever rate replaces that leak, and the fuel divided by the rate is how long the star lasts. Below, those steps are taken one at a time, tested against two real star clusters, and followed to what's left when the fuel runs out.

A ball of gas holding itself up

A star neither collapses nor flies apart, so at every radius the weight of the gas above must be carried by pressure from below. Take a thin shell at radius r, of thickness dr, with mass m(r) inside it. Gravity pulls each unit volume of the shell inward with force G mρ/r2, and the pressure falling across the shell pushes it outward with force −dP/dr. These already say something without being solved. Replace the derivative by the central pressure over the radius and the mass and density by the Sun's, and the centre must hold up a pressure of order GM2/R4, about 1016 dyn/cm2, ten billion atmospheres. If ideal gas supplies that pressure, the centre has to be millions of kelvin hot. The star's temperature isn't set by its fuel. It is set by gravity, and the fuel has to keep up.

Two equations with three unknown functions, P, ρ and m, need a third to close them. The full answer ties pressure to density through the temperature, which depends on how energy is made and carried out, and needs a computer. The simplest closure that keeps the physics is a polytrope, a pressure that depends on density alone, P = Kρ1+1/n, where the index n says how compressible the gas is. Write the density as ρcθn and measure radius in a unit ξ built from K, n and ρc, and the two equations fold into one, the Lane-Emden equation, with no constants left in it:

Start at the centre with θ = 1 and zero slope, integrate outward, and the star's surface is the first zero, ξ1, where the density falls to nothing. An n = 3 polytrope is a surprisingly good Sun for a model with one free number. Its profiles have the right shape, its central pressure is half the solar model's, and it puts the centre at about 12 million kelvin, against the model's 15.7 million. The real Sun is twice as centrally concentrated, because billions of years of burning have piled helium into the core, and helium, with fewer particles per gram, needs a denser, hotter core to give the same pressure. A polytrope has one composition throughout, so it can't follow that change.

The lower indices describe other kinds of star. At n = 1.5 the pressure-density law is that of a monatomic gas stirred by convection, which keeps it on an adiabat, P ∝ ρ5/3. That is how the smallest red dwarfs are built, convective all the way through, and also, it will turn out, a cold white dwarf of modest mass. The same equation will return at the end of the article, with the pressure supplied by something other than heat.

Two ways to burn hydrogen

Fifteen million kelvin sounds hot enough for anything, but it isn't hot enough to fuse hydrogen, at least not classically. Two protons must come within about 10−13 cm of each other for the strong force to act, and at that distance their electric repulsion is a barrier of about a million electron volts. The typical thermal energy kT at the Sun's centre is 1.3 keV, a thousand times smaller. Fusion happens only because quantum mechanics lets a proton tunnel through the barrier, with a probability that falls off as exp(−√(EG/E)). The Gamow energy EG = 2mrc2(παZ1Z2)2 grows with the reduced mass mr and the square of the product of the two charges.

Meanwhile the number of particles with energy E falls off as exp(−E/kT). The product of the two is a narrow hump, the Gamow peak, far out in the tail of the thermal distribution: almost all reactions happen among the few particles energetic enough to have a fair chance of tunnelling, but not so energetic that there are none of them. Its area, and so the reaction rate, goes as

and that exponent makes the rate enormously sensitive to temperature. Differentiate its logarithm and the rate goes locally as Tν with ν = (τ − 2)/3. A larger barrier means a larger τ, and a steeper dependence.

Stars have two routes from hydrogen to helium. The pp chain starts with two protons, the lowest barrier there is, but its first step has to turn a proton into a neutron by the weak interaction while they are briefly in contact, which almost never happens: the reaction's strength, its S-factor, is 4.0 × 10−25 MeV barn. The CNO cycle uses carbon, nitrogen and oxygen nuclei as catalysts, and its slowest step, a proton captured by nitrogen-14, involves no weak interaction, with an S-factor of 1.66 keV barn, 4 × 1021 times larger. But nitrogen has seven times the charge of a proton, and the barrier is far higher.

At the Sun's centre the pp rate climbs as about the fourth power of temperature and the CNO rate as about the twentieth. The two energy-generation lines therefore cross, and where they cross decides which route a star uses. The Sun sits below the crossing. Summed over the standard solar model's interior, the page's two formulas give 0.6% of the Sun's energy to the CNO cycle, and MIST's model of a one-solar-mass star gives 0.8%; the Borexino experiment, which caught the cycle's neutrinos for the first time in 2020, puts the pp chain's share at about 99%. Drag the mass up and the central temperature rises with it; in the full stellar models the two routes share the work equally at about 1.5 solar masses.

The switch changes how a star is built. A rate that goes as T20 concentrates the burning in a tiny region at the very centre, pouring out more energy than radiation can carry. In the MIST models a convective core appears at about 1.2 solar masses, once the CNO cycle takes a real share, and grows with mass, while the Sun's core is still. And the steepness explains the main sequence itself. A star a little too hot at its centre burns faster, heats and expands, and cools back; one a little too cool contracts and heats. Each mass settles at the one central temperature where the burning just supplies what the surface radiates, which is why mass alone decides where a star sits.

The formulas leave out two things that the solar model includes: electron screening, in which the electrons crowding round each nucleus partly cancel its charge and lower the barrier, and the side branches of the pp chain. Summed over the model, the bare formulas give 0.79 of the Sun's luminosity, so they are right to within about a fifth, not better. The shape of the curves, which is what decides the crossover, is set by the exponent and survives both corrections.

Tracks and clusters

Put the pieces together and you have a stellar model: the structure equations, a real equation of state instead of a polytrope, the nuclear rates, and a description of how energy gets out, by radiation or convection. Step it forward in time, updating the composition as hydrogen becomes helium, and the star moves across the HR diagram. The path is its evolutionary track. The tracks below come from MIST, the MESA Isochrones and Stellar Tracks project, which ran the open-source MESA code for stars from a tenth of a solar mass to three hundred, at the Sun's composition among others.

Heavier stars are brighter than their mass alone would suggest. Their centres are hotter, and radiation leaks out of their less opaque interiors more easily, so along the main sequence luminosity rises roughly as the fourth power of mass between one and ten solar masses. The fuel only rises in proportion to the mass, so the lifetime falls steeply: a star ten times the Sun's mass burns through its core hydrogen hundreds of times faster.

Every track begins the same way. The star spends most of its life on the main sequence, drifting slowly upward as helium builds up in its core. When the core hydrogen is gone, the core contracts and heats, hydrogen keeps burning in a shell around it, and the envelope swells: the star crosses to the right and climbs the giant branch, up to thousands of times the Sun's luminosity at a surface cooler than the Sun's. A star like the Sun then ignites helium in its core, and later, after a second climb, sheds its envelope and leaves the bare core behind, which moves quickly to the far left, very hot and faint, and fades. The heaviest stars cross the top of the diagram at nearly constant luminosity to become red supergiants, and their tracks stop where the models stop, once carbon is burning in the core.

No one can watch a star do this; a lifetime is millions to billions of years. What we can watch is a star cluster, a few hundred to a few thousand stars born together from one cloud, at one time, with one composition, and at one distance. Their spread in mass means that at any moment the heavy ones have used up their hydrogen and left the main sequence while the light ones haven't started to. A line through all of them at a single age is an isochrone, and the point where the cluster's main sequence ends, its turnoff, moves down the main sequence as the cluster ages. Measuring a cluster's age is then a fitting problem: find the isochrone that matches.

Below are two clusters from Gaia DR3, with members chosen by their positions, motions and parallaxes in the Hunt and Reffert (2023) cluster catalogue. The Pleiades is 135 parsecs away and young; M67 is the same cluster whose parallaxes misbehaved in the last article, 837 parsecs away and old. Each cluster's distance modulus comes from the mean parallax of its members, the reddening from the Gaia team's own fits, and the only free parameter left is the age. Slide it, and the fit statistic beside it shows how well the isochrone passes through the bright stars, the ones above the turnoff's foot where age leaves its mark.

M67's fit is sharp. Its turnoff sits a little above the Sun's mass, its stars have begun climbing toward the giant branch in a clear subgiant bridge, and a handful of red giants and helium-burning clump stars sit above. The minimum lands at about 4.4 billion years, which the Gaia team describe as close to the Sun's age. The few stars above and to the left of the turnoff are real members too: blue stragglers, which look younger than their cluster, most likely because they gained mass from a companion. Below about G = 15 the isochrone runs a little bluer than the cluster's cooler dwarfs; the fit uses only the brighter stars, so that mismatch doesn't move the age.

The Pleiades is harder. Its turnoff should be among its brightest stars, and there are only a few of them, since heavy stars are rare, so the fit statistic has a broad, shallow minimum near 200 million years. Other methods do better for young clusters. One watches where lithium, which a fully convective star destroys once its centre is hot enough, survives among the faintest members; that and the Pleiades' eclipsing binaries give ages of about 110 to 125 million years. The gap comes partly from the models: an isochrone age inherits every assumption in the models, including how fast the stars rotate.

How stars end

A star leaves the main sequence when its core runs out of hydrogen, and what happens to it afterwards depends, again, on its mass. The core contracts until something new holds it up. For a star like the Sun that something is not heat. Squeeze electrons close enough and the Pauli exclusion principle forces them into higher and higher momentum states, whether or not the gas is hot, and the resulting degeneracy pressure can support a core that has no fuel left. Stripped of its envelope, that core is a white dwarf, the thin sequence at the bottom of the HR diagram: half a solar mass or more packed into something the size of the Earth.

For degenerate electrons that are slow compared with light, the pressure goes as ρ5/3, an n = 1.5 polytrope from the first figure, and heavier white dwarfs are smaller, R ∝ M−1/3. But packing electrons tighter raises their momentum, and once it approaches mec they become relativistic and the pressure softens toward ρ4/3, an n = 3 polytrope. An n = 3 polytrope with a fixed K has only one mass, whatever its radius. So as a white dwarf gets heavier, its radius shrinks toward zero as its mass approaches a limit, and no amount of squeezing will support more. Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar found the limit by solving the equations of figure 1 with the exact pressure of a degenerate electron gas, which is what the figure below does.

The computed limit is 1.46 solar masses for a white dwarf of carbon and oxygen, which have two nucleons for every electron. It comes out of the integration as the mass the curve approaches, and it matches the closed form Chandrasekhar derived, which the readout computes alongside. It is usually quoted as about 1.4. This calculation leaves out the electric attraction between the electrons and the nuclei, electron capture by nuclei at the highest densities, and general relativity, all of which fuller treatments include. The four measured white dwarfs sit close to the curve. Sirius B, the heaviest, holds the Sun's mass in a sphere smaller than the Earth.

The first panel says which stars end up above the limit. The white dwarfs MIST makes from stars up to 6 solar masses are between a half and one solar mass, well below it, and the carbon-oxygen cores of the heaviest tracks are far above it. The crossing, at about 9.5 solar masses in these models, is roughly where a star stops making a white dwarf and starts making something that collapses. It is only rough: the 7 and 8 solar-mass tracks stop while their cores are still growing, and stars between about 8 and 10 solar masses are the hardest to model. Above it the core goes on to burn carbon, then heavier elements, until it is iron, which yields no energy by fusing. Electrons can't hold that core up, and it falls in until it is as dense as an atomic nucleus. What happens next is still being worked out. The core may leave a neutron star and drive a supernova that blows the rest of the star apart, or it may fail to explode and become a black hole, and in one large set of models the two outcomes are interleaved in initial mass rather than divided at a single boundary; the neutron stars average about 1.4 solar masses.

A star's luminosity and colour give its radius, and its place on a track gives its mass. The next article uses those two numbers to turn the tiny dips and wobbles in a star's light into the sizes and masses of the planets around it.
