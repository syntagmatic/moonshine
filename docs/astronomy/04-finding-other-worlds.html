Finding Other Worlds

Planets are too faint to see beside their stars, so we find them in the star's own light: a shadow, a wobble, a brief flash. Each way of looking sees a different slice of what is out there.

Jupiter reflects a few billionths of the Sun's light toward a distant observer, from a point that would sit half an arcsecond from the Sun's glare as seen from ten parsecs. Pick out those billionths and you have imaged a planet, which has been done for a hundred or so young, hot, widely separated giants. For almost every one of the six thousand others we know, nobody has seen the planet's own light at all. They were found through what they do to their star.

A planet can do three things to its star's light that a telescope can measure. If its orbit is edge-on to us, it passes in front of the star once an orbit and blocks a little of the light, a transit. Whatever the orbit's tilt, the planet and star circle their common centre of mass, so the star's spectrum shifts back and forth by the Doppler effect, a radial velocity signal. And if the star happens to pass almost exactly in front of a more distant star, its gravity bends and brightens the distant star's light, and a planet orbiting it can add a brief extra flicker, microlensing.

Each of these measures the planet against the star. A transit gives the planet's size as a fraction of the star's, and a velocity gives its mass relative to the star's. The last article showed how a star's luminosity and colour fix its radius, and how its place among the evolutionary tracks fixes its mass. Those two numbers are what turn a dip and a wobble into a planet.

A shadow on the star

When a planet of radius Rp crosses a star of radius R★, it covers a fraction k2 of the star's disc, where k = Rp/R★. A Jupiter crossing the Sun covers about 1%; an Earth, 84 parts per million. The star is not a uniform disc, though. Near the edge we look through the atmosphere at a slant and see higher, cooler, dimmer layers, so a star is brightest at its centre and fades toward its limb. The coefficients depend on the star's temperature, gravity and the band in which it is observed, and they come from model atmospheres. Limb darkening makes the dip deeper than k2 when the planet crosses the middle, and rounds its floor, because the planet blocks less light near the edge than at the centre.

The rest of the dip's shape is geometry. A planet on a circular orbit of radius a crosses the disc along a straight chord a distance b R★ from the centre; b is the impact parameter. The time it takes depends on the length of the chord and on the planet's speed, 2πa/P, and Kepler's third law ties a to the period and the star's mass. Ingress and egress, the partial phases while the planet's disc straddles the limb, each take a fraction of about k/(1 − b2) of the whole transit, so a grazing transit is almost all edge and has a V-shaped bottom.

Below is HD 209458, the first star seen to be crossed by a planet, observed in 2022 for 28 days by NASA's TESS satellite. The planet was already known from the star's Doppler wobble when two groups caught it in transit in 1999. TESS saw eight transits, one every 3.52 days, and the right panel stacks them on one another. The fit finds a planet whose disc is 12% of the star's across, crossing about half-way between the star's centre and its edge. Everything that says how big the planet is lies in the star. With the method of the last article, HD 209458's Gaia colour gives a surface temperature of about 6,080 K, and its parallax and brightness, with the correction from Gaia's G band to the whole spectrum, give a luminosity of 1.66 Suns. A sphere radiating that much at that temperature has a radius of 1.16 solar radii, and 12% of that is 1.37 times Jupiter's radius. The first transit measurement of this planet, in 1999, assumed a radius of 1.1 Suns for the star and got 1.27 Jupiters for the planet. Most of that difference is the star: a planet's radius is only as good as its star's.

The planet is larger than Jupiter, and the star's Doppler wobble, which we come to next, says it has less mass, about 0.7 of Jupiter's. It orbits twenty times closer to its star than Earth is to the Sun. That orbit is also why we see it transit at all. From a random direction, a planet crosses its star only if our line of sight lies within an angle of about R★/a of the orbital plane, so the chance is about R★/a. For HD 209458 b, 12%. For the Earth seen from another star, half a percent. Most planets never transit as seen from Earth.

The shape carries one more number. For a given period, the duration and the ingress fix a/R★, and Kepler's third law, a3 ∝ M★P2, turns a/R★ and P into M★/R★3, so a transit alone measures the star's mean density. That offers a check on the stellar models: the readout compares the density the fit implies with the mass and radius used for the model.

The wobble

A star and its planet orbit their common centre of mass. The star's orbit is smaller than the planet's by the ratio of their masses, and so is its speed. Seen from a distance, only the part of that motion along our line of sight shifts the star's spectrum, and it swings back and forth once an orbit, with semi-amplitude

where i is the orbit's inclination (90° for edge-on) and e its eccentricity. Jupiter makes the Sun move at 12.5 m/s; the Earth makes it move at 9 cm/s. A velocity of 12.5 m/s shifts a spectral line by four parts in a hundred million of its wavelength. That this can be measured at all comes from averaging over thousands of lines at once, against a reference spectrum whose wavelengths are precisely known.

A circular orbit gives a pure sine. An eccentric one gives a lopsided curve: the planet whips around periastron quickly and dawdles at apoastron, and so does the star. The shape depends on which way the long axis of the orbit points relative to us.

The first planet found around a Sun-like star was found this way. In October 1995 Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz announced that 51 Pegasi, a star a little more massive than the Sun about 15 parsecs away, was moving back and forth every 4.2 days, pulled by a planet of about half Jupiter's mass. Below are 43 later velocities of 51 Peg, from the Keck telescope's HIRES spectrograph between 2006 and 2014, each good to about a metre per second. They are spread over seven and a half years at irregular times, so the first job is to find the period.

The tool for that is a periodogram. For each trial frequency, fit a sine wave (with a free offset) to the data by least squares and record how much of the variance it explains. With data this uneven, that is better than a Fourier transform, and it is what the Lomb-Scargle periodogram computes. A true signal shows up as a peak, and so do its aliases. A star can be observed only while it is up at night, and it returns to the same place in the sky once every sidereal day, so the observing times are spaced by whole sidereal days, and a signal at frequency f also fits at 1 − f and 1 + f cycles per sidereal day.

The peak at 4.231 days stands far above everything else, and the next highest, at 1.305 days, is its alias: 1/1.305 is one cycle per sidereal day (1.0027 per solar day) minus 1/4.231. Fold on the alias and the points form a sine too, only with more scatter; fold on almost anything else and they form a band. The Keplerian fit finds an orbit indistinguishable from a circle, and a semi-amplitude of 56.7 m/s with a scatter of 2.4 m/s around it, more than the measurement errors; part of the excess is the star itself, whose churning surface adds velocity noise of its own. Birkby and collaborators, combining 639 velocities from several instruments, found 54.9 m/s. The two values differ by more than either quoted error; formal errors from one dataset don't capture everything that differs between instruments and analyses.

The velocity gives the planet's mass only through the combination m sin i, because the spectrum sees only motion along our line of sight. An orbit tipped nearly face-on gives a small wobble for a large mass. So a radial velocity measures a minimum mass, and the planet could be heavier by any factor. For 51 Peg b, with a stellar mass of 1.10 Suns from models of its composition (it has about 60% more iron relative to hydrogen than the Sun; the solar-composition tracks of the last article give 0.96), m sin i is 0.48 of Jupiter's mass. It scales with the star's mass to the two-thirds power, so every 10% in the star's mass is 7% in the planet's.

For orbits oriented at random, the true mass is rarely much larger than the minimum. The orbit's normal points in a random direction, so cos i is uniform between 0 and 1, and the chance that the true mass exceeds the minimum by more than a factor x is 1 − √(1 − 1/x2): 13% for a factor of two, 2% for a factor of five. For 51 Peg b we don't have to guess. The planet's own spectrum has been detected, as faint absorption lines of water in the infrared that move at 133 km/s as the planet swings around. Momentum balance says the ratio of the two speeds is the ratio of the masses, which gives the true mass directly: 0.476 Jupiters. From their own semi-amplitude, 54.9 m/s, the same team's minimum mass is 0.466, so the true mass is 2% above it, and the orbit is tilted between 70° and 82°.

A lens made of a star

General relativity says a mass deflects light passing it, and a star exactly in line with a more distant star bends that light into a ring. The ring's angular radius is the Einstein radius,

which for a star of 0.3 solar masses (most stars are small) half-way to the centre of the Galaxy is about half a milliarcsecond, far too small for a single telescope to resolve. What can be seen is that the lensed star brightens. A source at an angular distance u Einstein radii from a point-mass lens has two images, one outside the ring and one inside, and together they magnify it by

Stars move, so the alignment lasts weeks, and the source rises and falls in a smooth, symmetric curve that depends only on the closest approach u0 and the time to cross the Einstein radius. If the lens star has a planet, the planet is a second, much smaller lens. Its effect is concentrated on caustics, small curves in the source plane where the magnification of a point source would be infinite. A source crossing one brightens for hours on top of the weeks-long event. The images show where it happens: the planet perturbs the light only if it sits near one of the images, which lie close to the Einstein ring, so microlensing is most sensitive to planets whose projected distance from their star is near the Einstein radius, a few astronomical units.

The figure computes the light curve by tracing light rays backward. For a fine grid of points in the lens plane, the lens equation gives where each ray came from in the source plane, and the magnification is the number of rays landing on the source divided by the number that would land without the lens. The source is a disc, not a point, and its size caps the spike.

With the preset, the event peaks at a magnification of about 2.9, and ten days later, as the source passes the planet's caustic, it brightens by about 15% more for about half a day. This is the model that fits the real event of 2005, followed by six telescope networks around the world in 650 measurements. The fit gives the planet-to-star mass ratio, 7.6 × 10−5, and the projected separation, 1.61 Einstein radii, directly. Masses in kilograms and separations in AU need the lens star's mass and distance, which a light curve like this one doesn't give; a model of the Galaxy's stars gave a probable host of 0.22 solar masses, and so a planet of about 5.5 Earth masses some 2.6 AU from its star, at a temperature of about 50 K. At the time, no planet of so small a mass had been found more than 0.15 AU from an ordinary star.

Shrink the source and the spike gets taller and narrower; raise the mass ratio and the caustic grows. A giant planet very near the Einstein ring has a caustic that merges with the small one at the host star's position into a single larger one, so a source passing almost straight behind the host, at high magnification, crosses it; the second preset shows such an event, with a Jupiter-to-Sun mass ratio. Move the planet inside the Einstein ring (s < 1) and its caustic splits in two, off the axis on the far side of the host (with the second preset and s = 0.8, the two small marks left of the host); a source passing behind the host still feels the central caustic, which a planet inside the ring distorts too. Every microlensing event is seen once. The stars move apart and never line up again.

Who we find, and who we miss

Each method has its own blind spots. A transit survey sees only planets whose orbits happen to be edge-on, a fraction R★/a that falls with distance from the star, and needs to see more than one transit to be sure, so its reach in period is capped by how long it stares. What it detects depends on the depth, k2, against the photometric noise averaged over the transit and over all the transits observed. Radial velocity surveys detect planets whose semi-amplitude stands above the star's own jitter and the instrument's, which, since K falls as P−1/3, favours heavy planets close in. Microlensing finds planets near the Einstein radius, a few AU from small stars far away, which for such dim stars is beyond the snow line, the distance where water freezes into ice and where giant planets are thought to form; it is nearly blind to planets close to their stars. The empty regions of this diagram have the shape of the methods. Transits crowd the upper left, where large planets on short orbits give deep, frequent dips, and their lower edge rises toward long periods along the transit limit. Radial velocity planets sit above the velocity limit and stretch to periods of decades, since a spectrograph can keep watching a star for as long as astronomers keep funding it. Microlensing planets sit in their own band at periods of years to decades, and the few imaged planets are young giants far out. There are few known Earth-sized planets on year-long orbits around Sun-like stars, and the diagram alone can't say whether that is because they are rare; it shows that they are hard to find. The transit limit passes just under the Earth, at the edge of what Kepler could do, and a velocity limit of 1 m/s passes ten times above it.

Inside the region a method can see, the gaps are real. The histogram shows the sizes of Kepler's small, close-in planets. At the default cut of 8% there is a dip at about 1.7 to 2 Earth radii, with super-Earths below it and sub-Neptunes above; loosen the cut past 15% or so and imprecise radii scatter planets into it until it fades. Fulton and collaborators found the gap in 2017 once the California-Kepler Survey had measured the host stars precisely: after correcting for which planets Kepler could detect, a factor of two or more fewer planets per star between 1.5 and 2.0 Earth radii (the shaded band) than on either side. The reading they gave it is that close-in planets smaller than Neptune are rocky cores of up to about 1.5 Earth radii, wrapped in varying amounts of light gas that set their size; a core that has kept almost none sits below the gap.

Every number in that diagram rests on a chain of earlier ones. A planet's radius is a fraction of its star's, which came from the star's temperature and luminosity, which came from its colour, brightness and parallax. A planet's mass is a fraction of its star's, which came from where the star sits on an evolutionary track, which is the physics of a ball of gas in balance. The parallax is geometry on Earth's orbit, and the Earth's orbit is where this series started, with the Sun moving against the stars once a year.
