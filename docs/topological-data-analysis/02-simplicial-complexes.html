Simplicial Complexes as Combinatorial Shapes

Part 2 of 3. The Rips complex from explainer 1 lived in the plane. The algebra we build next needs only the combinatorics (which subsets of vertices form simplices), not the coordinates.

In the previous explainer, we built the Rips complex from points in the plane. The construction depended on geometry: we needed coordinates to compute distances. But the topological invariants we want to extract (connected components, loops, voids) depend only on which simplices are present, not on where they sit in space. The same abstract combinatorial structure can be drawn in many different ways, and homology will give the same answer for all of them.

This observation leads us to the concept of an abstract simplicial complex: a purely combinatorial object defined by a collection of subsets (simplices) closed under taking subsets (faces).

Definition

An abstract simplicial complex on a vertex set V is a collection K of non-empty finite subsets of V such that:

Each element σ ∈ K is called a simplex. If |σ| = k + 1, we say σ has dimension k. A 0-simplex is a vertex, a 1-simplex is an edge, a 2-simplex is a triangle, a 3-simplex is a tetrahedron. A subset τ ⊆ σ with τ ∈ K is called a face of σ.

The closure condition says: if a triangle {0, 1, 2} is in K, then all its edges {0,1}, {1,2}, {0,2} and all its vertices {0}, {1}, {2} must also be in K. You cannot have a solid triangle without its boundary.

The f-vector and Euler characteristic

The f-vector of a complex counts simplices by dimension: f0 is the number of vertices, f1 the number of edges, f2 the number of triangles, and so on. The Euler characteristic is the alternating sum:

Euler observed in 1758 that every convex polyhedron satisfies V − E + F = 2, where V, E, F are vertices, edges, and faces. The Euler characteristic generalises this to any simplicial complex in any dimension. It is a topological invariant: it depends only on the topology, not the geometry.

The Euler characteristic is the simplest topological invariant. It is a single integer computed from simplex counts, yet it constrains the topology: a sphere has χ = 2, a torus has χ = 0, and a genus-g surface has χ = 2 − 2g. But it is coarse; it cannot distinguish a sphere from two disjoint projective planes (both χ = 2). Homology, which we meet next, is the refinement.

The face lattice (Hasse diagram)

The Hasse diagram (or face lattice) of a simplicial complex arranges all simplices in layers by dimension, with edges connecting each simplex to its immediate faces. It encodes the full combinatorial structure without any geometry.

Geometric realisation is not unique

Any abstract simplicial complex can be geometrically realised by embedding its vertices as points in some Euclidean space and drawing the simplices as convex hulls. But the realisation is not unique: you can move the vertices around, stretch, rotate, or deform the picture, and the abstract complex, and therefore its topology, stays the same.

This is the central lesson of this explainer: topology is combinatorial, not geometric. The Rips complex in explainer 1 was geometric (it depended on distances), but once it is built, we can forget the coordinates and work purely with the combinatorial data. Homology is a functor on the combinatorial structure.

Topology depends on combinatorics, not geometry. Two geometric realisations of the same abstract complex have the same homology, the same Betti numbers, the same Euler characteristic. Moving vertices around in the plane changes the picture but not the topology. This is why we can build a complex from data (using geometry) and then analyse it using algebra (using only combinatorics).

Dimension and the skeleton

The dimension of a simplicial complex is the maximum dimension of any of its simplices. The k-skeleton is the subcomplex consisting of all simplices of dimension ≤ k. The 0-skeleton is the vertex set; the 1-skeleton is the underlying graph; the 2-skeleton includes all triangles.

For the Rips complex, the 1-skeleton already determines the entire complex (because it is a clique complex). For a general simplicial complex, this is not true: you can have three mutually adjacent vertices without a filled-in triangle.

Takeaways

  • An abstract simplicial complex is a collection of subsets closed under taking faces, a purely combinatorial object.
  • The f-vector counts simplices by dimension; the Euler characteristic is the alternating sum.
  • The Hasse diagram encodes the full face relation without coordinates.
  • Topological invariants depend on combinatorics, not geometry. Once we build the complex, we can forget the embedding.
