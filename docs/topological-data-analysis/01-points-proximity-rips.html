Points, Proximity, and the Rips Complex

Part 1 of 3. A scatter plot becomes a shape: draw a ball around each point, connect overlapping balls, and watch structure emerge as the radius grows.

Look at the scatter plot below. A thousand points scattered in the plane show density, but they don't easily tell a computer if the data forms a loop, encloses a void, or breaks into clusters. To answer these topological questions, we need to build a shape out of the points.

The structure built here is called a Vietoris-Rips complex. Because this only requires measuring distances between pairs of points, it works on any data—gene expression profiles, word embeddings, or sensor networks.

What the parameter ε controls

The radius dial is a resolution knob. At zero, every point is isolated. Increase the radius, and small, tight clusters fill in first. Push it further, and broad global features emerge. The interesting information lives in the change. A loop that survives for a long time as the radius grows is a genuine feature; one that flickers in and out instantly is just noise.

Notice how there is no need to check if three points overlap perfectly. If every pair of points in a trio is close enough, a triangle automatically fills in. This makes the Rips complex a clique complex—it's completely determined by its edges. This trick is what makes the math computable on thousands of points.

A first look at structure

On the annulus preset, small ε shows isolated points or small clusters. At a middle range of ε, a ring of edges appears, and the complex captures the loop in the annulus. Increase ε further and the ring fills in with triangles, killing the hole. The annulus had one genuine topological feature (a loop), and it was visible for exactly the range of ε where edges traced around the ring but triangles had not yet filled the interior.

On the clusters preset, small ε shows three separate clusters. As ε grows, each cluster becomes a connected blob, and eventually the blobs merge. The topological feature here is disconnectedness: the complex has three connected components that gradually merge into one. The number of connected components is a topological invariant called β0 (the zeroth Betti number).

Why not just use a graph?

If edges determine the Rips complex, why bother with triangles and tetrahedra? Because homology, the algebraic machinery that counts holes, needs the full simplicial complex. A loop of edges might bound a filled-in region or might enclose a hole; the graph alone cannot tell. The triangles and higher simplices resolve the ambiguity.

Edges detect adjacency; higher simplices detect holes. A graph tells you points are connected in a ring, but not whether the ring encloses a hole or is filled in. Homology requires the full simplicial complex.

The combinatorial explosion

There is a computational cost. A set of n points can generate up to 2n simplices (one for every non-empty subset). In practice the Rips complex at moderate ε has far fewer, but the growth is still rapid: the number of triangles can be cubic in the number of edges, and the number of tetrahedra quartic. Computing the full Rips complex for more than a few hundred points at moderate ε is already expensive; practical TDA software uses clever algorithms that compute persistent homology without ever materialising the full complex.

The Rips complex is the bridge between geometry (point positions, pairwise distances) and topology (connected components, loops, voids).

Takeaways

  • Build shapes from distances: By drawing connections between nearby points, turn a disconnected scatter plot into a solid geometric structure (a simplicial complex).
  • Edges do all the work: The Rips complex is completely determined by its edges. If the edges form a complete network, the higher-dimensional shapes fill in automatically.
  • Watch for persistence: The most meaningful features of your data are the loops and clusters that survive across a wide range of radii, rather than the ones that flicker in and out instantly.
