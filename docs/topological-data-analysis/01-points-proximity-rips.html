Points, Proximity, and the Rips Complex

Part 1 of 3. A scatter plot becomes a shape: draw a ball around each point, connect overlapping balls, and watch structure emerge as the radius grows.

Suppose you have data, say a thousand points in the plane sampled from some unknown shape. The raw scatter plot shows you where points are dense and where they are sparse, but it does not tell you whether the data wraps around a loop, encloses a void, or breaks into clusters. Those are topological questions, and answering them requires turning the point cloud into a combinatorial object whose topology we can compute.

The simplest construction that does this is the Vietoris-Rips complex. Fix a radius parameter ε > 0. Draw a ball of radius ε around each point. Whenever two balls overlap (equivalently, whenever two points are within distance 2ε of each other) draw an edge. Whenever three points are mutually within distance 2ε, fill in a triangle. Whenever four points are mutually close, fill in a tetrahedron. The result is a simplicial complex (a shape built from vertices, edges, triangles, and their higher-dimensional cousins) whose topology reflects the geometry of the sample at scale ε.

This is a pure distance condition. No coordinates are needed beyond the ability to measure pairwise distances, which is why TDA works on any metric space: gene expression profiles, persistence images, word embeddings, shapes in a sensor network.

Growing the complex

The magic is in what happens when ε changes. At ε = 0, every point is isolated: no edges, no triangles, just a discrete cloud. As ε increases, edges appear between nearby points, triangles fill in between nearby triples, and the complex grows. At some intermediate ε, the complex may capture genuine structure: a loop of edges traces a hole in the data, a shell of triangles encloses a void. At very large ε, everything is connected to everything else: the complex collapses into a single contractible blob.

The question that defines topological data analysis is: which features of the complex persist across a wide range of ε, and which are fleeting? We will formalise that question in Act II. For now, let's build the intuition.

What the parameter ε controls

Think of ε as a resolution dial. At fine resolution (small ε), you see only the most tightly clustered structure. At coarse resolution (large ε), you see only the broadest features. Neither extreme is "right"; the interesting information lives in the change as ε sweeps. A loop that appears at ε = 0.3 and persists until ε = 0.8 is a more credible topological feature than one that flickers into and out of existence at ε = 0.31.

The Rips complex is defined by a single inequality: vertices i and j are connected whenever d(i, j) ≤ 2ε. A k-simplex exists whenever all pairwise distances among its k + 1 vertices are ≤ 2ε, equivalently whenever the k + 1 points form a clique in the graph of edges. This makes the Rips complex a clique complex (or flag complex): it is completely determined by its 1-skeleton.

The Rips complex is a clique complex. You only need pairwise distances to build it. Once you know the edges (which pairs of points are within 2ε), the higher simplices are forced: every clique of edges fills in. This makes computation feasible: you never need to check a condition on three or more points simultaneously, only on pairs.

A first look at structure

On the annulus preset, small ε shows isolated points or small clusters. At a middle range of ε, a ring of edges appears, and the complex captures the loop in the annulus. Increase ε further and the ring fills in with triangles, killing the hole. The annulus had one genuine topological feature (a loop), and it was visible for exactly the range of ε where edges traced around the ring but triangles had not yet filled the interior.

On the clusters preset, small ε shows three separate clusters. As ε grows, each cluster becomes a connected blob, and eventually the blobs merge. The topological feature here is disconnectedness: the complex has three connected components that gradually merge into one. The number of connected components is a topological invariant called β0 (the zeroth Betti number).

Why not just use a graph?

If edges are enough to determine the Rips complex, why bother with triangles and tetrahedra? Because homology, the algebraic machinery that counts holes, needs the full simplicial complex to work. A loop of edges might bound a filled-in region (in which case there is no hole) or it might not. You cannot tell from the graph alone whether a cycle is the boundary of something. The triangles and higher simplices are exactly the information that resolves this ambiguity.

Edges detect adjacency; higher simplices detect holes. A graph can tell you that points are connected in a ring, but it cannot tell you whether the ring encloses a hole or is filled in. That distinction is the domain of homology, which requires the full simplicial complex.

The combinatorial explosion

There is a computational cost. A set of n points can generate up to 2n simplices (one for every non-empty subset). In practice the Rips complex at moderate ε has far fewer, but the growth is still rapid: the number of triangles can be cubic in the number of edges, and the number of tetrahedra quartic. Computing the full Rips complex for more than a few hundred points at moderate ε is already expensive; practical TDA software uses clever algorithms that compute persistent homology without ever materialising the full complex.

For now, the important conceptual point is that the Rips complex is the bridge between geometry (point positions, pairwise distances) and topology (connected components, loops, voids). Everything in this series flows from this bridge.

Takeaways

  • A point cloud becomes a simplicial complex via the Vietoris-Rips construction: connect every pair within distance 2ε, fill in every clique.
  • The Rips complex is a clique complex, fully determined by its edges.
  • Varying ε produces a nested family of complexes (a filtration) in which topological features are born and die.
  • The interesting question is which features persist across a wide range of ε.
