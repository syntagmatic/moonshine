Root Systems as Types

A lattice of six points in the plane, and a group of six reflections that permute them. Four simulations from earlier in the series turn out to be living on this same lattice under this same group. The state space has geometry; here it is.

§1 · Cold viz

A lattice and its group

Every explainer in the series has been some version of a lattice together with a group that acts on it. The quadtree in #1. The automorphism group of the contact graph in #2. The Z₃ cycle of rock-paper-scissors species in #4. The contact-process lattice's directed-percolation class in #4 again. The spatial, regular-ring, and scale-free topologies of #6. The 1-D traffic lattice coarse-graining toward an RG fixed point in #5. The C₆ᵥ point group required to recover Navier–Stokes in #9. The continuous SO(2) of an equivariant GA in #10. The five Bravais classes of #11. Now, at the end, the smallest non-trivial example where the lattice is defined by the group: two simple roots α₁ and α₂ at 120° to each other, six points total arranged as a hexagon, and the Weyl group — six reflections — that closes the set under its action. For A₂, that group has six elements. Pick one and watch.

Each button applies one element of the Weyl group to all six roots simultaneously. The result is always another permutation of the same six labelled points — the set is closed under the group action.
§2 · Name the types

Six roots, six group elements, one closed algebra

The roots are literally a finite sum type with six inhabitants. The group is another. The action is a function between them — and because both are finite, it's a total, statically enumerable, finite-case function.

data A2Root = A1 -- α₁ | A2 -- α₂ | AB -- α₁ + α₂ | NegA1 -- -α₁ | NegA2 -- -α₂ | NegAB -- -(α₁ + α₂) data Weyl -- Weyl(A₂) ≅ S₃ = WId | S1 | S2 | S1S2 | S2S1 | S1S2S1 act :: Weyl -> A2Root -> A2Root -- total; no runtime failure possible

Both types are finite; their product is 6 × 6 = 36 cases, all enumerable. A compiler can verify act is total by exhaustive matching. More: because A₂Root is closed under the Weyl action, any function f :: A2Root -> b with f . act w = f for all w is automatically Weyl-invariant — a type-level statement about physical symmetry.

The stronger version is equivariance. When a simulation's step :: State -> State shares the same symmetry, the claim the type system can check is:

-- Equivariance: step commutes with the group action. -- For all w : Weyl and all x : State, -- step (apply_sym w x) = apply_sym w (step x) equivariant :: (w : Weyl) -> (x : State) -> step (apply_sym w x) ≡ apply_sym w (step x)

Read the two sides as a choice of order: reflect the world first and then step, or step first and then reflect. The equation says: the answer is the same. This is the property a proof assistant like Lean or Coq mechanically verifies once — and thereafter every future run of the simulation is protected, across every trajectory and every timestep, without a single test case. The typing judgment Γ ⊢ step : Equivariant is the whole argument of the series compressed into one line.

§4 · What it lets you say

Four simulations from this series share this lattice

Here is the claim, not as an observation but as a demonstration. Each tile below is a miniature revisiting one earlier explainer — respecialized so the lattice it runs on is the A₂ root system and the group acting on that lattice is the Weyl group. The hex overlay on each is the same hex we've been staring at since §1.

Each tile's caption names a specific physical feature the lattice-and-group pairing protects: L4/L5 stability, three-species coexistence, six-fold flock isotropy, a Turing-pattern geometry not aligned to the memory layout. A non-equivariant implementation is one whose step function does not commute with a Weyl reflection; its pathology is whichever feature the tile's caption mentions. The type system's job is to make the commuting square step · apply_sym ≡ apply_sym · step compulsory; the physics then falls out.

§5a · Three equal masses (#7)

Lagrange's triangular equilibrium — S₃ permuting the bodies
Three equal-mass bodies rotate about their common centre. Permuting which body is "first" is S₃ = Weyl(A₂). Lagrange's equilateral solution and its L4/L5 Trojan points live at the fixed points of this action — and drift off them is exactly what a non-equivariant integrator injects, one rounding error at a time.

§5b · Three-species predator-prey (#4)

Rock-paper-scissors cycle — S₃ permuting species
The phase simplex has an S₃ action; the rock-paper-scissors cycle A→B→C→A is its Z₃ subgroup. Relabel the species and the dynamics commute. Unequal update ordering on a lattice quietly biases one species — the spiral waves collapse and the ecosystem wrongly reports itself extinct. Equivariance is what rules the bias out.

§5c · Hex-neighbourhood boids (#2)

Six neighbour directions = six A₂ roots
On a hex lattice each boid sees six neighbours: the six roots of A₂. A square grid silently collapses continuous SO(2) rotation into an unbalanced subgroup, and flocks drift toward the four axes; the hex lattice keeps a six-fold Weyl action that the rule step actually respects.

§5d · Hex-stencil reaction-diffusion (#9)

Seven-point stencil invariant under Weyl(A₂)
A centre weight plus six equal outer weights — the only pattern that survives every Weyl reflection. A 5-point square stencil makes Turing stripes line up with the grid axes; the hex stencil commutes with the lattice symmetry, so the pattern is the reaction-diffusion's, not the memory layout's.
Four unrelated-looking simulations. One underlying group. This is the thesis in its strongest form: the state space of a well-typed simulation is a mathematical object, and when the physics is right, the object has a name.
§5 · Carry-forward

The series ends here, but the geometry does not. A₂ is the smallest non-trivial root system. Bigger ones exist: A₃, B₃, D₄, F₄, E₆, E₇, and the largest that occurs in classical Lie theory — E₈, with 240 roots in eight dimensions. Every one can be the symmetry group of a simulation, and every one can be named as a finite type whose Weyl group acts by reflection.

E₈ is where the naive encoding breaks. Its Weyl group has 696,729,600 elements — no sum type is going to enumerate that. The proof assistants that verify these simulations (Lean, Coq, Agda — all built on the Calculus of Inductive Constructions) therefore reason about classes rather than elements: F-conjugacy classes lifted through the short exact sequence 1 → G° → G → C → 1, the same structural trick the series' sum types use in miniature. The commuting diagram in §2 scales, the finite-case listing does not — and that's the move that makes formal verification feasible for the big groups.

Two bars per classical root system. Dark violet: |W|, the number of Weyl-group elements a sum-type enumeration would need. Teal: the number of conjugacy classes — the count of orbits any equivariance proof actually has to reason about. On a log scale the violet bars grow through eight orders of magnitude; the teal bars stay in a narrow band. That gap is why W(E₈)'s nearly 700-million elements don't blow up the proof.

The final image of this series is E₈'s shadow in the Coxeter plane. It is the geometry of the 240-root type rendered in 2D.
