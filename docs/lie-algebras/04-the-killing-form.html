The Cartan–Killing Form

Part 4 of 15. A canonical bilinear form κ(x, y) = tr(adx ady) on every Lie algebra. On a semisimple algebra the form is non-degenerate; on the real span of the roots it is positive definite. This is the pivot where the classification leaves algebra for Euclidean geometry.

At the end of the last explainer we had the root space decomposition 𝔤 = 𝔥 ⊕ ⊕α ∈ Δ 𝔤α, with roots α as abstract linear functionals on the Cartan subalgebra 𝔥. That is the structure we want to classify. But classifying sets of linear functionals is still an abstract linear-algebra problem, we do not yet have a geometry on 𝔥* that would tell us when two roots are "close," or what angle they form, or whether they are "long" or "short."

This explainer supplies that geometry. We will build, from nothing more than the bracket, a canonical bilinear form on 𝔤 itself, the Killing form, show that it descends to a well-defined inner product on the Cartan subalgebra, transport that inner product to 𝔥* via the canonical isomorphism 𝔥 ≅ 𝔥*, and verify that the resulting form is strictly positive definite on the real span of the roots. At that point the roots stop being abstract functionals and become genuine vectors in a real Euclidean space 𝔼. From here to the end of Act II, the classification is a problem in the geometry of 𝔼.

The Killing form

Given a Lie algebra 𝔤, every element x ∈ 𝔤 produces a linear operator adx : 𝔤 → 𝔤. Composing two such operators gives another linear operator, adx ∘ ady, whose trace is a number, a coordinate-independent scalar extracted from the composition. That trace, as a function of the two inputs x and y, is the Killing form:

It is a bilinear form κ : 𝔤 × 𝔤 → ℂ (or ℝ, if 𝔤 is a real Lie algebra), symmetric because the trace is cyclic: tr(AB) = tr(BA). It has one additional property that makes it uniquely useful for Lie-theoretic calculations, the ad-invariance property:

That positivity will be our toehold for proving positive-definiteness on the real span of the roots in general.

Cartan's criterion

Non-degeneracy of the Killing form is not just a nice property; it is the defining property of semisimple Lie algebras. The theorem is due to Cartan, and we take it as a black box.

Cartan's criterion. A finite-dimensional Lie algebra 𝔤 over a field of characteristic zero is semisimple if and only if its Killing form is non-degenerate: for every non-zero x ∈ 𝔤 there is some y ∈ 𝔤 with κ(x, y) ≠ 0. Equivalently, the Gram matrix of κ in any basis has non-zero determinant.

For sln, we have verified non-degeneracy by computation (for sl2 directly, for sln via κ(X, Y) = 2n tr(XY)). For an abstractly specified semisimple algebra, non-degeneracy is how one knows it is semisimple. The criterion is the bridge between "the bracket has no proper non-trivial ideals" and "we have a non-degenerate bilinear form to work with."

Cartan's criterion has a remarkable consequence: because the Killing form is non-degenerate on all of 𝔤, it remains non-degenerate when restricted to certain subspaces, in particular, to the Cartan subalgebra 𝔥. The restriction κ|𝔥 × 𝔥 is a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form on 𝔥, which lets us build an inner-product structure on 𝔥 that we can push forward to 𝔥*. That push-forward is where the roots become vectors.

Positive-definiteness on the real span

Here is the fact that anchors the whole classification.

Theorem. The induced inner product (·, ·) on the real linear span of the roots is strictly positive definite: for any non-zero ξ = Σ cα α in the real span of Δ, we have (ξ, ξ) > 0.

The proof, which we sketch rather than give, uses three ingredients. First, the Killing form of a simple Lie algebra, restricted to its Cartan, takes integer values on a specific integer lattice spanned by the co-roots. Second, for any root α, the value κ(tα, tα) equals a positive integer (essentially the number of roots in the "α-string" weighted by their α-components, a non-negative count). Third, the Gram matrix of any set of linearly independent roots is therefore symmetric with positive diagonal and interlaced integer off-diagonal entries, and a linear-algebra lemma shows that such a matrix is positive definite.

What this means, operationally, is: the root system Δ lives inside a real vector space

of real dimension equal to the rank of 𝔤, and that real vector space carries an honest Euclidean inner product. Distances between roots, angles between roots, lengths of roots, all of these are well-defined and obey the usual axioms of Euclidean geometry. From this moment on, every classification argument becomes a statement about the geometry of a finite configuration of vectors in 𝔼.

What the shift to geometry buys us

Once we are doing Euclidean geometry on Δ ⊂ 𝔼, a lot of machinery becomes available that would have been painful to derive directly from the Lie bracket. Here are three consequences we will cash in on in the next explainer and throughout Act II:

  • Angles and lengths. Every root has a squared length (α, α) > 0, and every pair of roots has a well-defined angle via the standard cosine formula. In the classification, only a handful of angles are allowed, 90°, 120°, 135°, 150°, and the discreteness of the allowed set is what makes the classification tractable.
  • Reflections. Each root α defines a reflection of 𝔼 across the hyperplane perpendicular to α. Because these reflections send roots to roots (this is the sl2 fact from the next explainer), they generate a finite reflection group, the Weyl group, and that group is the second main actor of the classification story.
  • Finite configurations of vectors. The root system Δ is now a finite, symmetric-about-the-origin configuration of vectors in 𝔼, and we can ask the combinatorial question: which such configurations are allowed? The answer, it turns out, is spectacularly restrictive.

The pivot. Up to now we have been doing algebra: Lie brackets, ideals, semisimplicity, adjoint representations. From the next explainer forward we are doing Euclidean geometry: angles, hyperplanes, reflections, finite configurations of vectors. The bridge is κ, and the positive-definiteness theorem above is the reason the bridge holds up. The rest of the series is what we can cross over to the other side.

What we take with us

  • Killing form. κ(x, y) = tr(adx ady), a canonical symmetric bilinear form on any Lie algebra. It is ad-invariant: κ([z, x], y) + κ(x, [z, y]) = 0 for all z ∈ 𝔤.
  • Cartan's criterion. A finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a characteristic-zero field is semisimple if and only if its Killing form is non-degenerate. We accept this as a black box.
  • Restriction and transport. κ stays non-degenerate when restricted to the Cartan subalgebra 𝔥, and the resulting iso 𝔥 ≅ 𝔥* pushes the form forward to a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form (·, ·) on 𝔥*.
  • Real positive-definiteness. The real linear span 𝔼 = spanℝ Δ of the roots inside 𝔥* is a real vector space of dimension equal to the rank of 𝔤, on which (·, ·) is strictly positive definite. This turns Δ into an honest Euclidean configuration.
  • The geometry handoff. From this point on, classification arguments use Euclidean geometry, angles, hyperplanes, reflections, and finite configurations of vectors, rather than algebra. The algebra is still the source of every restriction we impose, but it gets imposed via geometric constraints on the root system.

Next we use the sl2 triples embedded in 𝔤 to extract the first genuinely restrictive constraint on Δ: that Weyl reflections send roots to roots, with integer coefficients that force the pairing ⟨α, β⟩ := 2(α, β)/(β, β) to be an integer for every pair of roots. That integrality is the crystallographic restriction, and it is what makes the set of allowed pairwise angles discrete.
