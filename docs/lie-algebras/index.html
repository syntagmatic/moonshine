The Classification of Simple Lie Algebras

Cartan–Killing's theorem, one Dynkin diagram at a time. Fifteen interactive explainers from the definition of a Lie algebra to the full list, four classical families A, B, C, D and five exceptions E6, E7, E8, F4, G2, and why nothing else exists.

In 1888, Wilhelm Killing published a rough classification of the finite-dimensional simple Lie algebras over ℂ. Six years later Élie Cartan published a corrected and rigorously proved version. Half a century after that, Eugene Dynkin reframed the entire result as a short list of small diagrams, nodes connected by single, double, or triple edges, which you can draw on the back of a napkin. The Cartan–Killing classification theorem says those diagrams are everything.

More precisely, it says: every finite-dimensional simple Lie algebra over the complex numbers is isomorphic to exactly one entry on a short list. Four of the entries are infinite families indexed by rank, An, Bn, Cn, Dn, corresponding to sln+1, so(2n+1), sp(2n), so(2n). The remaining five are isolated singular exceptions that live at ranks 2, 4, 6, 7, and 8: G2, F4, E6, E7, and E8. No tenth type exists, at any rank, over any extension, up to isomorphism.

This series tells the classification story as a three-act arc. Act I builds the machinery, what a Lie algebra is, what a Cartan subalgebra is, how the root space decomposition works, how the Killing form turns algebra into Euclidean geometry, and how Weyl reflections fall out of embedded sl2 triples. Act II runs the classification: the crystallographic restriction on root-system angles, Cartan matrices and Dynkin diagrams, the four classical families as worked examples, and then the graph-pruning argument that produces the three exceptional E-series solutions. Act III tours the five exceptions one at a time, G2 and the octonions, F4 and the exceptional Jordan algebra, E6 and the Cayley plane, E7 and E8, and the Freudenthal magic square that unites all five through the four real normed division algebras ℝ, ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆.

The companion E8 lattice series handles E8 in full geometric detail, and the octonions series builds the non-associative structure that underlies G2, F4, E6, E7, and E8. The three series are independent entry points into the same body of mathematics; this one takes the algebraic route.

The nine families

At the end of the classification, the full list consists of these nine types. The first four are infinite families parameterised by rank; the last five are singular.
