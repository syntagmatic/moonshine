The Four Classical Families

Part 8 of 15. A, B, C, D. Before we prove the classification terminates, we instantiate the Dynkin machinery on the four infinite families we can build by hand, special linear, odd and even orthogonal, and symplectic Lie algebras. They give us the raw material that the termination proof in the next two explainers will rule in.

We have all the vocabulary now, Cartan subalgebras, root space decompositions, Killing forms, Weyl groups, crystallographic restrictions, simple roots, Cartan matrices, Dynkin diagrams, and the classification problem has been reduced to: which connected Dynkin diagrams correspond to positive-definite Cartan matrices? The next two explainers (09 and 10) answer that question with a graph-theoretic argument and a Diophantine inequality. But before we run that argument, it is worth instantiating the Dynkin machinery on four families of matrix Lie algebras that we already know exist, so that we have a concrete library of examples on hand.

The four families are An, Bn, Cn, Dn, and together they correspond to four classical matrix algebras: sln+1(ℂ), so(2n+1, ℂ), sp(2n, ℂ), and so(2n, ℂ). Each has a Cartan subalgebra that is easy to describe (diagonal matrices, in each case), a root system that is easy to list (combinations of coordinate functionals), and a Dynkin diagram shape that is distinctive. Once we have them in hand, the termination proof in explainers 9 and 10 will show that only these four shapes and three exceptional shapes (E6, E7, E8) can occur, plus the two non-simply-laced outliers F4 and G2. Everything else is ruled out.

An: the special linear family

sln+1(ℂ), the Lie algebra of (n+1) × (n+1) complex matrices with trace zero, is the most familiar simple Lie algebra. Bn: odd-dimensional special orthogonal

so(2n+1, ℂ), the Lie algebra of (2n+1) × (2n+1) skew-symmetric complex matrices, has dimension n(2n+1). Its Cartan subalgebra is a particular n-dimensional subspace of block-diagonal skew-symmetric matrices, in suitable coordinates, the span of n commuting rotation generators. Its root system is

consisting of 2n(n − 1) "long" roots (the first family, squared length 2 relative to the standard inner product) and 2n "short" roots (the second family, squared length 1). Choosing simple roots αi = εi − εi+1 for i < n and αn = εn gives a Cartan matrix that matches An's pattern everywhere except in the (n−1, n) and (n, n−1) entries, which become −2 and −1 respectively. The corresponding Dynkin diagram is a linear chain of n nodes with the last edge doubled and an arrow pointing toward the end (from long αn−1 to short αn).

Bn starts at n = 2, because B1 would be so(3, ℂ), a 3-dimensional algebra which coincides with sl2(ℂ) = A1 under the isomorphism so(3) ≅ sl2. So B1 = A1, and the classification lists B starting at rank 2.

Cn: the symplectic family

sp(2n, ℂ), the symplectic Lie algebra, is the subspace of gl(2n, ℂ) preserving a standard non-degenerate alternating bilinear form J. It has dimension n(2n+1), the same as so(2n+1, ℂ), so Bn and Cn have the same dimension but are nonetheless non-isomorphic for n ≥ 3 (they differ as abstract Lie algebras, and their root systems, while related by a duality, are not literally the same). Its Cartan subalgebra is the space of diagonal matrices of a particular block shape, and its roots are

2n(n − 1) "short" roots (squared length 2) and 2n "long" roots (squared length 4). The simple roots are αi = εi − εi+1 for i < n and αn = 2εn. The resulting Dynkin diagram is Bn's with the arrow reversed: a chain of n nodes, double edge at the end, but the arrow now points from the long αn back into the chain.

Cn starts at n = 3. C1 would be sp(2) = sl2 = A1. C2 is isomorphic to B2 as abstract Lie algebras, their Dynkin diagrams have the arrow pointing in different directions, but the underlying algebras agree (so(5) ≅ sp(4)). So C2 does not give a new family member; the first genuinely new C is C3 = sp(6).

Dn: even-dimensional special orthogonal

so(2n, ℂ), the even-dimensional orthogonal Lie algebra, has dimension n(2n − 1). Its root system is

the 2n(n − 1) roots ±εi ± εj, all of squared length 2. Unlike Bn, there are no short roots: Dn is simply-laced. The simple roots are αi = εi − εi+1 for i < n and αn = εn−1 + εn. The resulting Dynkin diagram has a main chain with a fork at the end: n − 1 nodes in a row, plus one extra node branching off the node αn−2 (the second-to-last node of the main chain).

Dn starts at n = 4. D1 is 1-dimensional (abelian, not simple). D2 splits as so(4) ≅ sl(2) ⊕ sl(2), a non-simple but semisimple decomposition into two commuting A1 factors. D3 is so(6) which is isomorphic to sl(4) = A3, so it is not a new entry on the classification list either. The first genuinely new D is D4 = so(8), which is famous for its triality: its Dynkin diagram has three arms of length 1 at a central node, and the symmetry group of the diagram is the full symmetric group S3, a feature unique to D4.

Common features

All four classical families share a few structural features that matter for the classification:

  • Diagonal Cartan subalgebra. In each family, the Cartan is a space of diagonal (or block-diagonal) matrices. The rank n equals the dimension of this diagonal subalgebra, and the individual Cartan elements are indexed by coordinate functionals εi.
  • Coordinate-difference roots. The roots are linear combinations of coordinate functionals with small integer coefficients: εi − εj, ±εi ± εj, ±εi, or ±2εi, depending on the family. The matrix realisation of the associated root space is a specific matrix unit Eij or a small linear combination thereof.
  • Dynkin diagrams are trees. An and Bn and Cn are linear chains (trees with a single path). Dn is a tree with one Y-shaped branch. None of them has a cycle, and none has a node of valency more than 3. Tree-ness and bounded valency are necessary conditions for a Dynkin diagram, and the next explainer proves both of them from scratch.
  • At most one multiple edge. An and Dn have no multiple edges. Bn and Cn each have exactly one double edge, always at the end of the chain. This pattern is also forced by the classification, any Dynkin diagram of a simple Lie algebra has at most one multiple edge, and in the non-simply-laced classical cases it is always a double edge at the "non-symmetric" end.

The raw material. An, Bn, Cn, Dn are the four "obvious" Dynkin-diagram shapes we get from well-known matrix constructions. The next two explainers will show that only six other shapes can exist: five singular exceptions G2, F4, E6, E7, E8, plus the trivial case A1. That's the content of the classification.

What we take with us

  • An: sln+1(ℂ). Diagonal Cartan, n(n+1) roots of form εi − εj. Dynkin = linear chain, n nodes, single edges.
  • Bn: so(2n+1, ℂ). Block-diagonal Cartan, 2n² roots (long and short). Dynkin = chain of n nodes with double edge at the end, arrow outward.
  • Cn: sp(2n, ℂ). Block Cartan, 2n² roots (short and long, swapped from B). Dynkin = chain of n nodes with double edge at the end, arrow inward.
  • Dn: so(2n, ℂ). Block Cartan, 2n(n − 1) roots (all the same length). Dynkin = main chain of n − 1 nodes plus a fork at αn−2.
  • Low-rank coincidences. B1 = A1, C1 = A1, C2 ≅ B2, D1 abelian, D2 ≅ A1 ⊕ A1 (reducible), D3 ≅ A3. These force the starting ranks n ≥ 1, 2, 3, 4 for the four families.
  • Handoff. The four classical shapes, linear chain, chain with double edge at end (two variants), branched tree, are the full list of tree shapes that survive the termination proof in the simply-laced and "one-multiple-edge" cases. The remaining question for explainer 9 is whether any other tree shape can exist, which turns out to be "yes, three of them, all with a specific kind of branch, and those are E6, E7, E8."
