The Full Classification and the Magic Square

Part 15 of 15. Nine families, four infinite classical A, B, C, D plus five singular exceptions G₂, F₄, E₆, E₇, E₈, and no others. The exceptions are not random: the Freudenthal magic square arranges F₄, E₆, E₇, E₈ into the octonion row and column of a 4×4 table indexed by the normed division algebras ℝ, ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆. The classification exists because the octonions exist.

The Cartan–Killing classification is now complete. We have every simple Lie algebra over ℂ, organised by rank and diagram shape, each with a construction and a characteristic signature. This capstone explainer steps back and takes in the whole list, places the five exceptions on a surprisingly clean combinatorial grid called the Freudenthal magic square, and closes with two pointers forward, one into the infinite-dimensional territory of affine Kac-Moody algebras, the other into the octonions-and-E8 sibling series that make this framework look less like a taxonomy and more like a toolkit.

The Freudenthal magic square

The five exceptional algebras are not a random list. They are all built from the same ingredient, the octonions, and Freudenthal and Tits (working independently in the 1950s and 60s) organised them into a strikingly symmetric table called the magic square. The rows and columns are indexed by the four normed division algebras ℝ, ℂ, ℍ (quaternions), and 𝕆 (octonions), and each cell contains the Lie algebra produced by a uniform construction applied to the pair (row, column).

The construction takes two normed division algebras 𝔸 and 𝔹 and produces a Lie algebra M(𝔸, 𝔹). The details vary (Tits' original construction, Vinberg's symmetric version, and Barton–Sudbery's triality-based version all give the same answer), but the outputs are forced by dimension counting and the algebra of 3×3 Hermitian matrices. Two things leap out of this table:

  1. The exceptions are exactly the octonion entries. F4, E6, E7, E8 fill the fourth row and fourth column (the 𝕆 row and 𝕆 column). The classical algebras fill the 3×3 ℝ, ℂ, ℍ block. The boundary between "classical" and "exceptional" is drawn exactly by the presence or absence of 𝕆 in the construction.
  2. The table is symmetric. M(𝔸, 𝔹) = M(𝔹, 𝔸), so the (ℝ, 𝕆) and (𝕆, ℝ) entries are both F4, and similarly for E6 and E7. This reflects a deep compatibility in the Tits construction: the Lie algebra does not care which of the two division algebras you "start" from.

G2, the fifth exception, does not appear in the 4×4 table. Instead, G2 sits outside the square, as Der(𝕆), the automorphism Lie algebra of the octonion product itself. The magic square applies a doubled construction to two copies of a division algebra, but G2 is just the "automorphisms of one copy" rather than any doubled version. In that sense, G2 is the zeroth step of the exceptional ladder: it is the small exceptional Lie algebra that sits directly on top of the octonions without any Jordan-algebra detour.

The exceptions exist because the octonions exist. The classification of simple Lie algebras tells you there are four infinite families plus five exceptions. The exceptions are not parachute drops from nowhere, they exist precisely because one of the four normed division algebras (𝕆) is non-associative, and that non-associativity is rigid enough to produce exactly the algebras F4, E6, E7, E8 through the magic-square construction, plus G2 through its automorphism Lie algebra. Remove the octonions, and the classification list becomes ABCD, full stop. Keep them, and you get five more entries plus one (G2) on the side.

The H₀ ascent

The research framing for this series described the classification as the H₀ ascent, a combinatorial algorithm that adds one node at a time to a Dynkin diagram, constrained at every step by the requirement that the associated Cartan matrix remain positive definite. Walking from the smallest simple Lie algebra A1 upward one node at a time traces out the entire classification tree:

The exceptional algebras appear at five specific ranks (2, 4, 6, 7, 8) and nowhere else. Every subsequent rank admits only classical-family extensions: the four infinite families A, B, C, D climb forever, but no sixth exception ever shows up.

Pointers forward

Two natural directions extend beyond the classification we have proved.

Affine Kac-Moody algebras

Extending any finite Dynkin diagram by one additional node, in a specific way that produces the extended Dynkin diagram, gives a graph whose Cartan matrix is positive semi-definite with a 1-dimensional kernel. The associated Lie algebra is no longer finite-dimensional; it is a so-called affine Kac-Moody algebra, typically denoted with a tilde: Ãn, D̃n, Ẽ6, Ẽ7, Ẽ8. These algebras are infinite-dimensional but highly structured, and they form the mathematical backbone of:

  • 2D conformal field theory. Every rational CFT has an underlying affine Lie algebra whose representation theory determines its structure.
  • Integrable lattice models. Classical and quantum integrable systems (Toda, sine-Gordon, etc.) organise themselves around affine Lie-algebra data.
  • String theory. The gauge symmetries of the heterotic string at level k = 1 come from affine E8 × affine E8.
  • Monstrous moonshine. The Moonshine Module of Frenkel, Lepowsky, and Meurman, the vertex operator algebra V♮, is built from an affine Lie-algebra construction.

The (6, 3, 2) boundary case we encountered in explainer 14 is exactly the affine extension Ẽ8. If you slide past the crystallographic threshold instead of stopping at it, you drop further into hyperbolic Kac-Moody algebras, where one eigenvalue becomes strictly negative and the structure becomes even richer. These extensions are the frontier of Lie-theoretic work in mathematical physics, and they are the subject of a future series in this project.

What we take with us

  • Nine families. The complete list is An, Bn, Cn, Dn, G2, F4, E6, E7, E8. Four infinite classical families plus five singular exceptions. No others exist, over ℂ, up to isomorphism.
  • The exceptions follow a pattern. F4, E6, E7, E8 are exactly the octonion row/column of the Freudenthal magic square, the Lie algebras that arise when at least one of the two inputs to Tits' M(𝔸, 𝔹) construction is the octonions. G2 sits outside the square as Der(𝕆), the derivation algebra of the octonions themselves.
  • The classification is rigid. Any attempt to extend beyond E8, either by adding a simple root or by relaxing the positive-definite constraint on the Cartan matrix, forces the algebra to become infinite-dimensional (affine Kac-Moody) or to acquire a Lorentzian signature (hyperbolic Kac-Moody). The finite classification stops here.
  • One story, three series. The classification of simple Lie algebras, the geometry of E8, and the algebra of the octonions are three vantage points on the same body of mathematics. Each is a self-contained entry point, and each cross-references the other two.

Cartan–Killing's theorem is done. We began with a bracket satisfying three axioms, walked through twenty-odd pages of machinery, and arrived at a list of nine families that has stood for a century and a quarter as one of the cleanest closure theorems in mathematics. The classification is tight. The exceptions are justified. And the octonions are the reason.
