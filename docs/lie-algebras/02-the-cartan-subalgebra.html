The Cartan Subalgebra

Part 2 of 15. A maximal commuting set of simultaneously diagonalisable elements. Once we fix one, every element of 𝔤 becomes either a Cartan element or an eigenvector of the whole Cartan, and the eigenvalues will become the roots.

The adjoint representation from the last explainer gave us a linear operator adx : 𝔤 → 𝔤 for every element x ∈ 𝔤. To understand 𝔤, we would like to diagonalise these operators and read off their eigenspaces. That is the standard linear-algebra manoeuvre: any interesting linear operator is best understood through its spectrum.

But 𝔤 has many elements, and each one gives a different operator adx, usually with a different eigenspace decomposition. Rather than picking one x and living with a single decomposition, we pick as large a subset as possible where all the adjoint operators can be diagonalised simultaneously. That subset is called a Cartan subalgebra, and it is the organising scaffold for every classification argument that follows.

Why we want to diagonalise

An operator T on a vector space V is diagonalisable if V decomposes as a direct sum of eigenspaces of T. That decomposition is the cleanest possible description of T: it tells you which subspaces are invariant, what T does on each one, and it lets you take functions of T (powers, exponentials, logarithms) by working eigenspace-by-eigenspace. Given a Lie algebra 𝔤 whose adjoint operators are all simultaneously diagonalisable on some subspace 𝔥 ⊂ 𝔤, we can decompose 𝔤 into joint eigenspaces, one eigenspace for each linear functional 𝔥 → ℂ, and ask what structure on those functionals the bracket forces. In the next explainer this is exactly what happens, and the forced structure is the root system.

The constraint is that simultaneous diagonalisation of a family of operators requires two conditions. First, each operator in the family must itself be diagonalisable. Second, any two operators in the family must commute. Both conditions are facts about linear algebra on a fixed vector space:

Linear-algebra fact. A family of linear operators {Ti} on a finite-dimensional vector space V can be simultaneously diagonalised, meaning there is a single basis of V that is an eigenbasis for every Ti at once, if and only if (a) each Ti is diagonalisable on its own, and (b) Ti Tj = Tj Ti for all i, j. These two conditions are necessary and sufficient.

So to get a simultaneous eigenspace decomposition of 𝔤 via the adjoint representation, we need to find a subspace 𝔥 ⊂ 𝔤 of elements x for which adx is diagonalisable and for which any two of them give commuting adjoint operators. The second condition has a beautifully simple translation at the level of 𝔤 itself: adx and ady commute as operators on 𝔤 if and only if [x, y] lies in the centre of 𝔤. For simple Lie algebras the centre is zero, so adx and ady commute precisely when [x, y] = 0, that is, when x and y commute in 𝔤. So we are looking for a subspace 𝔥 of commuting elements, each of which has a diagonalisable adjoint.

Toral subalgebras and the Cartan

An element x ∈ 𝔤 whose adjoint operator adx is diagonalisable is called semisimple (or ad-diagonalisable, if we want to be explicit that we mean the adjoint and not some other representation). A subspace 𝔥 ⊂ 𝔤 is toral if every element of 𝔥 is semisimple and 𝔥 is abelian, all its elements bracket to zero with each other. A Cartan subalgebra is a maximal toral subalgebra: a toral subalgebra that is not strictly contained in any larger toral subalgebra.

"Maximal" has a concrete meaning: you cannot extend 𝔥 by adding any new semisimple element y ∉ 𝔥 while keeping the space commutative. Try to add such a y, and either y fails to be semisimple, or the extended space fails to be abelian. The upshot is that a Cartan subalgebra captures the largest possible commuting diagonalisable family, the largest batch of operators whose joint eigenspaces we can compute.

Three theorems about Cartan subalgebras hold for every finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebra over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero (such as ℂ, the field we use throughout). We use each one as a black box:

  1. Existence. A Cartan subalgebra exists. One classical construction picks any element whose adjoint is as close to being "generic" as possible and takes the centraliser of its semisimple part.
  2. Abelianness in the simple case. For a simple (or more generally semisimple) 𝔤, the Cartan subalgebra 𝔥 is honestly abelian as a subspace of 𝔤, there is no hidden nilpotent part. This is what lets us treat adh for h ∈ 𝔥 as a family of genuinely commuting diagonalisable operators.
  3. Conjugacy. Any two Cartan subalgebras are conjugate to each other under an inner automorphism of 𝔤. Equivalently, the abstract data we extract from 𝔥, its dimension, its eigenspace structure on 𝔤, the geometry of the resulting root system, do not depend on which Cartan subalgebra we picked.

The third theorem is the one we lean on most. It means that the dimension of a Cartan subalgebra is an invariant of 𝔤 itself: it does not depend on the choice of 𝔥. That invariant has a name.

Rank. The rank of a semisimple Lie algebra 𝔤 is the dimension of any (hence every) Cartan subalgebra. Rank is one of the two fundamental invariants of a simple Lie algebra, alongside its dimension. We will see later that the nine families in the classification are indexed naturally by rank: A1, A2, A3, … for increasing sln; E6, E7, E8 for the rank-6, 7, 8 exceptionals; and so on.

Diagonal matrices in sln

The construction is easiest to see in sln(ℂ), the trace-zero n × n complex matrices. Inside sln, the diagonal trace-zero matrices form an (n − 1)-dimensional subspace. They form an abelian subalgebra because any two diagonal matrices commute. Each one is diagonal, hence diagonalisable in the most trivial possible sense, which means its adjoint operator is also diagonalisable (on the space of all matrices, acting by commutation). And this subspace is maximal: you cannot enlarge it while keeping it abelian and semisimple. So it is a Cartan subalgebra of sln, and the rank of sln is n − 1.

Concretely, for sl3 the Cartan subalgebra is the 2-dimensional space of diagonal matrices

where the trace constraint h1 + h2 + h3 = 0 kills one of the three degrees of freedom. The sl3 algebra as a whole is 8-dimensional (as a trace-zero 3×3 matrix has 9 − 1 = 8 free entries). Two of those eight dimensions are the diagonal Cartan; the other six are the six off-diagonal matrix units Eij (with i ≠ j). Each Eij is an eigenvector of every diagonal matrix H = diag(h1, h2, h3) under the adjoint action: a direct computation gives [H, Eij] = (hi − hj) Eij, so the eigenvalue is the linear functional H ↦ hi − hj, and the 1-dimensional eigenspace is the span of Eij.

This is already the root space decomposition of sl3, previewed one explainer ahead of schedule. There are six off-diagonal positions (i, j), so six linear functionals, and sl3 breaks up as a 2-dimensional Cartan plus six 1-dimensional eigenspaces. The joint eigenspace decomposition

Now we can state the result we want, which justifies the whole apparatus. Pick any Cartan subalgebra 𝔥 ⊂ 𝔤. Because 𝔥 is abelian and consists of semisimple elements, the family of adjoint operators {adh : h ∈ 𝔥} is a commuting family of diagonalisable operators on 𝔤. The linear-algebra fact quoted above says this family can be simultaneously diagonalised. So 𝔤 decomposes as a direct sum of joint eigenspaces: for each linear functional α : 𝔥 → ℂ, define

These 𝔤α are mostly zero (α has to "come from" an actual eigenvector for 𝔤α to be non-trivial), but at finitely many values of α they are non-zero subspaces. Taking the direct sum over all α gives the decomposition

where 𝔥 itself is the α = 0 piece (every element of 𝔥 is acted on trivially by the abelian bracket with itself), and Δ is the set of non-zero functionals α for which 𝔤α is non-empty. The set Δ is called the root system, and it is the next explainer's subject.

For sl3, this decomposition is the one we already saw: 𝔥 is the 2-dimensional Cartan, and there are six functionals α (one for each ordered pair (i, j) with i ≠ j) which give six 1-dimensional root spaces. The resulting picture, six roots in a 2-dimensional plane, is a hexagon, and it is the A2 root system. Seeing that hexagon is the reader's first glimpse of the geometry that will drive the classification.

Generalising beyond matrices

For matrix Lie algebras, sln, so(n), sp(n), and the other classical families, the Cartan subalgebra is always spanned by a carefully chosen family of diagonal matrices. The choice differs slightly between families because of how the defining bilinear form is normalised, but the idea is the same: a Cartan is a maximal abelian collection of diagonal elements. For sln it is the trace-zero diagonals. For so(2n) and sp(2n) it is a block-diagonal construction that puts rotations or symplectic transformations into their own eigenspace blocks. The explicit recipes are standard and live in any classical-Lie-algebras reference.

For the exceptional Lie algebras, G2, F4, E6, E7, E8, there is no matrix realisation that falls out of the definition cleanly, so we build the Cartan subalgebra abstractly. In practice this means: write down the full root system using the combinatorial data (Dynkin diagram + Cartan matrix) we will develop in Acts I and II, and read the Cartan back off as the span of the simple co-roots. The machinery we are building is precisely what lets us do this for the exceptions.

The rank of everything we will meet. sln+1 has rank n (type An). so(2n+1) has rank n (type Bn). sp(2n) has rank n (type Cn). so(2n) has rank n (type Dn). G2, F4, E6, E7, E8 have ranks 2, 4, 6, 7, 8 respectively. When the classification is complete it will be a list indexed by this rank, and we will walk the entire list in Acts II and III.

What we take with us

  • Cartan subalgebra: a maximal abelian subspace of 𝔤 all of whose elements are semisimple (ad-diagonalisable). Existence, conjugacy, and abelianness are three theorems we accept as inputs, we do not reprove them.
  • Rank = dimension of any Cartan subalgebra = the invariant that indexes the classification. sln has rank n − 1. so(2n+1) and sp(2n) have rank n. The E-series goes up to rank 8.
  • Simultaneous diagonalisation. Because 𝔥 is abelian, the family {adh : h ∈ 𝔥} of adjoint operators on 𝔤 is a commuting family of diagonalisable operators, and any such family can be simultaneously diagonalised. This is the single linear-algebra fact that converts a Lie algebra question into an eigenvalue-and-eigenspace question.
  • Joint eigenspaces. For each linear functional α : 𝔥 → ℂ, define 𝔤α = {y ∈ 𝔤 : [h, y] = α(h) · y for all h ∈ 𝔥}. Only finitely many α give non-zero 𝔤α, and those α form the root system Δ. The decomposition 𝔤 = 𝔥 ⊕ ⊕α ∈ Δ 𝔤α is the root space decomposition, which is the next explainer.
  • sl3 as the test case. Six root spaces, one per ordered pair (i, j) with i ≠ j, sitting in a 2-dimensional Cartan. The roots are the six linear functionals h ↦ hi − hj, and they form the regular hexagon known as the A2 root system.
