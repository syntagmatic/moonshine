G₂ and the Octonions

Part 11 of 15. The smallest exception. Rank 2, dimension 14, a triple-edge Dynkin diagram with two nodes, and an algebra that shows up as the derivation algebra of the octonions, the holonomy group of G2-manifolds in seven dimensions, and the first place the exceptional structures of mathematics make themselves felt.

Of the five exceptional simple Lie algebras, G2 is the smallest, smaller than any of the classical families at the same rank. Its Dynkin diagram has only two nodes, connected by a triple edge with an arrow from the long root to the short root. Everything that matters about G2 fits on a napkin: 2 simple roots, 12 total roots, rank 2, dimension 14, Weyl group order 12 (the dihedral group of the regular hexagram). And yet this tiny algebra is the automorphism algebra of one of the four normed division algebras, the octonions, and that connection is the reason G2 exists as an exception at all.

This explainer walks through G2 from both sides: first as a root system with a specific Cartan matrix and a striking rank-2 geometry, and second as Der(𝕆), the Lie algebra of infinitesimal automorphisms of the octonion multiplication. The two perspectives meet in the 7-dimensional irreducible representation of G2 on Im(𝕆) ≅ ℝ7, and the octonions series picks up the story of the octonions themselves in parallel. Here we stay with the Lie algebra side.

Where G₂ sits in the classification

Recall the endpoint of Act II. The classification inequality 1/p + 1/q + 1/r > 1 for branched simply-laced Dynkin diagrams gave us Dn, E6, E7, and E8. The non-simply-laced cases, those with at least one double or triple edge, were handled by analogous finite checks. The triple edge case, in particular, is remarkably restrictive: explainer 9 showed that each node can have total edge weight at most 3, and a triple edge contributes weight 3 to both of its endpoints. So both endpoints of a triple edge are completely saturated, and no further nodes can be attached to either side.

That leaves exactly one possible triple-edge Dynkin diagram: two nodes connected by a single triple edge, and nothing else. No third node, no second edge anywhere. The associated Lie algebra is G2, named by Killing in 1888 as the first of the exceptions he could not fit into the classical sl/so/sp families. It is literally the only simple Lie algebra with a triple edge, and it is the only exceptional Lie algebra of rank 2.

The root system

G2 has 12 roots in a 2-dimensional Euclidean space, split into 6 short roots and 6 long roots. The 6 short roots form a regular hexagon, and the 6 long roots form a larger regular hexagon rotated by 30°. Overlaying the two hexagons gives a star of David, the 12-pointed rosette that is the signature image of G2.

Written in coordinates, the short roots are the 6 vectors of the form ±(ei − ej) for i ≠ j in {1, 2, 3}, living in the hyperplane x1 + x2 + x3 = 0 inside ℝ3. They have squared length 2, and they are exactly the root system of A2 = sl3. G2 contains A2 as a sub-root-system, and this embedding is precise: the short roots of G2 form the A2 root system verbatim.

The 6 long roots are of the form ±(2ei − ej − ek) for i ∈ {1, 2, 3} and {j, k} = {1, 2, 3} \ {i}. Each long root is perpendicular to one pair of short roots and at 30°/150° angles to the other four. The 12-fold symmetry of the whole configuration is its Weyl group, the dihedral group D6 of order 12.

G₂ as Der(𝕆)

Where does G2 come from? It has no matrix construction analogous to the classical families; there is no "G2(ℂ) = gl(something)" formula. Instead, the cleanest way to build G2 is as the derivation algebra of the octonions (the G2 chapter of the octonions series builds the same identification from the opposite direction).

The octonions 𝕆 are the largest of the four normed division algebras over ℝ, following ℝ, ℂ, and the quaternions ℍ. They form an 8-dimensional non-associative algebra with a bilinear multiplication. As a vector space 𝕆 splits as ℝ · 1 ⊕ Im(𝕆), where the 7-dimensional imaginary part is spanned by seven distinguished units e1, …, e7 that square to −1, satisfy eiej = −ejei for i ≠ j, and multiply according to a specific 7 × 7 table encoded by the Fano plane, the smallest projective plane, with 7 points and 7 lines.

Derivations form a Lie algebra under the commutator bracket [D1, D2] = D1D2 − D2D1 (this is a direct check, using the fact that commutators of derivations are themselves derivations). For ℝ there are no non-zero derivations, ℝ is "too rigid." For ℂ and ℍ, the derivations are respectively 0 and a 3-dimensional Lie algebra isomorphic to sl2 ≅ so(3). This is the cleanest definition of G2. Cartan himself identified G2 with Der(𝕆) in his 1894 thesis, making the octonions the first non-classical structure to enter the Cartan–Killing framework and the reason the classification has any exceptions at all. Without the octonions, the classification list would stop at the classical A, B, C, D families.

The 7-dimensional representation

The smallest non-trivial representation of G2 is 7-dimensional. It acts on the imaginary octonions Im(𝕆) ≅ ℝ7 by derivations: since any derivation D satisfies D(1) = 0 (differentiating 1 · 1 = 1 gives 2D(1) = D(1), so D(1) = 0), every derivation preserves the 7-dimensional subspace Im(𝕆), and acts there by a linear map that preserves the octonion product.

Equivalently, the 7-dimensional irrep of G2 can be described as the kernel of the projection from the defining representation of so(7) down to its spin representation, a rank reduction that is specific to dimension 7 and exists precisely because Spin(7) has a 7-dimensional "alternative" action on ℝ7 coming from the octonion product. The G2 subalgebra of so(7) is the stabiliser of a specific vector in the 7-dimensional exterior 3-power ∧3ℝ7, and that stabiliser is exactly G2.

A concrete consequence: the 7-dimensional cross product, the only multilinear "cross product" on any ℝn other than ℝ3, exists because of the octonion multiplication on Im(𝕆) ≅ ℝ7, and its automorphism group is exactly G2. This is covered in the octonions series under seven-dimensional cross products. Of the infinitely many normed-division-algebra-like possibilities for cross products in arbitrary dimensions, only two work, 3D (from the quaternions, symmetry group so(3) = A1) and 7D (from the octonions, symmetry group G2), and each one sits on top of the corresponding exceptional structure.

G₂ outside pure algebra

G2 shows up in several places outside Lie theory proper. Some highlights:

  • Differential geometry: G2-manifolds. A 7-dimensional Riemannian manifold whose holonomy group (the group of parallel-transport operators around closed loops) is exactly G2 is called a G2-manifold. Such manifolds carry a parallel 3-form inherited from the octonion product, and they are one of the two "exceptional" cases in Berger's 1955 classification of Riemannian holonomy groups, the other being Spin(7) in dimension 8.
  • Physics: M-theory compactification. 11-dimensional M-theory compactified on a 7-dimensional G2-manifold produces a 4-dimensional theory with the right amount of residual supersymmetry (one quarter) to be potentially physically interesting. This is one of the main motivations for studying G2-manifolds in the first place.
  • Algebraic geometry: nearly Kähler 6-manifolds. The "sine cone" of a nearly Kähler 6-manifold is a 7-manifold with a G2 structure, connecting G2 to the classification of special holonomies in even dimension via a dimension-raising trick.
  • The Cayley integers. There is an integer form of the octonions called the Cayley integers, and the automorphism group of the Cayley integers is a specific arithmetic form of G2. The lattice structure parallels the E8 lattice construction, and the connection is a building block in many number-theoretic applications of exceptional Lie algebras.

G2 is the rank-2 exception, but also the door. Every other exceptional Lie algebra (F4, E6, E7, E8) can be built from a construction that begins with G2 or with the octonion algebra that gives G2 its identity. The Freudenthal magic square, covered in explainer 15, organises all five exceptions around the four normed division algebras ℝ, ℂ, ℍ, 𝕆, and the octonion column is where F4, E6, E7, and E8 live. G2 itself is the automorphism group of the last entry 𝕆, so it is the "root" of the whole exceptional side of the classification.

What we take with us

  • The Dynkin signature. G2 is the unique simple Lie algebra with a triple edge in its Dynkin diagram. Two nodes, one triple edge, arrow from the long simple root to the short one. Cartan matrix [[2, −1], [−3, 2]].
  • Rank-2 root system. 12 roots in a plane: 6 short at inner hexagonal vertices, 6 long at outer hexagonal vertices rotated 30°. The 12-fold dihedral Weyl group D6 is the symmetry group of the combined star-of-David figure.
  • Dimension 14. Rank 2 + |Δ| = 12 = dim 14. The smallest exceptional simple Lie algebra.
  • G2 = Der(𝕆). G2 is the 14-dimensional Lie algebra of derivations of the octonions under the commutator bracket. Cartan's 1894 thesis made this identification and thereby put the octonions on the classification map.
  • 7-dimensional irrep. G2 acts irreducibly on Im(𝕆) ≅ ℝ7, preserving the octonion product. This is the smallest non-trivial representation of G2 and is the reason the 7-dimensional cross product exists.
  • Beyond pure algebra. G2 appears as a holonomy group in 7-dimensional differential geometry, as a candidate structure group in M-theory compactifications, and as the automorphism group of the Cayley integer lattice. Every exceptional Lie algebra is like this, they are never just algebra; they are the signature of a deeper exceptional structure that connects many fields.
