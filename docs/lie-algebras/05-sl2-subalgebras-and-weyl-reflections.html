sl2 Subalgebras and Weyl Reflections

Part 5 of 15. Every root gives an embedded sl2(ℂ) inside 𝔤, and the representation theory of sl2 forces every pair of roots to satisfy an integer constraint. From that integrality, the Weyl reflections fall out as a finite symmetry group acting on the root system.

The last explainer put a Euclidean inner product on the real span of the roots. We now have finitely many vectors in a finite-dimensional real inner-product space, but we do not yet have any constraint on how those vectors can be arranged. This explainer extracts the constraint. It comes from a single, beautiful observation: the root space decomposition of 𝔤 packages every root α together with an embedded copy of sl2(ℂ) inside 𝔤, and the representation theory of sl2, which we take as a black box, forces eigenvalues of the distinguished Cartan element of that sl2 to be integers. Integrality translates into a rigid constraint on how roots can be combined, and from that constraint, the Weyl reflections emerge.

By the end of this explainer, every root α will carry a reflection sα : 𝔼 → 𝔼 that permutes Δ, and the group generated by all such reflections, the Weyl group W, will be a finite reflection group acting on the root system. These two objects, sl2 triples and Weyl reflections, are the two final pieces of machinery Act I contributes to the classification.

One sl2 per root

Fix a root α ∈ Δ. Explainer 3's four facts tell us: the opposite functional −α is also a root, each of 𝔤α and 𝔤−α is one-dimensional, and [𝔤α, 𝔤−α] ⊆ 𝔤0 = 𝔥. So if we pick any non-zero eα ∈ 𝔤α and any non-zero fα ∈ 𝔤−α, their bracket [eα, fα] is a specific element of 𝔥. Call it hα. A short calculation using κ and ad-invariance shows that hα is non-zero, and in fact equal (up to normalisation) to the dual Cartan element tα of α from the last explainer.

That is, the 3-dimensional subspace 𝔤α ⊕ 𝔤−α ⊕ ℂ·hα is a Lie subalgebra of 𝔤 isomorphic to sl2(ℂ), the prototype non-abelian simple Lie algebra from explainer 1. The embedding sends H ↦ hα, E ↦ eα, F ↦ fα, and it matches up brackets exactly. There is one such embedded sl2 for every pair {α, −α} of roots, so a rank-r simple Lie algebra with root count |Δ| contains |Δ|/2 distinct sl2-subalgebras whose union (non-disjoint, since they all meet in 𝔥) accounts for every direction in 𝔤 except the Cartan.

Why this is a big deal. Every semisimple Lie algebra is built out of overlapping copies of a single 3-dimensional algebra, one copy per pair of opposite roots. Any fact we can prove about sl2 and then transport back to 𝔤 gives us a fact about 𝔤 as a whole, and the classification proof is precisely a long chain of such transported facts. The single most important transported fact is integrality of eigenvalues, which we turn to next.

Integrality, from sl2 representation theory

Here is the black-box fact about sl2(ℂ) that we use. A proof is a standard undergraduate exercise, it is built from the observation that E acts as a "raising" operator and F as a "lowering" operator on H-eigenspaces, but we just state the result.

Black-box theorem (representations of sl2). Let V be a finite-dimensional complex representation of sl2(ℂ). Then V decomposes as a direct sum of H-eigenspaces with strictly integer eigenvalues, and those eigenvalues are symmetric about 0: whenever k is an H-eigenvalue on V, so is −k, with the same eigenspace dimension.

We apply this theorem by making 𝔤 itself a representation of the embedded sl2(α) via the adjoint action. The three basis vectors of sl2(α) act on 𝔤 as adhα, adeα, adfα, and 𝔤 becomes a finite-dimensional sl2-module under this action. The theorem says the eigenvalues of adhα on 𝔤 are integers.

But those eigenvalues are exactly the values of other roots β on hα. Specifically, if y ∈ 𝔤β is a non-zero root vector, then [hα, y] = β(hα) · y, so β(hα) is an adhα-eigenvalue. By the theorem, β(hα) ∈ ℤ. This holds for every pair of roots α, β ∈ Δ.

Converting from the algebra side to the Euclidean side via the iso 𝔥 ≅ 𝔥*, the quantity β(hα) has a name: it is the Cartan integer of β against α,

(the factor of 2 and the denominator come out of normalising hα to make its eigenvalues integers rather than half-integers). The integrality theorem says: for every ordered pair of roots α, β, the Cartan integer nβ,α is an integer. That is the first hard constraint on Δ that comes from the algebra.

Weyl reflections

The Cartan integers feed directly into a reflection. Fix α ∈ Δ and define the linear map sα : 𝔼 → 𝔼 by the formula

Two facts about sα are immediate from the definition. First, sα is the Euclidean reflection of 𝔼 across the hyperplane orthogonal to α, because sα(α) = −α and sα(ξ) = ξ for any ξ ⊥ α. Second, sα(β) has integer coefficient on α (namely −nβ,α ∈ ℤ). So sα(β) = β − nβ,α · α is a linear combination of two roots with integer coefficients, and in particular it is an integer linear combination of the roots β and α.

The Weyl group W is the subgroup of the orthogonal group O(𝔼) generated by all the reflections sα for α ∈ Δ:

W acts on Δ by permutations (since each generator does, and compositions of permutations are permutations), and since Δ is finite this embeds W into the symmetric group on |Δ| letters. In particular W itself is finite, it is a subgroup of a finite group. The Weyl group of the classification is the finite reflection group attached to 𝔤, and it is the main combinatorial invariant that distinguishes one simple Lie algebra from another at the level of root systems.

The structural punchline. Every simple Lie algebra 𝔤 over ℂ determines a finite set Δ ⊂ 𝔼 of vectors in a real Euclidean space, and that set is closed under a finite reflection group W. Classifying simple Lie algebras reduces, via all the machinery of explainers 1 to 5, to classifying finite reflection groups acting on Euclidean space. Those reflection groups and their root systems are the only things that survive into Act II.

Orbits of the Weyl group

Since W permutes the roots, we can ask what its orbits on Δ look like. The orbit of α under W is the smallest W-stable subset of Δ containing α, and it is found by repeatedly applying reflections and taking the union. The next explainer will prove that the Cartan integers nβ,α are restricted to {0, ±1, ±2, ±3, ±4}, which is the main tool for classifying root systems in the abstract. For now we can see the restriction at work concretely: the Weyl orbits on the two-dimensional root systems A2 (the sl3 hexagon) and B2 (the sl5-style square with its four diagonal roots) already display the two characteristic behaviours of reflection groups.

For a simply-laced root system, one where all roots have the same squared length, the Weyl group acts transitively on Δ: every root is in the same orbit as every other. A2 and all the ADE types are simply-laced. For a non-simply-laced root system, the Weyl group acts transitively on each length class separately: long roots stay long, short roots stay short. B2, C2, B3, G2, F4 are non-simply-laced, and their Weyl groups have two orbits, one per length.

A specific check: the B2 Cartan integers

To make integrality concrete, let us compute the Cartan integers for B2 directly. Its simple roots are α1 = e1 − e2 (long, squared length 2) and α2 = e2 (short, squared length 1). The Cartan integers are:

  • nα1, α1 = 2(α1, α1)/(α1, α1) = 2, same for nα2, α2, diagonal entries are always 2.
  • (α1, α2) = −1, (α2, α2) = 1, so nα1, α2 = 2·(−1)/1 = −2 ∈ ℤ.
  • (α1, α1) = 2, so nα2, α1 = 2·(−1)/2 = −1 ∈ ℤ.

The two off-diagonal Cartan integers are −2 and −1, they are integers, but they are not equal. That asymmetry is the signature of a non-simply-laced root system: the product nα1, α2 · nα2, α1 = 2 is what eventually controls the angle between α1 and α2 via the identity nα, β · nβ, α = 4 cos²θ. We will meet this relation head-on in explainer 6, where it becomes the crystallographic restriction.

What we take with us

  • sl2 triples. Each root α ∈ Δ determines an embedded sl2(ℂ) inside 𝔤, spanned by a raising operator eα ∈ 𝔤α, a lowering operator fα ∈ 𝔤−α, and a Cartan element hα ∈ 𝔥 satisfying the sl2 relations exactly.
  • Integrality of eigenvalues (black box). Finite-dimensional sl2 representations have integer H-eigenvalues symmetric about zero. Applied to 𝔤 as an sl2(α)-module via the adjoint action, this forces every value β(hα), equivalently every Cartan integer nβ, α = 2(β, α)/(α, α), to be an integer.
  • Weyl reflections. Each root α gives a reflection sα(β) = β − nβ, α · α of 𝔼, and sα maps roots to roots. It is the Euclidean reflection across the hyperplane perpendicular to α, and its action on Δ has integer coefficients.
  • The Weyl group. W = ⟨sα : α ∈ Δ⟩ is a finite subgroup of O(𝔼) that permutes Δ. It has one orbit on Δ for simply-laced types (one length class) and two orbits for non-simply-laced types (long and short).
  • What is next. The Cartan integers nβ, α are constrained further than merely being integers, they can only take values in {0, ±1, ±2, ±3, ±4}, because nα, β · nβ, α equals 4 cos²θ, which lies in [0, 4]. Explainer 6 turns that observation into the crystallographic restriction: only four angles between roots are allowed.

Act I is complete. We have defined Lie algebras, extracted the root space decomposition, shifted to Euclidean geometry via the Killing form, and pulled out the Weyl group as a finite reflection group on Δ. The setup is finished. Act II applies that setup to prove the classification.
