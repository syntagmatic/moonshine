Graph Pruning: Trees, Loops, and Valency Bounds

Part 9 of 15. Two short vector-norm arguments rule out every candidate Dynkin diagram that has a cycle, and every one that has a node with more than three incident edges. What is left is a surprisingly small list of shapes, and we will finish that list off in the next explainer.

We have the classification problem reduced to: which connected graphs with edges labelled (single, double, triple) can arise as Dynkin diagrams of positive-definite Cartan matrices? Act I supplied the machinery. Explainer 6 proved the crystallographic restriction. Explainers 7 and 8 instantiated the Dynkin encoding on the classical families. We are now ready to prune.

Two structural constraints do nearly all the work. First, an admissible Dynkin diagram has no cycles, it must be a tree. Second, every node in an admissible diagram has total edge weight at most 3, counting each edge with its multiplicity (single = 1, double = 2, triple = 3). Put together, these two constraints eliminate stars with four arms, cycles of any length, graphs with a triple edge adjacent to anything else, and most dense configurations. The only shapes that survive are trees with at most one branch-point of valency 3 and at most one multiple edge, and that is exactly what the classification will end up classifying.

Both proofs are short vector-norm arguments: we choose specific unit vectors and compute their sum's squared length. Because the Cartan matrix is positive definite (that's what it means for the Lie algebra to be finite-dimensional and semisimple), any linear combination of simple roots that is non-zero must have strictly positive squared length. Turning that into an inequality on the structure of the graph gives the two bounds.

Setup: unit simple roots

Given a Dynkin diagram with n nodes, pick one simple root vi at each node, normalised to unit length: (vi, vi) = 1 for every i. For a simply-laced diagram (all edges single), this costs nothing because all simple roots already have the same length and we just rescale. For a non-simply-laced diagram we rescale each root individually so that every vi has unit length; this changes the Cartan integers but preserves the angles and the edge structure. What we gain is that every unit vector has (vi, vi) = 1, and pairwise inner products satisfy

In particular, when the edge is a single edge we have 2(vi, vj) = −1; when it is a double edge, 2(vi, vj) = −√2; when it is a triple edge, 2(vi, vj) = −√3. These will plug straight into our norm computations.

The argument is tight: if the diagram had even one extra edge, say n edges on n nodes, forming a single cycle, the inequality 0 < n − n = 0 would fail. The positive-definiteness of the Cartan matrix is precisely what forbids cycles.

What trees buy us. Every admissible Dynkin diagram has a unique path between any two nodes. This is what lets the classification argument in the next explainer reason about diagrams in terms of arms radiating from a single branch node, rather than in terms of arbitrary graph structure. Take away tree-ness, and the classification becomes wildly harder.

Valency at most 3

Claim: in an admissible Dynkin diagram, every node has total edge weight at most 3, where a single edge counts as weight 1, a double as 2, and a triple as 3.

Proof sketch. Pick a node with unit vector v, and let u1, …, uk be the unit simple roots at its k neighbours. Since the diagram is a tree (by the previous claim), the ui are not connected to each other, any path between ui and uj must pass through v. But all we need is the slightly weaker fact that the ui are pairwise orthogonal, which holds whenever the edge between ui and uj is absent from the diagram.

Now, the projection of v onto any subspace has squared length at most (v, v) = 1, with equality iff v lies in the subspace. And v certainly does not lie in the span of its neighbours, because v is linearly independent from them (as part of a basis Π of 𝔥*). So the inequality is strict:

Each term (v, ui)² equals cos²θi = (edge-multiplicity at ui)/4. Summing over all neighbours gives (total edge-multiplicity at v)/4 < 1, i.e. the total edge weight at any node is strictly less than 4. Since edge multiplicities are integers, the total is at most 3.

This is a surprisingly sharp bound. A node can have three single edges (one-one-one), but not four, so the "star with four arms" is forbidden. A node can have one single edge plus one double edge (one+two=3), but not two double edges (four). A node can have one triple edge and nothing else (three), but not a triple edge plus anything else.

Taking the tree constraint and the valency constraint together, the list of possible connected admissible diagrams collapses to a very short list of shapes:

  • Linear chains of simple edges, the An family, arbitrarily long.
  • Linear chains with one double edge at an end, the Bn/Cn families. The double edge exhausts the valency at one end, so no further nodes can be attached on that side.
  • Linear chains with one double edge in the middle, F4-style diagrams. The double edge uses up valency on both sides, so the chain has at most two additional nodes.
  • Trees with exactly one branch-point of valency 3, all edges single, the Dn family plus the E-series candidates.
  • Isolated triple edge, the G2-style diagram. The triple edge uses up valency 3 at both endpoints immediately, so no further nodes can be attached. This gives exactly one candidate.

Nothing else survives. Loops are out (no cycles). Stars with four arms are out (valency too big). Two double edges in a single diagram are out (either they share a node, exceeding valency, or they are separated by a chain which also exceeds valency). Every other dense configuration you can think of fails one of the two constraints.

What remains

The pruning constraints have reduced the classification to a short list of candidate shapes, but they have not yet said which of those candidates give actual simple Lie algebras. Specifically, the branched trees with valency 3 could in principle have branches of any length, and we have not yet ruled out diagrams like a branch with arms of lengths (5, 4, 3) or (10, 2, 2). What we take with us

  • Tree constraint. Any admissible connected Dynkin diagram has n − 1 edges on n nodes, it is a tree. The proof uses |v1 + ⋯ + vn|² > 0 and the observation that each edge contributes −√(mult) ≤ −1 to the double-sum.
  • Valency constraint. Any node has total edge weight at most 3. The proof projects the central node onto an orthogonal basis of its neighbours and uses Σ (cos²θi) < 1, which forces (total weight)/4 < 1.
  • Shape catalogue. Taking both constraints together leaves only five kinds of shape: linear chains (A), chains ending in a double edge (B, C), chains with a middle double edge (F4), trees with one valency-3 branch and all single edges (D, E-series), and the isolated triple edge (G2).
  • What is still open. The branched-tree candidates are not yet restricted in arm length. The next explainer plugs the branch data into the spherical Diophantine inequality 1/p + 1/q + 1/r > 1 and reads off the remaining cases.
