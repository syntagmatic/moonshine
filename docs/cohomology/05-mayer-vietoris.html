Mayer–Vietoris as a computation engine

Chop a space in two. Compute the cohomology of each piece and of their intersection. Bookkeep the result through a long exact sequence and a third H* falls out — same answer as the matrix reduction, same answer as the integrated forms.

So far this series has computed H* two ways: combinatorially in essays 02 and 03, by transposing a boundary matrix and reducing; analytically in essay 04, by integrating closed forms on a sphere. This essay is about a third way, which is neither combinatorial nor analytic — it is recursive. Pick a cover X = U ∪ V. Compute H* of U, of V, and of their overlap U ∩ V. Plug those three answers into a long exact sequence. Out comes H*(X), computed without ever touching the full coboundary of X itself.

The theorem that makes the recursion run is Mayer–Vietoris. The cleanest way to state it is not as a theorem but as an algorithm with a correctness certificate. The algorithm reads off the Betti numbers of each piece. The certificate is exactness — every arrow in the sequence balances its neighbours. This essay watches the algorithm execute on a small torus and three friends.

The long exact sequence

Given a space X with a two-piece cover X = U ∪ V, there is a sequence of cohomology groups stitched together by three maps. It runs in both directions forever — in each degree k, three new groups appear:

The maps are not mysterious. The middle map π* is restriction: take a cocycle on X and read off its values on simplices that happen to live in U, then again on those in V. The next map subtracts the two restrictions on the overlap: if you came from a global cocycle, U and V agreed there, so the difference is zero. The connecting map δ* goes the other way around. It is the conceptual mountain.

Read literally: given a cocycle η on the overlap, extend it however you like to a cochain ηU on U (filling in arbitrary values on simplices outside the overlap). The extension is not a cocycle — its coboundary δηU is nonzero. But its coboundary is supported only on simplices entirely in U (the part where you extended), and that part also lives inside X. Push it forward and you get a cocycle on X. Its class is δ*[η]. The arbitrary choice of extension changes the cocycle but not its class — that is the content of the lemma.

The word exact means image equals kernel. At each group in the sequence, the rank of the incoming map plus the rank of the outgoing map equals the dimension of the group. There is no slack. Whatever the incoming map fills, the outgoing map kills. This is the algebraic statement that the cover glues without overcounting.

The point. Three Betti tables plus exactness equals one Betti table. The reader does not solve a 49×49 system on the torus — they solve three small systems on the pieces and let the exact sequence balance the books.

What the figure shows on the torus

Default cover, default surface: two disks each containing four or five vertices of the torus, overlapping on two. The scorecards report something like H*(U) = (1, 0, 0), H*(V) = (1, 0, 0), H*(U ∩ V) = (1, 0, 0). Three contractible-ish pieces. The bottom row of boxes shows the LES: β0(X) = 1, β1(X) = 2, β2(X) = 2 are the actual Betti numbers of the 7-vertex torus, but those are not what falls out of this cover — a cover by disks like these does not even reach the torus's first homology, because no single disk wraps a meridian.

Drag one disk wide enough to contain a meridian's worth of vertices. The scorecard for that piece jumps: H*(U) = (1, 1, 0). A loop is now visible inside U. Drag the other disk to a longitude. Both pieces now see a loop. The overlap, depending on placement, becomes a thicker strip — U ∩ V may inherit a loop or stay contractible. The LES boxes update live. Press "Verify exactness"; the boxes light green when the rank balance holds.

The "Step through" button walks the maps in order: starting at H0(U ∩ V), then the connecting δ* into H1(X), then restriction to H1(U) ⊕ H1(V), then difference on the overlap, then around again into H2(X). At each step the current map highlights red and the target box pulses.

The covers are vertex partitions. Rather than open disks on a smooth surface, the figure uses a vertex mask: vertex v is in U, in V, or in both. The simplices of U are exactly those whose vertices are all in U, and similarly for V and U ∩ V. This is exactly what COH.mv.cover does. It is the simplicial analogue of an open cover — the same Mayer–Vietoris LES runs on it.

The connecting map by hand

For the torus cover where both pieces are annular strips and the overlap is two parallel arcs, the connecting map is the source of the second cohomology class. Pick a 0-cocycle on the overlap that takes value 1 on one arc and 0 on the other. Extend by zero into U: the cochain is 1 on vertices of one arc and 0 everywhere else in U. The coboundary δηU is a 1-cochain: it is nonzero exactly on edges of U with one endpoint on the chosen arc and the other endpoint off it. These edges thread through U from one arc toward the other, picking up a loop class on the way. The cohomology class of this 1-cochain in H1(X) is the connecting image. It is one of the two generators of H1(T2).

The other generator comes from H0(U ∩ V) contributing two components but U and V each contributing one: δ* sees a kernel of dimension 1 and pushes it forward as a class. Mayer–Vietoris hands you H1(T2) = (ℤ/2)2 by counting: H0(U ∩ V) has rank 2, the restriction map kills rank 1, the connecting map contributes the remaining 1; combined with the rank of H1(U) ⊕ H1(V) that survives the difference, the two pieces add to 2. The full count appears in the bottom row of the figure whenever the cover is set up to see both loops.

A sentence about sheaves

A cover is the data of subsets that glue, and sheaf cohomology generalises Mayer–Vietoris past two pieces — any number of opens, any pattern of overlaps, organised through the Čech complex. But the two-piece case is the whole flavour: the same gluing, the same connecting map, the same exactness check. The Čech complex of a two-element cover is exactly the Mayer–Vietoris sequence. Everything else is bookkeeping for more pieces.

What we take with us

  • H*(X) can be computed from H*(U), H*(V), and H*(U ∩ V) by exact-sequence bookkeeping. Three small problems, one answer.
  • The connecting map δ* is the new piece: extend a cocycle on the overlap to a cochain on one side, take its coboundary, read the result as a cocycle on the whole.
  • Exactness is a rank balance, not an abstract definition. The figure verifies it by counting Betti numbers at each box.
  • Same H* as the matrix reduction of essay 02. Same H* as the integrated forms of essay 04. Three computations, one cohomology.
