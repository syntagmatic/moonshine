Mayer–Vietoris as a computation engine

Mayer–Vietoris computes the cohomology of a space from two pieces that cover it and their overlap, joined by a long exact sequence. The result matches the matrix reduction and the integrated forms.

Essays 02 to 04 computed H* by reducing a boundary matrix and by integrating closed forms. This part computes it from pieces. Pick a cover X = U ∪ V. Compute H* of U, of V, and of their overlap U ∩ V. A long exact sequence joins those three answers to H*(X), without reducing the full coboundary of X. This is the Mayer–Vietoris sequence, and the figure below runs it on a small torus, a Klein bottle and an octahedron.

The long exact sequence

Given a space X with a two-piece cover X = U ∪ V, there is a sequence of cohomology groups joined by three maps, with three groups in each degree k:

The first map π* is restriction: take a cocycle on X and read off its values on simplices that happen to live in U, then again on those in V. The next map subtracts the two restrictions on the overlap: if you came from a global cocycle, U and V agreed there, so the difference is zero. The connecting map δ* takes more work:

Given a cocycle η on the overlap, extend it any way you like to a cochain ηU on U. The extension is usually not a cocycle, but its coboundary δηU vanishes on the overlap, where it equals δη = 0. So it extends by zero to a cocycle on X, whose class is δ*[η]. A different extension changes the cocycle but not its class.

Exact means image equals kernel. Counting dimensions, at each group the rank of the incoming map plus the rank of the outgoing map equals the dimension of the group.

Betti numbers of the pieces are not enough on their own; you also need the ranks of the maps. Over a field, exactness gives a formula that uses only the pieces, through the difference maps dk = iU* − iV*, which never look at X:

The first bracket is what the connecting map carries in from the overlap, the second is what survives from the pieces. For the torus below that means a handful of small reductions on annuli and circles in place of one reduction on the full 32-triangle complex.

Reading the torus

Start with the preset two annuli. Rows 0, 1 and 2 of the grid form U, rows 2, 3 and 0 form V, and since the grid wraps top to bottom both pieces are annular bands. Each has Betti numbers (1, 1, 0). Their overlap is rows 0 and 2, two disjoint circles, with Betti numbers (2, 2, 0). In degree 0 the restriction π* has rank 1 (a constant function restricts to a constant on each band), so the difference map has rank 2 − 1 = 1, and the connecting map out of the two-dimensional H0(U ∩ V) must have rank 2 − 1 = 1. That is one class in H1(T2), the one that measures how often a loop crosses from one band to the other. The other class comes from the bands themselves: π* has rank 1 in degree 1. So β1 = 1 + 1 = 2. In degree 2 the pieces have nothing, so the whole of H2(T2) is the image of the connecting map from the circles, and β2 = 1. The torus has Betti numbers (1, 2, 1), and the figure confirms each node.

The preset star of a vertex covers the torus differently: V is one vertex with its six neighbours, a disk, and U is everything except that vertex, a torus with a hole. Now both classes of H1 come from U, and H2 is the image of the single loop around the hole. The answer is the same and the bookkeeping is different, so in practice you pick the cover whose pieces you already understand.

Switch to the Klein bottle and the two annuli cover gives the same numbers, since over ℤ/2 the Klein bottle also has Betti numbers (1, 2, 1). Then try the same bands turned sideways, by painting columns instead of rows. On the torus that is still a cover. On the Klein bottle it is not: the flipped gluing makes column 1 adjacent to column 3, so an edge joins a U-only vertex to a V-only vertex and belongs to neither piece.

That breaks the one hypothesis the theorem needs: the pieces must cover X. The pieces here are built from vertices, and a simplex with one vertex only in U and another only in V belongs to neither piece. Then U ∪ V is a smaller complex than X, and the sequence computes the cohomology of that smaller complex. The preset no overlap shows the extreme case. The figure checks X = U ∪ V before it checks anything else.

The connecting map by hand

The octahedron with the two hemispheres cover is small enough to do on paper. U is the north pole with the four equator vertices, V the south pole with the same four, and U ∩ V is the equator, a 4-cycle. Every piece has trivial cohomology in degree 1 except the equator, whose H1 is generated by the cocycle η that is 1 on a single equator edge [e1, e2] and 0 on the other three.

Extend η by zero to the northern cap. Its coboundary is 1 on each northern triangle that contains the edge [e1, e2], and there is exactly one, [n, e1, e2]. Set it to 0 on the southern triangles. The result is a 2-cochain on the whole octahedron, 1 on one triangle and 0 on the other seven, and on a surface every 2-cochain is a cocycle. It is not a coboundary: each edge lies in two triangles, so the coboundary of any 1-cochain takes the value 1 on an even number of triangles, and one is odd. So δ* sends the generator of H1(equator) to the generator of H2(S2). In the figure, δ* from degree 1 to degree 2 has rank 1 for this cover.

Sheaves and Čech cohomology

Sheaf cohomology generalises Mayer–Vietoris to covers with any number of open sets and any pattern of overlaps, organised through the Čech complex. For a two-element cover the Čech complex reduces to the Mayer–Vietoris sequence, so the gluing and the connecting map above are already the general mechanism; more pieces only add bookkeeping.

Every complex so far came already triangulated, and the covers were painted on it by hand. Part 6 starts from a cloud of points, where the complex itself has to be built.
