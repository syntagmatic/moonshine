Singular cohomology on a triangulated torus

Take the boundary matrix you already built for homology. Transpose it. Reduce. Read off H0, H1, H2. That is the entire construction of cohomology over ℤ/2.

Explainer 01 motivated the quotient: cohomology classes are the things that survive a δ-perturbation slider. This one computes it. We pick a small closed surface, write down its boundary matrix $\partial$, transpose to get the coboundary $\delta$, and run Gaussian elimination over $\mathbb{Z}/2$ on each piece. The reader who has watched the TDA series build $\partial$ already has the pipeline — the punchline of this essay is that the matrix transpose is the whole new step. Nothing else changes. Historical anchor: Poincaré built homology in 1895; the dualisation came decades later (Alexander, Kolmogorov, Čech, then Eilenberg–Steenrod). On a finite simplicial complex with $\mathbb{Z}/2$ coefficients, the dust is a thirty-line linear-algebra subroutine.

Cochains as the dual of chains

Fix a finite simplicial complex $X$ and a degree $k$. The chain group $C_k = C_k(X; \mathbb{Z}/2)$ is the $\mathbb{Z}/2$-vector space spanned by the $k$-simplices. The cochain group is the dual,

which is the same vector space — a cochain $\alpha : C_k \to \mathbb{Z}/2$ is a 0/1 label on each $k$-simplex. The novelty is the differential. The boundary $\partial_k : C_k \to C_{k-1}$ sends a $k$-simplex to the sum of its codimension-1 faces. The coboundary $\delta_k : C^k \to C^{k+1}$ is its transpose:

Write $\partial_{k+1}$ as a matrix with rows indexed by $k$-simplices and columns by $(k+1)$-simplices; transpose it; that is $\delta_k$. The identity $\delta \circ \delta = 0$ is $\partial \circ \partial = 0$ on the other side. Once you have the transpose you have a cochain complex, and once you have a complex you have a quotient:

Closed cochains modulo exact cochains. The first time you see this it looks like vocabulary trading — closed instead of cycle, exact instead of boundary, $\delta$ instead of $\partial$. It is. Over $\mathbb{Z}/2$ on a finite complex, the singular cohomology functor agrees with the simplicial cohomology of any triangulation (Eilenberg–Steenrod, cited not proved), and both reduce to this linear-algebra computation.

The whole construction in one paragraph. Build $\partial_{k+1}$ as a 0/1 matrix. Transpose. Call it $\delta_k$. Compute its kernel — the cocycles. Compute the image of $\delta_{k-1}$ — the coboundaries. The cocycles modulo coboundaries are $H^k$. Both kernel and image come out of the same Gaussian elimination subroutine. Nothing in this essay is more complicated than "transpose a matrix and row-reduce it."

The four answers

Over $\mathbb{Z}/2$ every closed surface is orientable in the sense that matters here — the obstruction to a global orientation is the same obstruction that makes $H^2 \ne 0$, and we are working modulo 2, so it vanishes.

All four surfaces have $H^0 = \mathbb{Z}/2$ (one connected component → the constant function) and $H^2 = \mathbb{Z}/2$ (a single triangle, dual to the fundamental class). They split on $H^1$: torus and Klein each have $\beta_1 = 2$ over ℤ/2 (for the Klein bottle, the ℤ/2 torsion in integral $H_1$ shows up as an extra ℤ/2 summand here, via universal coefficients), ℝP² has $\beta_1 = 1$, the sphere has $\beta_1 = 0$. Betti numbers alone cannot distinguish $T^2$ from $S^1 \vee S^1 \vee S^2$, nor $T^2$ from Klein — that takes the cup product, the subject of essay 03.

How the reduction works

Once $\delta_k$ is a matrix, the only algorithm in the room is Gaussian elimination over $\mathbb{Z}/2$. Sweep left to right: find a row with a 1 in the current column (the pivot), then XOR that row into every other row that also has a 1 there. After the sweep, each pivot column holds a single 1, and the columns with no pivot are "free" — they generate the kernel via:

For $\delta_1 : C^1 \to C^2$ on the torus the matrix is $14 \times 21$ with rank 13 (one triangle-row is dependent — the sum of all 14 triangles is the boundary of the missing 3-chain). So $\dim \ker \delta_1 = 21 - 13 = 8$. From the other side, $\dim \mathrm{im}\,\delta_0 = 7 - 1 = 6$ (the 7 vertex-cochains minus the all-ones constant). The quotient: $8 - 6 = 2 = \dim H^1$. The two surviving cocycles, drawn from the nullspace basis modulo $\mathrm{im}\,\delta_0$, are the meridian and longitude in emerald above.

The transpose is the whole construction. In TDA you reduced $\partial$ to compute homology. Here you transpose and reduce again. Same XOR sweep, same pivot bookkeeping. The reason this works at all is that transposition preserves rank, and $\dim H^k = \dim H_k$ over a field — but that secondary fact is not what makes the algorithm tractable. What makes it tractable is that "transpose a 0/1 matrix" is one line of code.

What this construction does not see

The Betti numbers are coarse. Both $S^1 \vee S^1 \vee S^2$ and the Klein bottle have $H^0 = H^2 = \mathbb{Z}/2$ and $H^1 = (\mathbb{Z}/2)^2$ over $\mathbb{Z}/2$ — the same Betti numbers as $T^2$ — but all three are patently different spaces. The graded group $H^*$ is a thin invariant; what makes it strong is the ring structure given by multiplication of cocycles, the cup product, which we build in essay 03. On $T^2$ the two generators of $H^1$ cup to a generator of $H^2$; on the wedge they cup to zero; on the Klein bottle one generator squares to a nonzero element. The ring separates them.

This construction also does not see anything continuous. We work simplex by simplex, bit by bit. Differential forms — the topic of essay 04 — give the same answer (over $\mathbb{R}$ instead of $\mathbb{Z}/2$) on a smooth manifold, and de Rham's theorem says they agree. Cohomology is the invariant that ignores whether you are working with chains, cochains, sheaves, or forms.

What we take with us

  • The cochain complex is $\mathrm{Hom}(C_k, \mathbb{Z}/2)$ with differential $\delta = \partial^T$. Over $\mathbb{Z}/2$ this is the literal transpose of a 0/1 matrix.
  • $H^k(X; \mathbb{Z}/2) = \ker \delta_k / \mathrm{im}\,\delta_{k-1}$, computed by two Gaussian eliminations.
  • For the torus, $H^1(T^2; \mathbb{Z}/2) = (\mathbb{Z}/2)^2$, with explicit cocycle generators on the meridian and longitude.
  • $H^*(S^2; \mathbb{Z}/2) = (\mathbb{Z}/2,\, 0,\, \mathbb{Z}/2)$, $H^*(\mathbb{RP}^2; \mathbb{Z}/2) = (\mathbb{Z}/2,\, \mathbb{Z}/2,\, \mathbb{Z}/2)$, $H^*(\text{Klein}; \mathbb{Z}/2) = (\mathbb{Z}/2,\, (\mathbb{Z}/2)^2,\, \mathbb{Z}/2)$ — the universal-coefficient theorem lifts integer $\mathbb{Z}/2$-torsion in $H_1$ to an extra $\mathbb{Z}/2$ in $H^1$.
  • Betti numbers do not separate $T^2$ from $S^1 \vee S^1 \vee S^2$. The cup product does — next essay.
