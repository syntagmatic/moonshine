de Rham: differential forms on $S^2$

Differential forms on a smooth manifold give a second construction of cohomology, with the exterior derivative $d$ in place of $\delta$. Integrating a closed form around a loop gives a number that depends only on the loop's class.

So far cohomology has been combinatorial: triangulate, dualize the chain complex, quotient. This part does the same with calculus. A 1-form on a smooth manifold is a smooth assignment of a covector to each tangent space; locally it looks like $\omega = P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz$. It is closed when $d\omega = 0$ and exact when $\omega = df$ for some function $f$. The quotient $\ker d / \operatorname{im} d$ is de Rham cohomology. The two stories agree:

This is the de Rham theorem, and the agreement is natural with respect to the integration pairing $\langle [\omega], [\gamma] \rangle = \oint_\gamma \omega$. It is not proved here; the figures check it on two surfaces. On $S^2$ every closed 1-form is exact, so $H^1_{\text{dR}}(S^2) = 0$ and every loop integral of a closed form is zero. On an annulus, the angular form $d\theta$ is closed but not exact and generates $H^1$: its integral is $2\pi$ around the hole and zero around any contractible loop.

The exterior derivative

$d$ raises form degree by one and squares to zero:

For a 0-form (a function) $f$, the 1-form $df$ is its differential. For a 1-form $\omega = P\,dx + Q\,dy$, the 2-form $d\omega$ is the familiar curl-like expression

By direct computation $d(df) = d(\partial_x f\,dx + \partial_y f\,dy) = (\partial_x \partial_y f - \partial_y \partial_x f)\,dx \wedge dy = 0$, because partial derivatives commute. So every exact form is closed. Whether every closed form is exact depends on the manifold: yes on the sphere, no on the annulus.

In explainer 02, $\delta^2 = 0$ came from transposing $\partial\partial = 0$. Here $d^2 = 0$ is the equality of mixed partials. Stated precisely, for a smooth manifold $M$, integrating closed forms over smooth cycles gives a natural isomorphism

The isomorphism is a theorem: de Rham and singular cohomology are different functors that agree on smooth manifolds with real coefficients, and the integration pairing identifies them.

A 1-form on the icosphere

The mesh is an icosphere, an icosahedron subdivided twice and projected to $S^2$. Because it is a discretization, the figure approximates each integral by summing edge values along the loop. Each arrow points along its edge with length equal to the signed edge value $\omega(i \to j)$. There are four preset forms.

  • $df_z$: the exterior derivative of $f(p) = p_z$. Arrows point toward the north pole, longest at the equator and vanishing at the poles. Exact, hence closed, hence integrates to zero on any loop.
  • $df_x$: same construction with $f(p) = p_x$. Arrows point toward $+x$. Again exact.
  • bump: $df$ for a Gaussian bump at the north pole. Exact, but visibly local.
  • latitude ($d\theta$ where $\theta = \arctan(y/x)$): closed off the poles. It lives on $S^2 \setminus \{N, S\}$ and is not a smooth form on the whole sphere. At the poles $\theta$ is undefined, and the $d\omega$ inset is nonzero on one triangle at each pole.

Click vertices in order to build a loop (the green path) and click the last vertex again to close it. The gold readout is $\oint_\gamma \omega$. For the three exact presets it reads ≈ 0 on any loop, because the integral of $df$ around a closed path telescopes. For latitude it reads $2\pi$ times the number of times the loop winds around the $z$-axis: zero on a loop that does not go around a pole, and $\pm 2\pi$ on one that does, even a small loop inside one hemisphere. That is how you can tell $d\theta$ is not globally exact.

What the inset says

The value of $d\omega$ on a face is the sum of oriented edge values around its boundary, which is Stokes' theorem for a single triangle. For $df_z$, $df_x$ and bump every triangle is white. For latitude every triangle is white except one at each pole, which reads $\pm 2\pi$. The poles are where $\theta = \arctan(y/x)$ stops being smooth, and the whole turn of $\theta$ around each pole lands in the one triangle there that the jump from $\theta = \pi$ to $-\pi$ passes through.

The noise slider mixes a random non-closed component into $\omega$. The inset lights up everywhere and the loop integral starts to depend on the loop itself. On $S^2$ every loop bounds, because $H_1(S^2) = 0$, so every closed form integrates to zero around every loop. Once $d\omega \ne 0$, the right-hand side no longer vanishes.

The annulus

The sphere can only show the pairing vanishing. To see it nonzero we change manifolds. The flat annulus $A = \{ (x, y) : r_0 \le \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \le r_1 \}$ deformation-retracts onto $S^1$, so $H^1(A;\mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}$ with generator

This form is closed on $A$, since $d(d\theta) = 0$ wherever $\theta$ is locally defined. It is not exact: its integral around the hole is $2\pi$, so no function on $A$ has $d\theta$ as its differential. Up to scale it is the unique generator of $H^1_{\text{dR}}(A)$.

Drag the points of the loop below. The integral $\oint_\gamma d\theta$ depends only on the loop's winding number around the hole: stretching the loop without crossing the inner boundary leaves it unchanged, and crossing changes it by $2\pi$. The integral computes the loop's class in $H_1(A;\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}$, scaled by $2\pi$.

Comparison with the simplicial computation

The combinatorial side gives the same two answers. On a triangulation of $S^2$ (the boundary tetrahedron, or the icosphere), Gaussian elimination on $\delta_1 = \partial_2^T$ gives $H^1(S^2;\mathbb{R}) = 0$. On a band of triangles around the hole of $A$, one independent 1-cocycle survives, so $H^1(A;\mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}$. Its Kronecker pairing with a generator of $H_1$, the evaluation-on-edges pairing from explainer 01, matches the annulus readout up to the factor $2\pi$; the figure gets there by integration instead of evaluation.

A finer mesh makes the figures' integrals more accurate, but it does not prove the comparison. That the two cohomologies agree is the content of de Rham's theorem. The next explainer computes cohomology a third way, by cutting a space into pieces and gluing the answers.
