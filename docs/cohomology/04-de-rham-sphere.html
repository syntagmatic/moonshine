de Rham: differential forms on $S^2$

A different construction gives the same cohomology. Trade cochains-on-simplices for differential forms on a smooth manifold, trade $\delta$ for the exterior derivative $d$, integrate a closed form along a loop, and watch the number depend on the loop's class — not the loop itself.

Cohomology has been a combinatorial story so far. Take a triangulation, dualize the chain complex, transpose the boundary, quotient out. Now we tell it again from the other side. A 1-form on a smooth manifold is a smooth assignment of a covector to each tangent space; locally it looks like $\omega = P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz$. It is closed when $d\omega = 0$ and exact when $\omega = df$ for some function $f$. The quotient $\ker d / \operatorname{im} d$ is de Rham cohomology. The two stories agree:

The de Rham theorem states that the two cohomologies agree, naturally with respect to the integration pairing $\langle [\omega], [\gamma] \rangle = \oint_\gamma \omega$. We do not prove it here. We witness it. On $S^2$ every closed 1-form is exact, so $H^1_{\text{dR}}(S^2) = 0$, and the loop integral of any closed form must be zero. Slip the sphere off, replace it with an annulus, and a single closed-but-not-exact form (the angular differential $d\theta$) reappears as the generator of $H^1$ — its integral around the central hole reads $2\pi$, and around any contractible loop reads zero. The number depends only on which homology class your loop lives in. That is the pairing made tactile.

The exterior derivative

$d$ raises form degree by one and squares to zero:

For a 0-form (a function) $f$, the 1-form $df$ is its differential. For a 1-form $\omega = P\,dx + Q\,dy$, the 2-form $d\omega$ is the familiar curl-like expression

By direct computation $d(df) = d(\partial_x f\,dx + \partial_y f\,dy) = (\partial_y \partial_x f - \partial_x \partial_y f)\,dx \wedge dy = 0$, because partial derivatives commute. So every exact form is closed. The cohomology question is whether the converse holds, and the answer depends on the manifold. On the sphere it does. On the annulus it does not.

Compare with the cochain side. In explainer 02 we built the coboundary $\delta = \partial^T$, the transpose of the simplicial boundary, and saw $\delta^2 = 0$ as $(\partial \partial)^T = 0^T = 0$. The identity is the same, the symbol is different. On the form side $d^2 = 0$ is the equality of mixed partials; on the cochain side it is a transpose. Cohomology is forgiving: one universe is smooth, the other combinatorial, and the quotient does not notice.

The de Rham theorem (stated, not proved). For a smooth manifold $M$, integration of closed forms along smooth cycles induces a natural isomorphism

It is an isomorphism by a theorem, not by definition. De Rham cohomology is not a "limit" or "smoothing" of singular cohomology; they are different functors that happen to agree on smooth manifolds, with real coefficients, via this pairing.

The figure: a 1-form on the icosphere

The mesh is an icosphere — an icosahedron subdivided twice, then projected to $S^2$. It is a discretization, so the figure approximates the integral by summing edge values along the loop, the way Stokes' theorem prescribes. The arrows show the 1-form sampled on edges: each arrow points along its edge, and its length is the signed edge value $\omega(i \to j)$. The presets are four named forms.

  • $df_z$: the exterior derivative of $f(p) = p_z$. Arrows point upward, vanishing at the equator. Exact, hence closed, hence integrates to zero on any loop.
  • $df_x$: same construction with $f(p) = p_x$. Arrows point toward $+x$. Again exact.
  • bump: $df$ for a Gaussian bump at the north pole. Exact, but visibly local.
  • latitude ($d\theta$ where $\theta = \arctan(y/x)$): closed off the poles. This is the form $d\theta$ on $S^2 \setminus \{N, S\}$, which is not a globally smooth form on the sphere — at the poles, $\theta$ is undefined and the form's discrete approximation has a visible branch cut. Its $d\omega$ inset will not be zero on triangles that wrap a pole.

Drag the sphere to rotate it. Click vertices in order to build a loop (the green path); click the last vertex again to close it, or click clear loop to start over. The gold readout below the sphere is the live line integral $\oint_\gamma \omega$. For the three exact presets it reads ≈ 0 for any loop you draw, because the integral of $df$ around any closed path telescopes. For latitude, it reads close to zero on loops that stay in one hemisphere and close to $\pm 2\pi$ on loops that wind around a pole — exactly the failure that says $d\theta$ is not globally exact.

What the inset says

The inset shades each triangle by the value of $d\omega$ on that face — computed as the sum of oriented edge values around the boundary, which is exactly Stokes' theorem for a single triangle. For $df_z$, $df_x$, and bump, every triangle shades to white; the form is genuinely exact and $d\omega = 0$ everywhere. For latitude, the equatorial band is white but the polar caps light up. Those polar triangles encircle the singularity where $\theta = \arctan(y/x)$ ceases to be a smooth function. The reader is seeing the branch cut on the figure.

Slide the noise control: a random non-closed component is mixed into $\omega$ at the chosen scale. The inset lights up everywhere, the loop integral starts depending on the loop, and the pairing fails to descend to homology classes. Closed is precisely the property that makes the integral a function of the loop's class instead of the loop itself.

The integral pairing. Stokes' theorem on a triangulated $S^2$ reads: for any closed form $\omega$ and any 1-cycle $\gamma = \partial \Sigma$ that bounds a 2-chain $\Sigma$,

On $S^2$ every loop bounds (because $H_1(S^2) = 0$), so the integral of any closed form around any loop is zero. The figure approximates that numerically; you will see ≈ 0 with a tag indicating tolerance. The "noise" perturbation breaks the closedness assumption, $d\omega \ne 0$, and Stokes can no longer dispatch the integral to zero.

Switch surface: the annulus

The sphere's $H^1$ is trivial, so the sphere figure is the negative witness — every closed 1-form integrates to zero on every loop. To see a nonzero pairing we change manifolds. The flat annulus $A = \{ (x, y) : r_0 \le \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \le r_1 \}$ deformation-retracts onto $S^1$, so $H^1(A;\mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}$ with generator

This 1-form is closed everywhere on $A$ (a direct computation; $d(d\theta) = 0$ wherever $\theta$ is locally defined), but it is not exact on $A$: integrating $d\theta$ around the central hole gives $2\pi$, and no global function on $A$ has $d\theta$ as its differential. It is the canonical closed-but-not-exact form. The form $d\theta$ is, up to scale, the unique generator of $H^1_{\text{dR}}(A)$.

Drag the polyline below to deform a loop. The integral $\oint_\gamma d\theta$ depends only on the loop's winding number around the central hole. If you stretch the loop without crossing the inner boundary, the integral stays the same; cross over and it jumps by $2\pi$. This is the de Rham theorem made tactile: the loop integral computes the loop's image in $H_1(A;\mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}$, scaled by $2\pi$.

Same answer, different machinery

What we have just done, by hand, is verify two pieces of the de Rham theorem on two manifolds.

  • $H^1_{\text{dR}}(S^2) = 0$: every closed 1-form on $S^2$ is exact, so its integral around every loop is zero. The sphere figure shows it.
  • $H^1_{\text{dR}}(A) = \mathbb{R}$, generated by $d\theta$: a closed-but-not-exact form whose integral counts winding. The annulus figure shows it.

Both answers match what the combinatorial side delivers. On a triangulation of $S^2$ (the boundary tetrahedron, or the icosphere), Gaussian elimination on $\delta_1 = \partial_2^T$ gives $H^1(S^2;\mathbb{R}) = 0$. On a triangulation of $A$ (a band of triangles around the hole), one independent 1-cocycle survives, giving $H^1(A;\mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}$. The pairing of that cocycle against a generator of $H_1$ is its Kronecker pairing, and that number is the same number you see on the gold readout. Two computations of the same invariant.

A reminder about caveats. The icosphere is a discretization — the integral is approximate, and on the latitude preset, the polar branch cut is the form's "non-globally-closed" nature on $S^2 \setminus \{\text{poles}\}$ made visible. The exterior derivative is not the limit of singular cohomology as the mesh refines; they are isomorphic by a theorem (de Rham), not by definition. A finer mesh tightens the approximation; it does not perform the comparison.

What we take with us

  • $d^2 = 0$ is the same identity as $\delta^2 = 0$ on cochains. Equality of mixed partials on one side, $(\partial \partial)^T = 0^T$ on the other.
  • On $S^2$, $H^1_{\text{dR}} = 0$: every closed 1-form is exact, and the loop integral of any closed form is zero. The figure shows it numerically, the de Rham theorem says it formally.
  • On the annulus, $d\theta$ is the canonical generator of $H^1$: closed everywhere, not exact globally; its integral counts winding number times $2\pi$.
  • The pairing $\langle [\omega], [\gamma] \rangle = \oint_\gamma \omega$ is the bridge between de Rham and singular. Same shape as the Kronecker pairing from explainer 01, evaluated by integration instead of evaluation-on-edges.
