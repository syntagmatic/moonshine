The cup product on ℝP² and T²

Part 3 of 6. The cup product turns the graded vector space $H^*$ into a ring. The ring detects shape that Betti numbers cannot — and Alexander–Whitney makes the multiplication a finite, combinatorial table you can fill in cell by cell.

The torus $T^2$ and the wedge $S^1 \vee S^1 \vee S^2$ share Betti numbers $(1, 2, 1)$: one component, two independent 1-cocycles, one 2-cocycle. By that measure they are indistinguishable. Yet they are not homotopy equivalent, and the difference is visible to a single arithmetic move. On $T^2$, the two $H^1$ generators cup to the generator of $H^2$. On the wedge, they cup to zero. Cohomology is a ring; Betti numbers are only its underlying dimensions.

The cup product is computed by Alexander–Whitney: pick a total order on the vertices, take a $(p+q)$-simplex, split it at vertex $v_p$, evaluate $\alpha$ on the front $p$-face and $\beta$ on the back $q$-face, and multiply. Over $\mathbb{Z}/2$ the multiplication is mod 2, so no signs intrude. The interactive figure below animates this splitting, one 2-simplex at a time, on the minimal triangulations of $\mathbb{RP}^2$, $T^2$, $S^1 \vee S^1 \vee S^2$, and $S^2$.

The formula, once

Alexander–Whitney is a combinatorial recipe with one prerequisite: a total order on the vertices. Number the vertices once, write every simplex in ascending order, and split at index $p$. The formula then says nothing more than "$\alpha$ on the front, $\beta$ on the back, multiply."

Two consequences follow immediately. First, the recipe defines $\smile$ at the cochain level: it is a map $C^p \otimes C^q \to C^{p+q}$. Second, a one-line check confirms $\delta(\alpha \smile \beta) = \delta\alpha \smile \beta + \alpha \smile \delta\beta$ — the Leibniz rule for $\delta$ — so $\smile$ descends to cohomology. Over $\mathbb{Z}$ there is a sign $(-1)^{pq}$ between $\alpha \smile \beta$ and $\beta \smile \alpha$; over $\mathbb{Z}/2$ that sign is $1$, so cup products on a triangulated surface really are AND-on-front-and-back.

What the table sees. The 3×3 table for surfaces is mostly degenerate. The $H^0$ row and column are multiplication by the unit and reduce to the identity. The $H^1 \otimes H^1 \to H^2$ cell at position $(1,1)$ is the load-bearing entry: it is the only cell where the topology of the surface, beyond its Betti numbers, can intervene.

What each surface says

The four presets in the dropdown were chosen so the $(1,1)$ cell is the entire story.

  • $\mathbb{RP}^2$. One $H^1$ generator $\alpha$. Computing $\alpha \smile \alpha$ on the 10 triangles of the minimal 6-vertex triangulation gives a 2-cochain that is the generator of $H^2$. So $\alpha^2 \neq 0$. With $\alpha^3 \in H^3 = 0$ this gives $\mathbb{Z}/2[\alpha]/(\alpha^3)$ — the simplest non-exterior cohomology ring on a surface.
  • $T^2$. Two $H^1$ generators $\alpha, \beta$ (meridian and longitude). $\alpha \smile \beta$ produces the generator of $H^2$. Both $\alpha \smile \alpha$ and $\beta \smile \beta$ are exact — they reduce to zero in $H^2$ after subtracting a coboundary. The result is the exterior algebra $\Lambda(\alpha, \beta) = \mathbb{Z}/2[\alpha, \beta]/(\alpha^2, \beta^2)$.
  • $S^1 \vee S^1 \vee S^2$. Same Betti numbers as $T^2$. But the two $H^1$ generators live on the two circle summands, and the only 2-simplices are on the sphere summand. No 2-simplex has both its front edge on the first circle and its back edge on the second. The $(1,1)$ cell is empty: every product is exact, and the ring is the truncated algebra $\mathbb{Z}/2 \langle 1, \alpha, \beta, \gamma \rangle$ with $\alpha\beta = \alpha^2 = \beta^2 = 0$.
  • $S^2$. $H^1 = 0$, so most cells are empty. Only $\gamma \in H^2$ and $1 \in H^0$ exist, giving $\mathbb{Z}/2[\gamma]/(\gamma^2)$. The boring case — but useful as a control.

The $T^2$ vs. wedge contrast is the canonical demonstration that the cohomology ring is a strictly finer invariant than the Betti numbers. The animation makes it visible: on the wedge, the violet "contributes to the cup" flash simply never fires for the $(1,1)$ cell.

Algorithm in two sentences

The lib does it in COH.cup.product(complex, alpha, beta, p, q): iterate the $(p+q)$-simplices, index the front $p$-face and back $q$-face in the precomputed $C^p$ and $C^q$ index tables, AND their values, write into the output cochain. Then COH.cup._expressInBasis solves a Gaussian-elimination system to write the resulting cocycle as a $\mathbb{Z}/2$-linear combination of the chosen $H^{p+q}$ basis modulo $\mathrm{im}\,\delta_{p+q-1}$.

The animation in the right panel walks the same iteration. Each 2-simplex is visited in turn; the front edge highlights in emerald (the $H^1$ color), the back edge in amber (the $H^2$ color), and a triangle that contributes flashes violet. The pace is set to at least 300ms per simplex; the checkbox toggles it down to ~70ms for impatient readers. The prefers-reduced-motion CSS query collapses the animation to a single instantaneous update.

The closing observation

The ring $\mathbb{Z}/2[\alpha]/(\alpha^3)$ that drops out of the $\mathbb{RP}^2$ cup product is a small algebra. It is also the cohomology ring of a much larger space — the Cayley plane $\mathrm{OP}^2$, the 16-real-dimensional compact symmetric space $F_4/\mathrm{Spin}(9)$, carries cohomology $H^*(\mathrm{OP}^2; \mathbb{Z}) = \mathbb{Z}[\alpha]/(\alpha^3)$ with $|\alpha| = 8$. The shape of the algebra is the same; only the degree of the generator shifts. The squishy-thing dossier 36 records this fact alongside the chart formulas and the Severi table; the cohomology ring is the cleanest invariant in its bridge to the magic square. Same ring, different geometry; cohomology composes across enormous gaps.
