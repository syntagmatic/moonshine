From holes to obstructions: why dualize at all

Homology counts holes. Cohomology gives those holes names you can pair against chains and get a number. Why the quotient ker δ / im δ is the right object — by sliding an exact piece in and out and watching the pairing not budge.

The hairy ball, named not proved

You cannot comb a coconut. A continuous tangent vector field on the 2-sphere has to vanish somewhere. That is the hairy ball theorem, and it is the cleanest topological fact you can state without using any topology: a function valued in tangent spaces on $S^2$ must hit zero. Homology cannot say why. Counting holes — one component, no loops, one void — does not produce the right kind of object to put in the way of a vector field.

The right kind of object is a cohomology class: a label attached to chains, glued together algebraically, that refuses to be zero. The Euler class of the tangent bundle of $S^2$ lives in $H^2(S^2; \mathbb{Z})$, evaluates to $2$ on the fundamental class, and that nonzero number is the obstruction. We are not going to prove this, here or anywhere in the series. We are going to do something smaller and more honest: build the smallest example of how naming-by-pairing works, and convince ourselves that $\ker \delta / \mathrm{im}\, \delta$ is the right quotient. The Euler class is the kind of thing that comes out the other side. The annulus comes first.

The reader who has been through the Topological Data Analysis series has already built simplicial complexes, boundary operators $\partial$, and homology $H_k = \ker \partial_k / \mathrm{im}\, \partial_{k+1}$ over $\mathbb{Z}/2$. The whole construction of cohomology, on the algebra side, is one move: take the transpose.

Dualize the chain complex

Recall the setup. A simplicial complex has chain groups $C_k$ — over $\mathbb{Z}/2$, the formal sums of $k$-simplices — and a boundary map $\partial_k : C_k \to C_{k-1}$ that records "what bounds what". The identity $\partial^2 = 0$ is the reason holes can be defined: cycles ($\ker \partial$) modulo boundaries ($\mathrm{im}\, \partial$) is $H_k$, and this is a topological invariant of the underlying space.

The cochain group is its dual:

A $k$-cochain $\alpha$ is a $\mathbb{Z}/2$-valued function on $k$-simplices. Over $\mathbb{Z}/2$ this just means a labelling: each $k$-simplex carries a $0$ or a $1$. The space $C^k$ has the same dimension as $C_k$, but a different job: chains are things that get evaluated, cochains are the things that do the evaluating. Their pairing is the Kronecker pairing:

Mod $2$, this counts the simplices on which both $\alpha$ and $c$ are $1$, then takes the parity. It is the most basic thing you can do with a function and a formal sum: sum the function over the support.

The coboundary $\delta_k : C^k \to C^{k+1}$ is defined to make this pairing well-behaved. It is forced by one rule — Stokes' theorem in its combinatorial form:

Read this from right to left: the coboundary $\delta$ is the transpose of $\partial$. If $\partial_{k+1}$ has matrix $B$ in the simplex bases, then $\delta_k$ has matrix $B^T$. Nothing else has changed. Same complex, same data, just the indices swapped. The TDA series built $\partial$; now read off its transpose row by row.

So $\delta$ acts on a vertex function by summing its values around an edge's endpoints, on an edge cochain by summing around a triangle's boundary, and so on. Over $\mathbb{Z}/2$ all the signs in the unoriented theory collapse to addition, which is the only reason this essay can use plain XOR everywhere.

Why the transpose is the whole construction. The Kronecker pairing $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ identifies $C^k$ with $\mathrm{Hom}(C_k, \mathbb{Z}/2)$ as vector spaces. Stokes is the demand that this identification commutes with the differentials. That demand has one solution: $\delta = \partial^T$.

Two consequences land in the same line. First, a closed cochain pairs to zero with every boundary, so the pairing $\langle \alpha, \cdot \rangle$ descends from $C_k$ to a function on cycles modulo boundaries — that is, to a function on $H_k$. because $\langle \delta f, \gamma \rangle = \langle f, \partial \gamma \rangle = \langle f, 0 \rangle = 0$. The pairing on cycles is blind to exact shifts. Two cocycles that differ by a coboundary give the same pairing on every cycle, so they should be the same thing as far as the pairing is concerned.

The annulus pairing demo

The flat annulus is the smallest space where this story has any bite. It deformation-retracts to a circle, so $H_1(\text{annulus}; \mathbb{Z}/2) = \mathbb{Z}/2$ — one nontrivial loop class. The complex below comes from COH.tri.annulus(8, 16): an inner ring of $8$ vertices, an outer ring of $16$, with triangles spanning between. There are two visible loops, the inner boundary $\gamma_\text{in}$ and the outer boundary $\gamma_\text{out}$. Both represent the same $H_1$ class — you can homotope one to the other through the strip — so for any cocycle $\alpha$ we must have $\langle \alpha, \gamma_\text{in} \rangle = \langle \alpha, \gamma_\text{out} \rangle$. That equality is what the two giant readouts on the right will display.

They do not move.

That is the whole point. The figure is the slogan made physical. $\delta f$ is exactly the thing you cannot detect with a cycle pairing.

An anchoring caveat. The flat embedding above is a drawing convention; the algebra is intrinsic to the simplicial complex. The annulus does not know which loop is "inner" — the names are ours, and the equality $\langle \alpha, \gamma_\text{in} \rangle = \langle \alpha, \gamma_\text{out} \rangle$ depends only on the fact that both loops bound the same chain when expanded into the strip.

What just happened, algebraically

The slider sweeps $\alpha$ over a coset: $\alpha + \mathrm{im}\, \delta_0$. Every element of that coset has the same image in $H^1$. The two readouts are functions on $H^1$, not on $C^1$ — they only know about the equivalence class. The reader is staring at a quotient.

The way to verify, on the page: when you toggle a single edge by clicking, you are changing the class — usually. Most edges are not closed on their own, and a few are coboundaries (try clicking only the two edges that meet at one inner vertex; the slider effect was already showing you that this combination is exact). The cocycles modulo coboundaries form a $\mathbb{Z}/2$, and the two readouts always agree on that quotient.

A cocycle is what a cochain becomes when you stop caring about exact shifts. Two cochains in the same cohomology class produce the same pairing on every cycle. The slider is the visual proof of that statement on the smallest example where it has any content.

Where this leaves us

The next essay computes $H^*$ of a closed surface — the standard $7$-vertex triangulation of the torus — and reads off $H^0 = \mathbb{Z}/2$, $H^1 = (\mathbb{Z}/2)^2$, $H^2 = \mathbb{Z}/2$ by literally taking the transpose of $\partial$ and reducing. There is nothing else to do; the rest is bookkeeping. The cup product, which turns these graded groups into a ring, is the third essay and the move that makes the Euler-class story above precise.

What we take with us

  • A $k$-cochain is a $\mathbb{Z}/2$-valued function on $k$-simplices. Same dimension as $C_k$, opposite job.
  • The coboundary $\delta_k$ is the transpose of $\partial_{k+1}$. Stokes — $\langle \delta \alpha, b \rangle = \langle \alpha, \partial b \rangle$ — forces it.
  • A cocycle is a cochain that vanishes on every boundary. Equivalently, $\delta \alpha = 0$.
  • An exact cochain is one that comes from a vertex function via $\delta$. Equivalently, $\alpha = \delta f$.
  • $H^k = \ker \delta_k / \mathrm{im}\, \delta_{k-1}$ is the right quotient because the Kronecker pairing on $H_k$ is well-defined exactly on this quotient.
  • Cohomology is the algebra of obstructions: a class is a number you can pair with a chain to get a topological invariant. Next, read off $H^*(T^2; \mathbb{Z}/2)$ by taking a single transpose.
