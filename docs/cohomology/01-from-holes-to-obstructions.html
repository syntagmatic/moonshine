From holes to obstructions: why dualize at all

Homology counts holes. Cohomology gives those holes names you can pair against chains to get a number. Sliding an exact piece in and out without moving the pairing shows why the quotient ker δ / im δ is the right object.

The hairy ball, named not proved

You cannot comb a coconut: a continuous tangent vector field on the 2-sphere has to vanish somewhere. That is the hairy ball theorem. Counting holes does not explain it. One component, no loops and one void are just numbers, and a count of holes is not the kind of object that can stand in the way of a vector field. (Homology can prove the theorem, through the degree of the antipodal map, but only by going beyond counting.)

The right kind of object is a cohomology class, a label on chains that refuses to be zero. The Euler class of the tangent bundle of $S^2$ lives in $H^2(S^2; \mathbb{Z})$ and evaluates to $2$ on the fundamental class, and that nonzero number is the obstruction. This series does not prove that. This article builds the smallest example of naming by pairing, on an annulus, and shows why $\ker \delta / \mathrm{im}\, \delta$ is the right quotient.

Homology is built from a simplicial complex, its boundary operators $\partial$, and the quotient $H_k = \ker \partial_k / \mathrm{im}\, \partial_{k+1}$ over $\mathbb{Z}/2$. Cohomology, on the algebra side, takes the transpose.

Dualize the chain complex

A simplicial complex has chain groups $C_k$, which over $\mathbb{Z}/2$ are the formal sums of $k$-simplices, and boundary maps $\partial_k : C_k \to C_{k-1}$. Because $\partial^2 = 0$, cycles ($\ker \partial$) modulo boundaries ($\mathrm{im}\, \partial$) make sense, and that quotient $H_k$ is a topological invariant of the underlying space.

The annulus pairing demo

The flat annulus is the smallest space where this has any bite. It deformation-retracts to a circle, so $H_1(\text{annulus}; \mathbb{Z}/2) = \mathbb{Z}/2$. The complex below comes from COH.tri.annulus(8, 16): an inner ring of $8$ vertices, an outer ring of $16$, with triangles between them. The inner boundary $\gamma_\text{in}$ and the outer boundary $\gamma_\text{out}$ represent the same $H_1$ class, since one can be pushed to the other through the strip, so every cocycle $\alpha$ must have $\langle \alpha, \gamma_\text{in} \rangle = \langle \alpha, \gamma_\text{out} \rangle$. The two large readouts on the right show both numbers.

Toggle some edges. The two readouts usually agree, and when they don't, the triangle strip has at least one red cell: $\alpha$ is not closed, so the pairing on homology classes is not defined and the readouts are only raw evaluations on two particular loops. Now reset and click "α = H¹ generator", which loads a closed, non-exact cocycle computed from ker δ / im δ. Both readouts show $1$. Over $\mathbb{Z}/2$ they have to, because the loop around the hole generates $H_1$ and the pairing between $H^1$ and $H_1$ is perfect. Drag the slider: edges flash as their values flip, and the readouts do not move. A $\delta f$ is exactly what a cycle pairing cannot detect.

The flat drawing is a convention; the algebra belongs to the simplicial complex. The annulus does not know which loop is "inner". The equality $\langle \alpha, \gamma_\text{in} \rangle = \langle \alpha, \gamma_\text{out} \rangle$ holds because the two loops together bound the strip.

What just happened, algebraically

The slider sweeps $\alpha$ over the coset $\alpha + \mathrm{im}\, \delta_0$, and every element of that coset has the same image in $H^1$. The readouts are functions on $H^1$, not on $C^1$.

Clicking a single edge usually changes the class, or breaks closedness. A single edge is never a coboundary, but clicking every edge at one vertex (five of them for an inner vertex) toggles $\alpha$ by $\delta$ of that vertex's indicator, which is exact, and the readouts stay put. The cocycles modulo coboundaries form a $\mathbb{Z}/2$, and on that quotient the two readouts always agree. Two cochains in the same cohomology class give the same pairing on every cycle.
