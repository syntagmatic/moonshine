The Single Tree

Chapter 1 of 6. A tree is grown by picking, at each node, the question that best separates the data, then recursing. What counts as best depends on the target, and choosing greedily has a cost.

A decision tree is a flowchart. Each internal node asks a yes/no question about one feature and sends the row left or right; each leaf holds a prediction. To grow one, put every row at the root, find the question that separates them best, split, and recurse until the groups are pure, small, or deep enough.

How is a split scored?

A split on one feature sends rows below a threshold left and the rest right. Its score is the gain: the parent's impurity minus the children's, weighted by size, I(parent) − (nL/n)·I(L) − (nR/n)·I(R). Misclassification error is the share of rows outside the majority class. Gini is the chance of mislabelling a row if you guess labels in proportion to the group. Entropy is, roughly, the number of yes/no questions needed to learn a row's label. All three vanish on a pure group and peak at 50/50.

Why not count mistakes?

Take 800 rows, 400 of each class. Split A makes two children of 300 against 100. Split B makes one child of 200 positives against 400 negatives and one pure child of 200 positives. Each child predicts its majority, and both splits get 200 rows wrong, so misclassification calls them tied.

Figure 3. Every split of that 800-row parent that makes exactly 200 mistakes, indexed by how many negatives go right. Drag the handle or use the arrow keys. The height is the gain as a share of the parent's impurity; levels differ between measures, shapes are what compare. Misclassification is flat; Gini and entropy are lowest at the balanced split A and highest at the ends, B and its mirror B′, where one child is pure.

Misclassification is linear on each side of 50/50, so the children's weighted impurity is just the mistake count over n. Gini and entropy are strictly concave: the chord in Figure 1 drops furthest when one child reaches an end, where the curve hits zero. CART grows trees with Gini and C4.5 with an entropy-based gain; misclassification error is more often used for pruning (chapter 2).

When the target is a number

For a numeric target, swap impurity for variance: the split that most reduces the size-weighted squared error wins, and each leaf predicts its mean. The fit is a staircase. A step cannot slope, and beyond the training range the tree repeats the edge leaf's mean.

Growing the tree greedily

Growing is mechanical: at each node, try every feature and every threshold, take the highest gain, and recurse until the children are pure, too small, or at a depth limit.

What does greed cost?

On XOR data a point is red when exactly one of x and y exceeds 4. Any single cut leaves both sides near half red, so every first split has a Gini gain near zero and sampling noise picks the winner, often a sliver near an edge. The best depth-2 tree cuts near x = 4, then near y = 4, and is exact. Greedy growth never looks ahead to find it.

Building an optimal tree is NP-complete (Hyafil and Rivest, 1976), so standard algorithms accept greed and compensate: pruning in chapter 2, many trees in chapters 3 and 4. Chapter 2 starts with geometry: every split is horizontal or vertical.
