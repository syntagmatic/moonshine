The Single Tree

Chapter 1 of 6. At each node, pick the question that separates the data best, then recurse. What counts as best depends on whether the target is a class or a number.

A decision tree is a flowchart. Each internal node asks a yes/no question about one feature ("is age greater than 40?") and sends the row left or right, and each leaf holds a prediction. The tree is built top-down: put all the data at the root, find the question that separates it best, split, and recurse on each side until the groups are small enough or pure enough.

Picking a split

Take a row of positives and negatives. A good split leaves each side mostly one kind. The obvious score counts mistakes: give each group its majority class and count the rows that disagree. This is misclassification error, and it has a flaw we will get to shortly.

Gini asks: if you pick a random row from the group and assign it a class weighted by the group's composition, what's the chance you get it wrong? Entropy asks: how many yes/no questions are needed on average to guess the label? Both peak at 50/50 and hit zero on a pure group. Their curved shapes prefer splits that produce pure leaves.

When the target is a number

Swap "how mixed is this bucket?" for "how spread out are these numbers?" and the algorithm carries over. The scoring rule becomes variance reduction: pick the split that shrinks the average squared distance from the mean. Each leaf's prediction is the mean of its numbers.

Why misclassification fails

Take 800 rows, 400 positives and 400 negatives, and two candidate splits. Split A gives two children that are each 300 of one class and 100 of the other (75/25). Split B gives one mixed child with 200/400 and one pure child with 200/0.

Split B settles 200 rows for good. Yet if each child predicts its majority class, both splits get 200 of 800 wrong, so misclassification calls them tied. Gini and entropy break the tie. Their curves sit strictly above straight lines, which gives a split with a pure child a bonus that the flat-sided triangle of misclassification can't register. Every mainstream tree algorithm uses one of the two.

What the staircase cannot do

A regression tree predicts with flat segments. With enough leaves it approximates any reasonable function, but it cannot produce a slope inside a leaf, since every row there gets the same number, and it cannot extrapolate. A query outside the training range gets the edge leaf's average, the nearest value the tree has seen.

In exchange, a tree handles mixed data types without preprocessing and trains in seconds, and ensembles of trees dominate tabular-data leaderboards.

The greedy algorithm

With a split score in hand, growing a tree is mechanical: at each node, try every feature and every threshold, pick the split with the highest gain, recurse on the children, stop when they are pure, too small, or at a depth limit. Figure 2. Left: the data with the tree's decision regions. Right: the tree; each leaf shows how many training points reach it, and clicking a leaf highlights its rectangle. "Step" adds one level of greedy Gini splits. "Best depth-2 tree" replaces the greedy tree with the depth-2 tree of highest training accuracy, found by trying every root split and the best split under each side of it.

The greedy algorithm doesn't look ahead, and the XOR dataset shows what that costs. Its class depends on both coordinates together: a point is red when exactly one of x and y is above 4. Any single vertical or horizontal cut leaves both sides close to half red, so every candidate first split has a Gini gain near zero, and the one greedy picks is decided by sampling noise. Often it is a cut near the edge that peels off a few points. The best depth-2 tree cuts near x = 4 and then near y = 4 and classifies every point correctly. Over 40 random samples of this dataset, the greedy depth-2 tree averaged 71% training accuracy, and "New XOR sample" draws more. Greedy growth gets to 100% eventually with more depth, through a larger and messier tree.

Finding the globally optimal tree is NP-hard in general (Hyafil and Rivest, 1976), so the standard algorithms accept the greedy heuristic. The rest of this series is largely about making up for it: pruning in chapter 2, and combining many trees in chapters 3 and 4.
