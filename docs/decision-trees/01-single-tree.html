The Single Tree

Chapter 1 of 8. Every tree is built the same way: at each node, pick the question that separates the data best, and recurse. "Best" is trickier than it looks, and the trick that makes it work is different for classes than for numbers.

A decision tree is a flowchart. Each internal node asks a yes/no question about one feature ("is age greater than 40?") and sends the row left or right. Each leaf is a final prediction. You build the tree top-down: start with all your data at the root, find the question that separates it best, split, recurse on each side, stop when each group is small enough or pure enough.

Everything else in this series follows from this. Before we move on, we need two things: a rule for scoring splits, and a sense of what the tree's outputs look like. Both depend on what you're predicting, a class or a number.

Picking a split

Imagine a row of positives and negatives. You want to split them into two groups with a single threshold, and each group to contain mostly one kind. Your first instinct is probably to count mistakes: pick the majority in each group, count how many rows got the wrong label, and minimise. This is called misclassification error, and it's a real scoring rule. It's also, it turns out, bad.

Two other measures fix the problem. Gini asks: if I pick a row at random from the group and assign it a random class weighted by the group's composition, what's the chance I get it wrong? Entropy asks: how many yes/no questions, on average, would I need to guess the label? Both peak at 50/50, both hit zero on a pure group. But unlike misclassification, they have curved shapes, and that curvature makes them prefer splits that produce pure leaves.

The main interactive is about feeling this difference directly.

When the target is a number

Swap "how mixed is this bucket?" for "how spread out are these numbers?" and the same algorithm works. The scoring rule becomes variance reduction: pick the split that makes the average squared distance from the mean shrink most. Each leaf's prediction collapses to the mean of its numbers.

The result is a staircase. Every leaf outputs a single value, so the tree's prediction is a patchwork of flat segments. More splits produce more steps. A fine enough staircase can approximate any reasonable pattern, but the tree literally cannot produce a slope or a curve, because every leaf is one number.

Why misclassification fails

Take 800 rows, evenly split: 400 positives, 400 negatives. Consider two candidate splits.

  • Split A, both children are 300 of one class and 100 of the other (75/25).
  • Split B, one child has 200/400 (mixed), the other has 200/0 (perfectly pure).

Intuitively, Split B is better: it locks in a final answer for 200 rows. But if you count mistakes (the majority class gets everyone) both splits get 200 wrong out of 800. Misclassification declares them tied. Try the preset above to see it happen in real time. Gini and entropy break the tie correctly because their curves sit strictly above straight lines, giving pure-leaf splits an additional bonus the flat triangle of misclassification can't see.

A split that isolates a pure leaf is strictly better than one that doesn't, even when they have the same weighted error rate. Gini and entropy can see this because they curve downward. Misclassification can't because it's a straight triangle. This is why every mainstream tree algorithm uses Gini or entropy, not the thing you'd reach for first.

What the staircase cannot do

In regression mode, the tree's prediction is always a set of flat segments. It can approximate any reasonable function if you give it enough leaves, but there are a few things it structurally cannot do.

It cannot produce a slope inside a leaf, every row in the same leaf gets the same number. It cannot draw a smooth curve. And most importantly, it cannot extrapolate. Ask it for a prediction outside the range of your training data, and it returns the edge leaf's average, literally the closest value it has ever seen. If the real world extrapolates (bigger houses cost more, even past the biggest house you've seen), a tree simply won't keep up.

That's the price of the staircase. Everything else works. You can throw mixed data types at a tree without preprocessing. You can train one in seconds. And as we'll see, when you stack many of them, you get something that dominates tabular-data leaderboards.

The greedy algorithm

With a split-scoring function in hand, the whole algorithm writes itself. At each node, try every feature, try every threshold, pick the split with the highest gain. Recurse on the two children. Stop when children are pure, too small, or you've hit a depth limit.

Notice what this doesn't do: look ahead. A mediocre split now might set up two great splits two levels down, but the greedy algorithm will never find out, because it won't pick the mediocre split. This shortsightedness is a real cost. It's not fixed by tweaking, finding the globally optimal tree is NP-hard, proven in 1976 by Hyafil and Rivest. Every standard tree algorithm is a workaround for that one hard fact, and chapter 8 closes the series by showing when and how modern optimisation methods finally beat the greedy heuristic.

Takeaways

  • Trees are built top-down, greedily. At every node, pick the best split, commit, recurse. Never backtrack.
  • For classes, Gini or entropy, not misclassification. The curved measures correctly prefer splits that isolate pure leaves. Gini and entropy agree on the optimal split over 99% of the time; the choice between them is mostly stylistic.
  • For numbers, variance reduction. Pick the split that most reduces the spread around the mean. Every leaf predicts the mean of its training rows.
  • Regression trees predict staircases. Flat segments only, no slopes, no curves, no extrapolation. It's a feature of the model class, not a bug.
  • The greedy algorithm isn't optimal. The optimal tree is intractable in the worst case. Everything in this series is either a workaround for that fact or, in chapter 8, a challenge to it.
