Decision Trees

Eight chapters on one of the oldest and most useful models in machine learning. Trees are flowcharts made of yes-or-no questions. Stack a thousand of them together and, forty years after their invention, they still win most tabular-data competitions.

A single decision tree picks questions greedily, cuts the input space into rectangles, and predicts by averaging whatever data lands in each rectangle. That's the whole basic idea, and it already gives you four chapters of interesting consequences. Then in chapter three we start stacking trees — first by voting, then by boosting — and that's where the modern empirical power of the method comes from.

Chapter five asks how to read a forest's mind: if a thousand trees vote on your prediction, can you figure out which features mattered? Chapter six upgrades the model itself: instead of predicting outcomes, train the tree to estimate what would change if you intervened. Chapter seven shows two places trees go when they leave tabular data — softened for neural networks, or grown one rollout at a time for game search. And chapter eight tours the research frontier, where globally optimal trees are finally practical and a forty-year assumption is being quietly overturned.

Every chapter has one rich interactive designed to let you feel the idea: drag thresholds, toggle modes, watch ensembles converge, step through boosting one tree at a time. The prose stays short; the playing is where the depth lives.

Two ideas, everywhere

The split
Every tree is built from the same move: at each node, pick the question that separates the data best. Trees split on rankings, not distances, so you never need to normalise your features. Log, cents, quantiles — the tree doesn't care.
The ensemble
One tree is jumpy. A thousand trees are stable. Different ensemble recipes attack different kinds of error — bagging cancels variance, random forests force disagreement, boosting fixes bias by building trees that correct each other.
Where they meet
A thousand trees aren't readable the way one tree is. Most of the modern literature is about getting readability back: SHAP decomposes predictions into exact contributions, causal forests answer what would change under intervention.
Where they fight
The field was built on the assumption that globally optimal trees are intractable and would overfit anyway. New optimisation methods have broken both halves of that. The greedy paradigm that built the field is being overtaken.
