Trees Unbound

Chapter 7 of 8. Two places trees go when they leave the tabular-fitting context. One softens the decisions so the tree can be trained by gradient descent. The other grows the tree through simulation, building a search algorithm instead of a predictive model.

Every tree so far has been fitted to tabular data. A dataset of rows with features and labels came in; a flowchart of greedy splits came out. That's one way of using trees, and it's where most of the practical power lives. But there are at least two other things trees can be, in very different contexts.

The first is a soft tree: replace the hard yes/no of a split with a smooth probability, and the whole tree becomes differentiable. This opens the door to training trees with gradient descent and stacking them into neural networks. The second is Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS): build a tree dynamically by running simulations, never fitting any data at all. This is the algorithm behind AlphaGo. Both stretch what a tree is.

Soft trees: replacing hard splits with sigmoids

A normal tree split is "is x greater than 5? go left", a discontinuous, discrete decision. There's no gradient. Change x by a little bit and the routing either stays the same or suddenly flips.

A soft tree does something different. Instead of routing a row down exactly one path, it routes it down all paths, each weighted by the probability that its sigmoid gates would have sent it there. At a split with threshold 5, a row with x = 4.9 goes left with probability ~52% and right with probability ~48%. The final prediction is the weighted expectation over all leaves.

Everything is now smooth. Change x by a little and the routing probabilities shift a little and the prediction shifts a little. The tree is a continuous, differentiable function of its inputs. You can train it with SGD. You can put a neural net in front of it to extract features. You can stack trees into deeper architectures. Deep Neural Decision Forests (Kontschieder 2015), NODE, TabNet, all variations on this theme.

The uncomfortable result

Here's where soft trees get their reputation. On paper, a soft tree combines the best of both worlds: trees' inductive bias for tabular data, neural networks' end-to-end trainability. In practice, hard trees usually win.

Two comprehensive 2022 benchmarks made this pointedly clear. Grinsztajn and colleagues at Inria evaluated 45 tabular datasets; XGBoost won 36 of them. Shwartz-Ziv and Armon at Intel ran 11 datasets; XGBoost won 8. In both cases the comparison was against state-of-the-art tabular-specific neural architectures, not old baselines. The hard version of the algorithm, thirty years old, kept winning.

The most plausible explanation: tabular data often has sharp, irregular decision boundaries that don't look anything like the smooth functions neural networks are biased toward. A thousand hard splits can stair-step an arbitrary boundary with surprising accuracy. A smooth function tries to approximate the same boundary and ends up over-smoothing the jagged edges. For images and text, where the underlying function is smooth, neural networks win. On tabular data, the hard-split inductive bias matches reality.

Soft trees remain useful for specific scenarios: end-to-end differentiable pipelines, architectures that want interpretable gating, cases where you need gradient-based fine-tuning of a pretrained model. But "just use a soft tree instead of XGBoost" is not the default answer it seemed to promise.

MCTS: trees that search instead of fit

Soft trees were still predictive models trained on data. MCTS is something entirely different: it builds a tree to search through possible futures in a simulator. There's no training data. There's no loss function. There's a game (or other sequential decision problem), a simulator that tells you what happens after each choice, and a structure called Monte Carlo Tree Search that builds a tree of choices asymmetrically by repeated trial.

MCTS is the algorithm behind AlphaGo and most modern game AI. Forty years old, still state of the art, still used.

The four-step loop

MCTS keeps a growing tree in memory. At the start, it has just the current game state as its root. Then it repeats four steps, thousands of times.

  1. Select. Walk down the tree, at each step choosing the child that looks most worth exploring further.
  2. Expand. When you reach a leaf of the tree, add one of its unexplored successors as a new node.
  3. Simulate. From the new node, play out a random game to the end. Record win or loss.
  4. Backpropagate. Walk back up the path, updating each node's visit count and running score.

After thousands of iterations, the promising branches of the tree are deeply expanded while the hopeless ones are stubby. Pick the root's child with the highest visit count, that's the move.

The trick: UCT balances exploitation and exploration

The critical choice is step 1, which child to descend into. Greedy would say "the one with the highest average win rate." But a child with one lucky rollout at 100% can't be trusted more than one with 100 rollouts at 51%. You need to balance exploitation (trust the current average) against exploration (try under-tested branches).

The UCT score does this. Each child's score is its current average reward plus a bonus for having been visited few times: UCT = W/N + C · √(ln(N_parent) / N). The constant C tunes how adventurous the search is.

AlphaGo and what came next

Pure MCTS works, but it's slow on complex games. Each rollout is a long random walk, and random walks through Go don't produce useful estimates, the random game usually ends badly for whoever made the random moves.

AlphaGo's innovation was to plug in neural networks where the slow parts were. Instead of a random rollout, a value network (trained on expert games) estimated position strength directly. Instead of pure UCT for selection, a policy network added a learned prior over moves. MCTS became the outer loop; deep learning filled in the inner ones.

AlphaZero went further: no expert games. Train the policy and value networks from scratch using only self-play with MCTS. The algorithm learned superhuman chess, Go, and shogi from nothing but the rules. The outer structure (the four-step MCTS loop) is what stayed the same across all these systems. It's one of the most robust ideas in game AI.

A note on "trees" as a word

It's worth noting how differently the word "tree" is being used across the chapters. In chapters 1–6, trees are predictive models, flowcharts fitted to data, used to map inputs to predictions. In MCTS, the tree is a search structure, a data structure representing future possibilities, built to decide what to do next. The algorithms share almost nothing beyond the word. The bridge here is only that both build up a branching structure one choice at a time.

Soft trees sit somewhere between. They're predictive models like chapters 1–6, but their "training" is gradient-based rather than greedy. And MCTS is closer to a search algorithm like A*. The three topics in this chapter (fit-with-gradients, search-with-rollouts) are what trees look like when the mainstream tabular context is loosened.

Takeaways

  • Soft trees replace hard splits with sigmoids. Routing probabilities instead of routing decisions. The tree becomes differentiable and stackable into neural architectures.
  • But hard trees still win on tabular data. Benchmarks keep confirming it, the hard-split inductive bias matches irregular tabular boundaries.
  • MCTS builds trees to search, not to fit. No training data, no loss function. Four-step loop: select by UCT, expand, simulate, backpropagate.
  • UCT balances exploration and exploitation. Pick branches with high win rate, but with a bonus for under-tested ones. The tuning constant C controls the balance.
  • AlphaGo combined MCTS with neural networks. Learned policy and value networks filled in the slow rollout step. MCTS remained the outer loop.
