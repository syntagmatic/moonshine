Reading the Forest

Chapter 5 of 6. A single tree can be read by looking at it; a thousand cannot. Feature importance shows what the model relies on overall, and Shapley values explain a single prediction.

A boosted model or a forest can't be read split by split: its prediction is a sum or average of hundreds of trees. This chapter reads one two ways: which features it leans on overall, and how one prediction divides among them.

What does split-based importance add up?

Sum a feature's information gain over the nodes that use it, weighting each by the share of rows passing through, and you get mean decrease in impurity (MDI), scikit-learn's feature_importances_ for trees. It measures how thoroughly a feature cut up the training rows, and a unique ID cuts them up completely.

Gain ratio (C4.5) divides by the split's own entropy, log2 N for the ID: a cut, not a knockout, leaving it level with age and ahead of job and city.

An importance ranking led by something that looks like an ID or a timestamp is a reason to check for leakage.

Does the forest need the feature?

Continuous features get a milder version of the trap. A deep tree keeps splitting on noise, and each such split still gains on the rows it was fit to (Strobl et al. 2007). Breiman's (2001) permutation importance instead shuffles one column in held-out rows and measures how far accuracy falls.

How do you split one prediction among features?

Shapley (1953) answered this for cooperative games: average each player's marginal contribution over every order in which the players could join. Here the players are features, and one that hasn't joined sits at a baseline value. It is the only split meeting a few fairness axioms that sums exactly to prediction minus baseline, and it needs the model on all 2p feature subsets.

What does "absent" mean inside a tree?

A trained tree has no reference house. TreeSHAP (Lundberg, Erion and Lee 2018) treats an absent feature by following both children wherever it is tested, weighted by the training rows down each side, and computes the Shapley average over all subsets in O(TLD2) time for T trees with L leaves and depth D, instead of 2p evaluations.

The bars enumerate all 8 subsets under this rule: TreeSHAP's path-dependent values, computed the slow way.

What SHAP does not tell you

SHAP explains the model. If it learned a proxy, SHAP credits the proxy faithfully: a large value says the prediction depends on the feature, not that changing it in the world would change the outcome. The values are also relative to a baseline; move the reference house and every bar in Figure 3 moves. The causal question needs a different kind of tree, the subject of the last chapter.
