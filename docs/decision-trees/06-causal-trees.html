Causal Trees

Chapter 6 of 6. Every tree so far has asked "what will happen?" Causal trees ask "what would change if I intervened?" Answering that changes the split criterion, and it also means splitting the data in half.

An online store sends a 20%-off promo code to the customers a model flags as likely buyers. That group includes people who would have bought anyway. The customers who matter are the persuadables, whose purchase depends on the code, and a model of who buys does not find them.

Why standard trees can't answer this

A predictive tree looks for splits that make the outcome homogeneous within each leaf. Here the splits should separate people who respond differently to the treatment. But for any one customer only one of "with code" and "without code" is observed, so there is no individual treatment effect to train on.

The causal split criterion

Athey and Imbens (2016) replace outcome purity with a different target: splits whose two children have different average treatment effects.

For each candidate split, estimate the uplift in each child as (treated mean) − (control mean). A good split separates a high-uplift group from a low-uplift one, say +25% vs. +2%. The score rewards the spread of the children's uplift estimates around the parent's, weighted by child size, which is heterogeneity of the effect across leaves. Within a leaf the effect should be as uniform as possible, since the leaf reports one number for everyone in it. The full criterion also subtracts a penalty for leaves whose estimates would be noisy.

A predictive tree asks "who has high Y?" A causal tree asks "who has high Y-treated − Y-control?"

The double-dipping problem

Using the same data to pick the split and to estimate the uplift in each leaf inflates the estimate. The split search tries hundreds of cuts and keeps those where treated and control happen to differ most in this sample, and part of that difference is noise that will not repeat. Confidence intervals break too, because their standard errors assume the leaf was chosen independently of the data used to estimate it.

Honest estimation

Athey and Imbens split the training data in half. The first half grows the tree: it picks the splits and fixes the structure, without ever seeing the second half. The second half is then dropped into those leaves to estimate each leaf's average treatment effect, and the structure is not touched again. Since the second half played no part in choosing the leaves, its estimates are unbiased for them and ordinary confidence intervals apply.

Figure 2 runs both procedures. On the first half it grows a depth-2 causal tree with the criterion above (each leaf needs at least 10 treated and 10 control customers) and picks the leaf with the largest estimated uplift, the one a marketer would target. It then reports that leaf's uplift three ways: estimated on the same half, estimated on the other half, and the true value, which is known exactly because the data are simulated. With the default settings, the same-half estimate overshoots the chosen leaf's true uplift by roughly 30 percentage points on average, and its nominal 95% interval covers the truth in well under half of the datasets. The other-half estimate is noisier on any one dataset but centred on the truth, and its interval covers it close to 95% of the time. Click through a few example datasets: often the leaf that looked best is not the persuadable corner at all, and its true uplift is the background +8pp. Set the persuadable uplift to zero and the same-half estimate still reports a large effect in whichever leaf looked best. More rows per half shrink the noise in each estimate, but the tree also makes smaller leaves possible, so the selection bias falls slowly.

The cost is half your data for each purpose. Causal forests (Wager and Athey, 2018) average many honest trees, each grown on a different subsample, which recovers much of the lost precision.

Caveats

The uplift in each leaf is a difference between treated and control customers, and it measures the effect of the code only if the two groups are comparable within the leaf. A randomized A/B test guarantees that. If treatment was assigned by feature (salespeople gave discounts to interested-looking people), the difference mixes the effect with who got selected.

Randomization is not the only way to get comparable groups. Causal forests are also used on observational data under the assumption that, given the recorded features, treatment is as good as random (unconfoundedness). The generalized random forest implementation of Athey, Tibshirani and Wager (2019) first subtracts estimates of each customer's expected outcome and probability of being treated, a step its authors call local centering, and then splits on what remains. The assumption does the work there, and it cannot be checked from the data alone.

When the treatment was randomized, or the assumption is credible, a causal tree can show where the treatment works, such as which customers the promo code actually persuades.
