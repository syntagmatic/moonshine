Causal Trees

Chapter 6 of 8. Every tree so far has asked "what will happen?" Causal trees ask a subtly different question: "what would change if I intervened?" That shift forces the algorithm to change, in a way that also forces it to split its data in half.

Imagine you run an online store. You have a predictive model that tells you which customers are most likely to buy a pair of sneakers. You target the high-probability customers with a 20%-off promo code. Did the code work?

Your model tells you who is most likely to buy. But you don't care about the customers who would have bought anyway — the code is just giving away discounts to them. And you don't care about the customers who would never have bought, with or without the code — those are wasted. You care about the customers whose purchase depends on whether they got the code. The persuadables. That's a different group than the high-probability one.

To find them, you need a different kind of tree.

Why standard trees can't answer this

A standard tree is trained to predict an outcome y from features x. It finds splits that make each leaf's y values as homogeneous as possible. If "age < 30" happens to correlate with "will buy," the tree will find it.

But what you actually want is splits that separate people with different treatment responses. For any single customer, you observe one of two things: their response after receiving the code, or their response after not receiving the code — never both. You can't train directly on individual-level treatment effects, because they're not observable. The tree has to work with a clever surrogate.

The causal split criterion

Here's the Athey–Imbens 2016 idea. Instead of looking for splits that maximise purity of the outcome, look for splits where the two resulting subgroups have different average treatment effects.

For each candidate split, estimate the uplift in both the left and right children: in each child, (treated group's average outcome) − (control group's average outcome). A good split separates high-uplift from low-uplift subpopulations — a subgroup that responds at +25% and a subgroup that responds at +2% is a split that found where the treatment works.

This changes the fundamental logic. A predictive tree asks "who has high Y?" A causal tree asks "who has high Y-under-treatment minus Y-under-control?"

The double-dipping problem

There's a subtle statistical issue here. If you use all your training data to pick the best uplift split, you're then using the same data to estimate how big the uplift actually is. Your split-finder was biased toward splits that happen to show high uplift on this sample, so the uplift estimate inside each leaf will be systematically inflated. The more splits you try, the bigger the inflation.

This matters a lot if you want confidence intervals. The usual standard-error calculations assume the tree structure was chosen independently of the data — which it wasn't. Your 95% confidence interval might actually be a 70% confidence interval.

Honest estimation

Athey and Imbens' fix is called honest estimation, and it's refreshingly simple. Split your training data in half:

  • Use the first half to grow the tree: pick splits, decide the structure. Never look at the second half during this phase.
  • Use the second half to estimate the uplift in each leaf: for each leaf built from the first half, drop the second-half rows in and compute the average treatment effect. Never touch the structure.

Because the two halves never interact, the leaf-level estimates are honest in the statistical sense. Standard confidence intervals work. Nobody has cheated.

You pay for this with half your data, which is why causal forests often use hundreds of trees — each tree's two halves combine via the ensemble to recover statistical power.

What this doesn't buy you

Causal trees only work when your data has randomised treatment assignment — i.e., when who got the treatment was decided by a coin flip, not by who looked like they'd respond. If treatment was assigned based on features (maybe salespeople gave discounts to people who seemed interested), then your "treatment effect" is confounded by selection, and the tree will happily estimate something that is not a causal effect.

This is why A/B tests are the gold standard: they randomise by design. Causal trees on A/B test data give you genuine heterogeneous treatment effects — the thing you cannot get from a normal predictive model. For observational data with no randomisation, there are more sophisticated methods (instrumental variables, propensity scores, doubly robust estimation), but the assumptions they require are strong and the methods sit outside this series.

What this buys you

When you do have randomised data, causal trees answer a question that standard ML simply can't: where does the treatment work? Not who will buy — where does the promo code cause a purchase that wouldn't have happened otherwise. Not who will churn — where does the retention campaign change behaviour. Not who will recover — where does the drug genuinely help.

Targeted marketing moves from "send to high-probability buyers" to "send to persuadable buyers." Personalised medicine moves from "predict outcomes" to "decide who benefits." This is the shift from prediction to intervention, and it's what decision trees become when you ask a different question.

Takeaways

  • Predictive trees minimise Var(Y | leaf). Causal trees maximise Var(τ | leaf), where τ is the treatment effect. The split changes; the algorithm stays recognisable.
  • You never observe τ. For any single row, either you have Y-under-treatment or Y-under-control, not both. Causal trees find the split by comparing the two groups' averages in each candidate child.
  • Honest estimation splits the data in half. First half grows the structure, second half estimates the magnitudes. Statistical validity restored.
  • Requires randomised data. A/B tests work; observational data needs extra care to control confounding.
  • Different deliverable. Standard trees tell you what will happen; causal trees tell you where you can change what happens.
