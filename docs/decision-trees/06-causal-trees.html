Causal Trees

Chapter 6 of 6. Trees that look for where an intervention changes the outcome, and why their leaf effects need data the tree never saw.

Chapter 5 ended on what SHAP cannot answer: what would happen if something changed. A store sends a 20%-off code to customers a model flags as likely buyers, many of whom would buy anyway. The ones worth reaching are the persuadables, and no customer is seen both with and without the code.

Why does the same-sample estimate overshoot?

The winning split is where treated and control customers happened to differ most in this sample, partly by noise that will not repeat. Figure 3 removes all heterogeneity: every customer's uplift is +8pp.

Honest estimation

Athey and Imbens split the data in half. The first half grows the tree; the second half is dropped into its leaves to estimate each effect. Having played no part in choosing the leaves, it gives unbiased estimates and valid intervals. Figure 4 targets the depth-2 leaf with the largest first-half uplift and estimates it both ways.

Often the leaf that looked best is not the persuadable corner, and at zero persuadable uplift the same half still reports a large effect. Athey and Imbens's simulations agree: honest 90% intervals reached nominal coverage, adaptive ones fell well short.

Can averaging win back the lost half?

Honesty spends half the data on each job. Causal forests (Wager and Athey, 2018) average many honest trees, each grown on a different random subsample.

What if the code was not randomized?

A leaf's uplift measures the code's effect only if its treated and control customers are comparable, which randomization guarantees. In Figure 6 salespeople give the code to customers who look interested, and interest also makes people buy.

Figure 6. 2,000 simulated customers; the code adds +8pp for everyone. Bars are control and treated buy rates by interest band and overall, with each group's share treated beneath and the gap in points above.

Comparing within bands works only because interest was recorded. Causal forests run on observational data under that assumption, unconfoundedness: given the recorded features, treatment is as good as random. Generalized random forests (Athey, Tibshirani and Wager, 2019) first subtract each customer's predicted outcome and chance of treatment, which they call local centering. Had interest gone unrecorded, only the pooled bars would exist, and nothing in the data would say so.
