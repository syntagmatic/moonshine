The Frontier

Chapter 8 of 8. Two topics here. A structural trick that makes inference extraordinarily fast and connects trees to a 40-year-old idea from hardware verification. And a tour of research that's quietly overturning the forty-year assumption that globally optimal trees are intractable.

This chapter closes the series on two notes. The first is a structural observation about how trees are represented: under a simple extra constraint, they collapse into a compressed data structure that makes prediction orders of magnitude faster at deployment. The second is a research update: methods that were considered hopeless for decades are finally within reach, and some of the foundational assumptions of the field are being quietly overturned.

Oblivious trees: same question at every depth

A normal decision tree can split however it wants at every node. Different nodes at the same depth can ask about different features with different thresholds. This flexibility is expensive at inference time: every prediction means walking a branchy, unpredictable path, which is slow on modern CPUs because it defeats branch prediction.

An oblivious tree adds one rule: every node at the same depth must use the same split predicate. You write down the D questions up front — "is age > 40? is income > 50k? does the row have a phone number?" — and the tree is just a mapping from the D-bit answer vector to a leaf value.

This seems brutal. In exchange you get: predictions that are a single array lookup instead of D branches, no pointer chasing, and an implicit regularising effect on training. CatBoost, the boosting library, uses oblivious trees exclusively — it's the reason its inference is so fast at scale.

The structural payoff

Oblivious trees have an even deeper property. If two subtrees of the tree end up computing the same function (i.e., two different root-to-leaf paths produce the same subsequent predictions), you can merge them: keep one copy of the subtree and point two parents at it. The tree becomes a directed acyclic graph, not a tree at all.

This is exactly what an Ordered Binary Decision Diagram (OBDD) does — a data structure invented by Randal Bryant in 1986 for hardware verification. The decision-tree world independently discovered it was useful for fast inference, but it's the same mathematical object. For functions with lots of redundancy, OBDDs can shrink exponentially — billion-node trees collapse to thousand-node graphs that compute identical functions.

Why this isn't the default

Oblivious trees are strictly less expressive than regular trees for the same depth. A regular tree can reach a given loss with fewer leaves because each branch gets its own splits. The constraint doesn't hurt much in practice — CatBoost is competitive with XGBoost on accuracy — but it does hurt a little, and only matters when you're deploying at scale. Most problems don't need the speedup enough to accept the constraint.

OBDD-style merging also doesn't come automatically from standard tree training. You have to build the tree oblivious-first, then merge identical subtrees as a post-processing step. This is standard in hardware-verification software but rare in ML libraries.

The other frontier: optimal trees

We opened chapter 1 with Hyafil and Rivest's 1976 result that finding the globally optimal decision tree is NP-complete. That result is still true. But its practical consequences are eroding.

Since 2017, two lines of work have made globally optimal trees practical for real problems. Bertsimas and Dunn showed that Mixed-Integer Optimisation solvers can find globally optimal trees on datasets with hundreds or thousands of rows. GOSDT (2019) and related algorithms push further with dynamic-programming-style pruning. These methods find optimal sparse trees in seconds where CART has been running for years.

The shocking empirical result: these optimal trees, chosen by exhaustive search, do not overfit more than greedy trees. Often they generalise better, because they can find deep structural patterns — like the classic XOR — that greedy search systematically misses.

Three more directions worth knowing

Beyond optimal trees, three other directions are actively shaping what decision-tree software can do.

Streaming trees

What if your data doesn't fit in memory? Hoeffding trees grow from infinite data streams, using probability bounds to decide when enough samples have been seen to commit to a split with statistical confidence. Used in real-time fraud detection, network intrusion detection, anywhere data arrives faster than you can retrain.

Privacy-preserving trees

Training on sensitive data (medical records, financial records) and publishing the trained tree can leak training examples. Differentially private trees inject calibrated noise during training — enough to hide any single row's influence while keeping the model useful. Used where GDPR/HIPAA requires provable non-identifiability.

Fair trees

Training data with historical bias (e.g., credit scoring that under-approved certain zip codes) will train trees that replicate the bias. Fair-tree training modifies the splitting criterion to penalise splits that correlate too strongly with protected attributes, or enforces equal error rates across groups. The definitions of "fair" are contested; the mechanisms are well-understood.

Optimal trees become standard

As MIO solvers keep improving, the default for "train a small interpretable tree" may shift from greedy CART to an optimal solver. Already happening in regulated finance and medicine where small optimal trees are preferred for auditability.

Where we've been

A decision tree is a flowchart. It picks questions greedily, splitting the data one feature at a time, and makes predictions by averaging whatever lands in each leaf. That's the whole basic idea, and forty years of research have filled in every angle of it. Ensembles make it more accurate. Boosting makes it more expressive. SHAP makes it readable again. Causal trees make it answer intervention questions. Soft variants make it trainable with gradient descent. MCTS uses its structure to search instead of fit. Oblivious variants make it fast at scale. And a new generation of solvers is, finally, making it truly optimal.

The algorithm is still deceptively simple. There are just a lot of ways to be simple.
