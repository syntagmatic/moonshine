Thirteen Centuries of Earthquakes

Japan's written record from 684 to 2024, and what each disaster changed

Japan has one of the longest written records of earthquakes anywhere. Court chronicles, temple records and local histories describe large earthquakes centuries before the first seismograph. The oldest well-documented one, the Hakuho earthquake of 684, struck the Nankai Trough the way great earthquakes still do.

This article draws on NOAA's Significant Earthquake Database, kept by the National Centers for Environmental Information. It is a different kind of catalog from the USGS one used elsewhere in this series. It reaches back to 2150 BCE, and an earthquake gets in only if it left a mark: it caused deaths, caused roughly $1 million or more in damage, reached magnitude 7.5 or intensity X, or generated a tsunami. So it records what people noticed and wrote down, which is not the same as everything that happened.

The pre-modern events near the top of the chart were genuinely large. The 1707 Hoei earthquake (M8.4) ruptured most of the Nankai Trough, and 49 days later Mount Fuji began its most recent eruption. The 869 Jogan earthquake (M8.6) sent a tsunami several kilometers inland across the Sendai plain. Magnitudes for events like these are back-calculated from damage descriptions and tsunami deposits, and they carry large uncertainties. An M8.4 in the seventh century is a best guess, and pre-modern death tolls come from texts that may exaggerate, omit, or fold in the famine and fires that followed.

The circle sizes tell a different story from the heights. The 1923 Great Kanto earthquake was M7.9, not the largest on the chart, but its toll of about 105,000 dwarfs everything else, and most of those deaths came from the fires that followed the shaking. The 1896 Meiji-Sanriku earthquake shook so gently that coastal towns barely reacted. Its surface-wave magnitude, which tracks the shaking, is only 7.2, well below the M8.3 plotted here, yet its tsunami, up to 38 meters high, killed about 27,000 people. Tsunami earthquakes like this one rupture slowly, radiating little strong shaking for the size of the wave they raise.

Magnitude and Human Cost

If magnitude predicted deaths, the chart below would show a clear upward trend. It shows a loose cloud instead: across the 36 events with a recorded toll, the rank correlation between magnitude and deaths is only 0.29. The M7.1 Kamakura earthquake of 1293 killed about 23,000 people, while the M7.6 Niigata earthquake of 1964 killed 36. Location, depth, building quality, time of day, and whether a tsunami follows all matter as much as raw seismic energy.

Where They Struck

Japan's significant earthquakes concentrate along the Pacific coast, where the oceanic plates dive beneath the islands. The Sanriku coast (northeastern Honshu) and the Nankai Trough (southwestern Pacific coast) appear again and again across the centuries. A few devastating inland events, like the 1891 Mino-Owari and 1995 Kobe earthquakes, show that shallow crustal faults under cities can be just as deadly.

The Jogan Precedent

The 869 Jogan earthquake is one of the most important entries in the record, though few people appreciated that until 2011. Historical texts describe a massive earthquake off the Sanriku coast, followed by a tsunami that flooded the Sendai plain and drowned about a thousand people (the recorded figure; the actual toll was likely higher).

For most of modern seismology, Jogan was a footnote. Then on March 11, 2011, the Tohoku earthquake struck on the same subduction zone, in a similar location, with a comparable magnitude and an even larger tsunami. Geologists who had studied Jogan's tsunami deposits (sand layers found kilometers inland) had warned that such an event was due. The pre-2011 seismic hazard models for the Fukushima coast did not fully account for this historical precedent.

Keeping centuries-old events alongside modern instrumental records lets researchers see recurrence that a few decades of seismographs would miss. The Sanriku coast alone appears in this record in 869, 1611, 1896, 1933 and 2011.

What Each Disaster Changed

Several earthquakes on the timeline became turning points. Each exposed a weakness, and each led to a specific change.

The 1923 Kanto earthquake struck at 11:58 a.m., while cooking fires burned across Tokyo and Yokohama, and the firestorms that followed caused most of the deaths. The next year a revision of the Urban Building Law introduced Japan's first seismic design requirement, and Tokyo was rebuilt with wider roads and firebreaks. September 1 is now National Disaster Prevention Day.

The 1948 Fukui earthquake hit a city still rebuilding from wartime bombing, and 5,131 people died by NOAA's count (Japanese sources give 3,769). The following year the Japan Meteorological Agency added a seventh, highest level to its intensity scale, and the 1950 Building Standard Law set the national seismic requirements that every later code builds on.

The 1995 Kobe earthquake struck directly beneath the city at 5:46 a.m. and killed 6,434 people, many in buildings put up before the 1981 revision of the seismic code. The toppled Hanshin Expressway became the defining image. Retrofit programs for pre-1981 buildings followed, along with an overhaul of how national and local governments coordinate disaster response.

During the 2004 Chuetsu earthquake a Joetsu Shinkansen train derailed at speed, the first derailment of a Shinkansen in service in forty years of operation. No one was hurt. Rail operators afterward fitted guides that keep a derailed train close to the track.

The 2011 Tohoku tsunami overtopped seawalls built for smaller waves, ran up as high as 40 meters, and reached about 5 kilometers inland on the Sendai plain. About 15,900 people were killed and some 2,500 are still missing, and the tsunami caused the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster. Tohoku broke the assumption that the largest earthquakes of the modern record set the upper bound. Hazard planning now considers the largest event a zone could physically produce.

In Kumamoto in 2016, a M6.2 foreshock (6.5 on the JMA scale) sent residents back into houses that looked undamaged. Twenty-eight hours later the M7.0 mainshock (JMA 7.3) brought many of them down. Afterward the JMA stopped issuing aftershock forecasts that assumed the largest shock had passed, and now warns that a quake of similar size may follow.

The Noto Peninsula earthquake on New Year's Day 2024 (M7.5; JMA 7.6) killed more than 200 people directly, mostly in older wooden houses (NOAA's total of 549 includes later disaster-related deaths), and landslides cut the peninsula's narrow roads for days. It showed how hard retrofitting and rescue are in rural areas with aging, shrinking populations.
