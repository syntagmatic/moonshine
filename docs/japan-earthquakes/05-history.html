Thirteen Centuries of Earthquakes

Japan's written earthquake record reaches back to 684. It shows the same coasts struck century after century, and how each great disaster changed the way the country builds and plans.

The instrumental catalog behind Part 4 covers 26 years, too few to show how often any one coast produces a great earthquake. For that, this part turns to the written record. Japan has one of the longest written records of earthquakes anywhere. Court chronicles, temple records and local histories describe large earthquakes centuries before the first seismograph. The oldest well-documented one, the Hakuho earthquake of 684, struck the Nankai Trough the way great earthquakes still do.

The record used here is NOAA's Significant Earthquake Database, kept by the National Centers for Environmental Information. It is a different kind of catalog from the USGS one used elsewhere in this series. It reaches back to 2150 BCE, and an earthquake gets in only if it left a mark: it caused deaths, caused roughly $1 million or more in damage, reached magnitude 7.5 or intensity X, or generated a tsunami. So it records what people noticed and wrote down, which is not the same as everything that happened.

How Much the Record Misses

Counting the record's large earthquakes year by year shows how much of the past it misses. For the 20th century NOAA lists 10 Japan earthquakes of magnitude 8 or more and 75 of magnitude 7 or more. Had the centuries before 1600 been as active, and as fully written down, they would hold about 92 M8+ and 687 M7+ earthquakes. The record has 8 and 33. Against the 20th-century rate it holds 9% of the expected M8+ earthquakes before 1600, 30% for 1600 to 1799 and 70% for the 1800s. The old record keeps the largest and deadliest events and loses most of the rest. Its longest silence, 1099 to 1241, is 142 years with no Japan entry at all, more likely a gap in the writing than a quiet stretch of the plates.

The pre-modern events near the top of the chart were large. The 1707 Hoei earthquake (M8.4) ruptured most of the Nankai Trough, and 49 days later Mount Fuji began its most recent eruption. The 869 Jogan earthquake (M8.6) sent a tsunami several kilometers inland across the Sendai plain. Magnitudes for events like these are back-calculated from damage descriptions and tsunami deposits, and they carry large uncertainties. An M8.4 in the seventh century is a best guess, and pre-modern death tolls come from texts that may exaggerate, omit, or fold in the famine and fires that followed.

The circle sizes tell a different story from the heights. The 1923 Great Kanto earthquake was M7.9, not the largest on the chart, but its toll of about 105,000 dwarfs everything else, and most of those deaths came from the fires that followed the shaking. The 1896 Meiji-Sanriku earthquake shook so gently that coastal towns barely reacted. Its surface-wave magnitude, which tracks the shaking, is only 7.2, well below the M8.3 plotted here, yet its tsunami, up to 38 meters high, killed about 27,000 people. Tsunami earthquakes like this one rupture slowly, radiating little strong shaking for the size of the wave they raise.

Where They Struck

Many of the deadliest earthquakes lie along the Pacific coast, where the oceanic plates dive beneath the islands, and the Sanriku coast (northeastern Honshu) and the Nankai Trough (southwestern Pacific coast) appear again and again across the centuries. Many others struck inland or on the Sea of Japan side. The 1891 Mino-Owari, 1847 Zenkoji, 1828 Sanjo and 1995 Kobe earthquakes show that shallow faults under towns can be just as deadly. How regularly one of these coasts repeats, and what that does and does not say about the next time, is the subject of the next section.

How the Official Odds of the Next Nankai Earthquake Are Built

The repeat visits on the map raise the question every reader asks: when is the next one? Japan's government publishes a number for the Nankai Trough, and the method behind it is simple enough to rebuild here. Treat each great earthquake as a reset. The time until the next one is a random interval with a mean, μ, and a relative spread, α; the 30-year probability is the share of that distribution that falls in the next 30 years, given the time the coast has already been quiet. The usual shape is the Brownian Passage Time (BPT) distribution. What follows shows how the official probability is built and how much it moves when its inputs change. It is not our forecast, and it says nothing about which year.

Where this misses. The pairs (1854; 1944 and 1946) are treated as single events, as HERP does, though the two halves broke up to two years apart. The 1605 Keicho earthquake may not have been a Nankai Trough event at all; case IV drops it. The sensitivity is plain in the figure: read three defensible ways, the same sequence gives anything from about % to about % for the same 30 years. The range of α and the flat prior are choices, taken from HERP and from the simplest default. And a renewal model is not a forecast of a date: it assumes each interval is independent of the one before (the time-predictable model is the exception) and says nothing about when.

The Jogan Precedent

The 869 Jogan earthquake is one of the most important entries in the record, though its importance was not widely recognized until 2011. Historical texts describe a massive earthquake off the Sanriku coast, followed by a tsunami that flooded the Sendai plain and drowned about a thousand people (the recorded figure; the actual toll was likely higher).

For most of modern seismology, Jogan received little attention. On March 11, 2011, the Tohoku earthquake (M9.1 in NOAA's catalog) struck the same subduction zone and raised an even larger tsunami. Geologists who had studied Jogan's tsunami deposits (sand layers found kilometers inland) had shown that tsunamis like it had flooded the Sendai plain before, and the pre-2011 seismic hazard models for the Fukushima coast did not fully account for that precedent. That is not the same as having predicted the earthquake. HERP's 2019 evaluation, written after 2011, puts Jogan at Mw to or more, somewhat smaller than 2011, and counts such earthquakes in the last years, with a mean interval of about to years. and run the Nankai method on that sequence for the day before the earthquake: % over the next 30 years if the previous event was the 1454 Kyotoku earthquake, % if it was the 1611 Keicho-Sanriku earthquake (the deposits cannot say which, and NOAA has no 1454 record). Both stay below the % where HERP's top rank starts, and neither says an earthquake of 2011's size was due.

Keeping centuries-old events alongside modern instrumental records lets researchers see recurrence that a few decades of seismographs would miss. Off the Sanriku coast alone, NOAA's record has an earthquake of magnitude 8 or more in 869, 1611, 1677, 1793, 1856, 1896, 1901, 1933, 1968 and 2011: 10 in all, and only 1 before 1600, which says more about the record than about the coast.

What Each Disaster Changed

Several earthquakes in this record exposed a specific weakness and led to a specific change in building codes, warnings or planning.

The 1923 Kanto earthquake struck at 11:58 a.m., while cooking fires burned across Tokyo and Yokohama, and the firestorms that followed caused most of the deaths. The next year a revision of the Urban Building Law introduced Japan's first seismic design requirement, and Tokyo was rebuilt with wider roads and firebreaks. September 1 is now National Disaster Prevention Day.

The 1948 Fukui earthquake hit a city still rebuilding from wartime bombing, and 5,131 people died by NOAA's count (Japanese sources give 3,769). The following year the Japan Meteorological Agency added a seventh, highest level to its intensity scale, and the 1950 Building Standard Law set the national seismic requirements that every later code builds on.

The 1995 Kobe earthquake struck directly beneath the city at 5:46 a.m. and killed 6,434 people, many in buildings put up before the 1981 revision of the seismic code. The toppled Hanshin Expressway became the defining image. Retrofit programs for pre-1981 buildings followed, along with an overhaul of how national and local governments coordinate disaster response.

During the 2004 Chuetsu earthquake a Joetsu Shinkansen train derailed at speed, the first derailment of a Shinkansen in service in forty years of operation. No one was hurt. Rail operators afterward fitted guides that keep a derailed train close to the track.

The 2011 Tohoku tsunami overtopped seawalls built for smaller waves, ran up as high as 40 meters, and reached about 5 kilometers inland on the Sendai plain. About 15,900 people were killed and some 2,500 are still missing, and the tsunami caused the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear disaster. Tohoku broke the assumption that the largest earthquakes of the modern record set the upper bound. Hazard planning now considers the largest event a zone could physically produce.

In Kumamoto in 2016, a M6.2 foreshock (6.5 on the JMA scale) sent residents back into houses that looked undamaged. Twenty-eight hours later the M7.0 mainshock (JMA 7.3) brought many of them down. Afterward the JMA stopped issuing aftershock forecasts that assumed the largest shock had passed, and now warns that a quake of similar size may follow.

The Noto Peninsula earthquake on New Year's Day 2024 (M7.5; JMA 7.6) killed more than 200 people directly, mostly in older wooden houses (NOAA's total of 549 includes later disaster-related deaths), and landslides cut the peninsula's narrow roads for days. It showed how hard retrofitting and rescue are in rural areas with aging, shrinking populations.
