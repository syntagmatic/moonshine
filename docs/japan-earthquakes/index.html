Japan Earthquakes

Seven interactive articles, made with Daniel Overstreet

Japan sits where four tectonic plates meet. These seven articles work from real data, mostly the 16,787 earthquakes of magnitude 4.5 and larger that the USGS recorded around Japan from 2000 through 2025. They cover where the earthquakes strike and why, how they cluster in time, how often the big ones come, and the history, tsunamis and early warnings that follow from them. The map above plots the 3,701 of those events at M5.0 or larger, colored by depth.

1 Where the Earth Cracks Open
Brush a time window or a patch of the map and the other view follows. Every dot is a real event from the USGS catalog.
2 Tectonic Plate Geometry of Japan
Pick a transect and watch the catalog's hypocenters trace the Pacific and Philippine Sea slabs, down to about 680 km.
3 When the Ground Won't Stop Shaking
Play 26 years of earthquakes and see why one M9.1 carries 86% of the catalog's energy.
4 How Often the Big Ones Come
Fit the Gutenberg-Richter law yourself. Drag the cutoff and watch the b-value, and the forecast for M7+, change.
5 Thirteen Centuries of Earthquakes
Japan's written record from 684 to 2024: its deadliest earthquakes, how much of the past it misses, the Jogan precedent, magnitude versus human cost, and what each disaster changed.
6 Tsunami Propagation
Wave speed from v = √(gd), checked against the 2011 tsunami's arrivals in Hawaii and California.
7 Earthquake Early Warning
P waves outrun S waves, and the gap is your warning. The 2011 alert shows what the system can and cannot do.

The Shindo Scale

Japan rates earthquakes by shindo, the intensity felt on the ground, not the magnitude at the fault. Ten levels, from imperceptible to destructive. The glyphs on the article cards above use these colors. The maps are colored by depth instead, red for shallow and blue for deep.

0
Not felt. Instruments record it, people do not.
1
Felt only by some people at rest indoors.
2
Felt by many indoors. Hanging lamps may swing slightly.
3
Felt by most indoors. Dishes rattle. Crockery in cupboards may clatter.
4
Most people are startled. Hanging objects swing notably. Unstable ornaments may fall.
5-lower
Many frightened. Unsecured furniture may shift. Unreinforced walls can crack.
5-upper
Difficult to walk without support. Unsecured furniture topples. Window glass can break.
6-lower
Hard to stand. Tiles fall from roofs. Some wooden houses suffer damage.
6-upper
Impossible to stand upright. Many wooden houses collapse. Ground cracks appear.
7
Cannot move by personal volition. Most wooden houses collapse. Even reinforced-concrete buildings suffer severe damage.
