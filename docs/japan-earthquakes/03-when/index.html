When the Ground Won't Stop Shaking

Twenty-six years of earthquakes around Japan, animated in time

Japan sits at the collision of four tectonic plates. If you've lived there for any length of time, you know the drill: the phone buzzes, the room sways, and you wait to see if it's going to get worse. Most of the time it doesn't. Sometimes it does, and then a single event can outweigh decades of everything else put together.

This animation plays through every earthquake of magnitude 4.5 and above that the USGS recorded near Japan from 2000 through 2025, all 16,792 of them. Pay attention to March 2011.

How earthquake sequences unfold

Most of the time, Japan's seismicity looks like a steady background hum. About ten dots a week, scattered across the map, mostly M4.5 to M5.5. This is normal stress release along plate boundaries and faults. Alarming if you're new to it, but it's the baseline. The earth is always adjusting.

Then there are the sequences. When a major earthquake strikes, it changes the stress field in the surrounding crust. Faults that were close to failure get pushed over the edge, and the result is a burst of aftershocks that can last weeks or months. In the animation, you can see this dramatically: the Tohoku M9.1 on March 11, 2011 produces a dense swarm of dots that lights up the entire northeast coast of Honshu. The catalog holds 531 events of M4.5 or larger in the first 24 hours alone.

For comparison, the 2001–2010 background averaged about one M4.5+ event a day. Over the 30 days after the mainshock the catalog counts 2,081, roughly 70 times the background rate. The stress change also reached faults far from the rupture. Within 15 hours a M6.2 struck near the Nagano-Niigata border and another M6.2 hit the Sea of Japan off Akita, on the opposite coast; four days later a M6.0 shook the foot of Mount Fuji.

The tyranny of the magnitude scale

The magnitude scale is logarithmic: each whole number represents about 32 times more energy. Add up the energy of all 16,792 earthquakes on this map and the M9.1 Tohoku mainshock accounts for 86% of it, about six times everything else combined. Look at the sparkline. The cumulative energy line is essentially flat for most of the timeline, then jumps vertically on March 11, 2011.

Here's another way to think about it: to match the energy of one M9.1 earthquake, you would need about 45,000 earthquakes of M6.0, or about 8 million of M4.5. The whole catalog holds 344 events of M6.0 or larger. Even its second-largest event, the M8.2 Tokachi-oki earthquake of 2003, released less than a twentieth of Tohoku's energy.

Play through the full timeline and you'll spot several other bursts: the 2003 Tokachi-oki earthquake off Hokkaido, the 2016 Kumamoto earthquakes (a foreshock on April 14, then a larger mainshock 28 hours later), the 2024 Noto Peninsula earthquake on New Year's Day, and recurring offshore swarms east of Honshu. Each has a different character. Kumamoto was shallow and destructive. Noto struck a region with many traditional wooden buildings. The offshore events mostly dissipate into the Pacific. Magnitudes on this page are the USGS catalog values, which can differ from the Japan Meteorological Agency's: USGS gives Kumamoto M7.0 and Noto M7.5, where JMA gives 7.3 and 7.6.

What this means for daily life

If you're living in Japan, the background seismicity is a constant companion. The shindo scale matters more than magnitude for how it actually feels. A deep M6.0 hundreds of kilometers offshore might produce only a gentle sway in Tokyo, while a shallow M5.5 directly beneath the city is a sharp jolt that knocks things off shelves. The animation can't show you that nuance, but it does show spatial patterns: some regions are consistently more active than others, and the offshore trench east of Honshu is the single biggest source of earthquakes.

Japan's building codes and early warning systems are among the best anywhere. But the physics is non-negotiable: another great earthquake is a matter of when, not if. Knowing what the difference between background noise and a major sequence looks like, that's part of living in earthquake country.
