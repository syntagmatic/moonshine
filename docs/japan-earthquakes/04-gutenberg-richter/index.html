How Often the Big Ones Come

The Gutenberg-Richter law, fitted to 26 years of Japan's earthquakes

Small earthquakes are common and large ones are rare, and the ratio between them is steady. In 1944 Beno Gutenberg and Charles Richter wrote it down: count every earthquake at or above magnitude M, and the count falls by roughly a factor of ten for each unit of magnitude. On a log scale that is a straight line, log10 N = a − bM. The slope b is called the b-value, and a b of 1 means exactly a factor of ten per magnitude unit.

The figure below tests the rule against the 16,792 earthquakes of M4.5 and larger that the USGS recorded around Japan from 2000 through 2025. Each point is the number of events at or above that magnitude. The solid line is a fit to every event above the cutoff, and the dashed line has b fixed at 1. Drag the cutoff line, or use the slider, to change where the fit starts.

Where the straight line bends

With the cutoff at M4.5, a b of 1 predicts 16,792 ÷ 102.5 ≈ 53 earthquakes of M7 or larger. The catalog holds 33. Fitting b to the data misses in the other direction: the maximum-likelihood fit gives b ≈ 1.21, which predicts only about 16. The curve is steeper at the small end than at the large end, so no single straight line from M4.5 fits both.

Drag the cutoff upward and watch the fitted b fall. By M5.2 it is close to 1.0, and with the cutoff anywhere from M5.2 to M5.5 the prediction lands between 32 and 36, close to the observed 33. The strip under the plot shows why the small end behaves differently. Below M5.0, nine events in ten are measured with the body-wave magnitude mb, a quick estimate from the first seconds of the P wave. Above M5.5, most get a moment magnitude Mw, computed from the whole rupture. The two scales don't agree exactly for moderate earthquakes, so the lower part of the curve is drawn with a different ruler than the upper part.

A b-value is only as good as the cutoff and the magnitude scale behind it. With a consistent choice, the law becomes a forecasting tool. It says nothing about when the next large earthquake will come, only how often large ones come on average, and that average is what hazard maps, building codes and insurance models are built on.

Eight regions

Cumulative count (log scale) versus magnitude for each region, USGS catalog, M4.5+, 2000–2025. The vertical tick marks the cutoff; the solid line is the maximum-likelihood fit above it. n is the number of events at or above the cutoff.

Tohoku dominates, with 4,584 events since 2000. The 2011 earthquake and its aftershocks account for much of that, but the Japan Trench off Tohoku's Pacific coast is busy in every year of the record. Kanto comes second with 3,126, and its box includes the offshore Ibaraki end of the 2011 aftershock zone. Okinawa is third at 2,312: the Ryukyu Trench runs from Kyushu toward Taiwan, and the box catches events along the whole arc.

Chubu, Kansai and Chugoku/Shikoku have the fewest events and the shortest lines. These regions sit away from the trenches, and their earthquakes come mostly from crustal faults. Kansai's largest event in the window is M5.8 and Chubu's is M6.2. A short line doesn't make a region safe: the 1995 Kobe earthquake (M6.9) was a crustal quake in Kansai, five years before this record starts.

Set the cutoff to M5.0 and compare slopes. Tohoku (0.95) and Kyushu (0.97) sit near 1. Hokkaido (1.09), Okinawa (1.16) and Kanto (1.17) are steeper, so large events there are rarer relative to small ones. Among these well-sampled regions the gap between Tohoku and Kanto is about four times its uncertainty. The interior regions show the danger of reading too much into a slope. Chugoku/Shikoku gives 0.80 and Kansai 1.18, but from only 24 and 12 events, with standard errors of about 0.16 and 0.34. A b-value from a few dozen earthquakes says little about a region's physics.

Data: USGS earthquake catalog, M4.5+, 2000–2025, magnitudes rounded to 0.1. Regions are latitude/longitude boxes, assigned in the order listed from north to south where boxes overlap. b-values are maximum-likelihood estimates (Aki 1965, with Utsu's half-bin correction), b = log10e / (mean M − (Mc − 0.05)), with standard error b/√n.
