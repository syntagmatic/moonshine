Where the Earth Cracks Open

Twenty-six years of earthquakes around Japan on a linked map and timeline. They bunch into a few narrow bands, and the bands trace the boundaries of the plates underneath.

If you have lived in Japan for any length of time, you know the feeling: a low rumble, dishes rattling, a few seconds deciding whether to duck under the table or keep eating. In a typical year the USGS records about 530 earthquakes of magnitude 4.5 or larger in and around Japan, and in 2011 it recorded 3,754. They are not spread evenly over the region; they cluster.

Spatial clustering means events bunching together in space rather than scattering at random. Where earthquakes cluster is set by the geology below, and in Japan the clusters trace the boundaries of four colliding tectonic plates.

The map and the timeline

Below is every M4.5+ earthquake the USGS recorded in and around Japan from 2000 through 2025, about 16,800 events. Each dot is colored by depth: quakes near the surface are orange-red and red, those 70 to 300 km down are magenta to purple, and the deep ones, 300 km down and more, are blue.

The three views are linked, so you can explore from any of them:

  • Drag on the timeline to select a time window. The map shows only quakes from that period.
  • Drag on the map to select a region (on a phone, press Draw a region first, so that swiping still scrolls the page). The timeline and the heatmap below it redraw for that region alone.
  • Click a depth label beside the heatmap to show only that depth band in every view, or click a month in the heatmap to select it.

Click or tap outside a brush to clear it, or press Clear.

The clusters and the plate boundaries

Brush the timeline to select just March 2011. That one month holds 1,980 of the catalog's events, the Tohoku mainshock and its aftershocks, packed into a band off the northeast coast. The band follows the Japan Trench, where the Pacific Plate dives beneath the Okhotsk Plate.

Japan sits where four tectonic plates meet: the Pacific, Philippine Sea, Eurasian, and Okhotsk (North American). Each boundary produces a different style of seismicity:

The Japan Trench (east of Honshu): the Pacific Plate subducts westward beneath the Okhotsk Plate. About half of the catalog's M7+ earthquakes (18 of 33) happened off Tohoku along this trench, and because they include the M9.1 Tohoku event they carry 93% of the M7+ energy. Quakes along this boundary are shallow to moderate depth and very frequent.

The Nankai Trough (south of Shikoku and the Kii Peninsula): the Philippine Sea Plate subducts beneath the Eurasian Plate. Historically this zone produces a great earthquake roughly every 100-150 years, and it is closely watched.

The Ryukyu Trench (Okinawa and the southwest islands): another segment of the Philippine Sea Plate boundary, reaching toward Taiwan. You can see a chain of dots stretching southwest.

Deep-focus clusters: brush the map south of Tokyo along the Izu-Bonin islands, or over the Sea of Japan west of Honshu. The blue dots there, 300 to 680 km down, trace the Wadati-Benioff zone: the subducted slab of the Pacific Plate, still generating earthquakes as it sinks into the mantle. Of the 522 events deeper than 300 km, 338 sit under the Izu-Bonin arc.

The heatmap under the timeline splits every month by depth and shades each cell against its own row's average month, so a row with a steady rate looks like an even texture and a burst shows as a dark cell. The shallow row is pale for years and then goes dark all at once. Of all events shallower than 70 km, March 2011 alone holds 13.5%, and their monthly counts vary 291 times as much as independent quakes arriving at a steady rate would (for those, the variance of the monthly count equals its mean).

The 300+ row never goes dark. It had 0 events in March 2011, between 10 and 33 in any year, and a variance just 1.3 times its mean. What changes, in both rows, is slow: the shallow rate rose from about 320 a year in 2000 to 2010 to 530 a year since 2012, and the deep rate fell from about 23 to 17. The long Tohoku aftershock sequence is part of the first, but over the same years the catalog changed how it assigns magnitudes near the M4.5 cutoff, and these data can't separate the two.

To see it in one place, press Select the Izu-Bonin arc and then click the 300+ label. The timeline redraws for the deep events under the arc alone: about one a month, with no spike in 2011.

Why clustering matters

Earthquakes that bunch together in space and time mark a structure: a fault, a subduction interface, a volcanic arc. Spatial clustering is one of the first things seismologists look for in a catalog, because it separates those structures from scattered background events and shows which faults are active and where stress is building.

The linked brushes are a standard technique from exploratory data analysis. Linking geography and time shows patterns that neither view shows alone. The 2011 Tohoku swarm is obvious in the timeline as a spike, but only the map shows where those quakes concentrate. The steady rhythm of deep-focus quakes is invisible on the map but plain in the depth heatmap.
