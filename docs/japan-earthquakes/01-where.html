Where the Earth Cracks Open

Twenty-six years of earthquakes around Japan on a linked map and timeline. They bunch into a few narrow bands, and the bands trace the boundaries of the plates underneath.

If you have lived in Japan for any length of time, you know the feeling: a low rumble, dishes rattling, a few seconds deciding whether to duck under the table or keep eating. In a typical year the USGS records about 500 earthquakes of magnitude 4.5 or larger in and around Japan, and in 2011 it recorded 3,754. They are not spread evenly over the region; they cluster.

Spatial clustering means events bunching together in space rather than scattering at random. Where earthquakes cluster is set by the geology below, and in Japan the clusters trace the boundaries of four colliding tectonic plates.

The map and the timeline

Below is every M4.5+ earthquake the USGS recorded in and around Japan from 2000 through 2025, about 16,800 events. Each dot is colored by depth: shallow quakes near the surface show up as warm reds and oranges, while deep quakes hundreds of kilometers down are cool blues and purples.

Two brushes let you explore the data from both directions:

The clusters and the plate boundaries

Brush the timeline to select just March 2011. That one month holds 1,980 of the catalog's events, the Tohoku mainshock and its aftershocks, packed into a band off the northeast coast. The band follows the Japan Trench, where the Pacific Plate dives beneath the Okhotsk Plate.

Japan sits where four tectonic plates meet: the Pacific, Philippine Sea, Eurasian, and Okhotsk (North American). Each boundary produces a different style of seismicity:

The Japan Trench (east of Honshu): the Pacific Plate subducts westward beneath the Okhotsk Plate. Most of Japan's largest earthquakes come from here, including the M9.1 Tohoku event. Quakes along this boundary are shallow to moderate depth and very frequent.

The Nankai Trough (south of Shikoku and the Kii Peninsula): the Philippine Sea Plate subducts beneath the Eurasian Plate. Historically this zone produces a great earthquake roughly every 100-150 years, and it is closely watched.

The Ryukyu Trench (Okinawa and the southwest islands): another segment of the Philippine Sea Plate boundary, reaching toward Taiwan. You can see a chain of dots stretching southwest.

Deep-focus clusters: brush the map south of Tokyo along the Izu-Bonin islands, or over the Sea of Japan west of Honshu. The deep-blue dots there, 300 to 680 km down, trace the Wadati-Benioff zone: the subducted slab of the Pacific Plate, still generating earthquakes as it sinks into the mantle. Of the 522 events deeper than 300 km, 338 sit under the Izu-Bonin arc.

Brush a rectangle over the deep-blue dots under the Bonin islands, then look at the timeline. The deep quakes arrive at a steady rate, between 10 and 33 a year below 300 km across the whole catalog, with no burst in 2011. Shallow aftershock sequences arrive in bursts instead, like the one in March 2011.

Why clustering matters

Earthquakes that bunch together in space and time mark a structure: a fault, a subduction interface, a volcanic arc. Spatial clustering is one of the first things seismologists look for in a catalog, because it separates those structures from scattered background events and shows which faults are active and where stress is building.

The linked brushes are a standard technique from exploratory data analysis. Linking geography and time shows patterns that neither view shows alone. The 2011 Tohoku swarm is obvious in the timeline as a spike, but only the map shows where those quakes concentrate. The steady rhythm of deep-focus quakes is invisible on the map but clear in the timeline.
