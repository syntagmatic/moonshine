Where the Earth Cracks Open

Twenty-six years of earthquakes around Japan on a linked map and timeline. They bunch into a few narrow bands that trace the plates underneath: … of the quakes lie within 30 km of a mapped plate surface, against … for the same quakes scattered at random.

If you have lived in Japan, you know the feeling: a low rumble, dishes rattling, a few seconds deciding whether to duck under the table or keep eating. In a typical year the USGS records about 530 earthquakes of magnitude 4.5 or larger in and around Japan; in 2011 it recorded 3,754. They are not spread evenly. They cluster.

Where they cluster is set by the geology below, and in Japan the clusters trace the boundaries of four colliding plates. The map below draws those boundaries and the sinking slabs. The second figure measures how far each quake sits from the nearest plate surface, against the same quakes scattered at random.

The map and the timeline

Below is every M4.5+ earthquake the USGS recorded in and around Japan from 2000 through 2025, about 16,800 events, colored by depth: orange-red to red near the surface, magenta to purple from 70 to 300 km, blue below 300 km.

The three views are linked:

  • Drag on the timeline to pick a time window; the map shows only that period.
  • Drag on the map to pick a region (on a phone, press Draw a region first so that swiping still scrolls). The timeline and heatmap redraw for that region.
  • Click a depth label beside the heatmap to show only that band in every view, or click a month to select it.

The clusters and the plate boundaries

Brush the timeline to select just March 2011. That one month holds 1,980 of the catalog's events, the Tohoku mainshock and its aftershocks, packed into a band off the northeast coast. The band follows the Japan Trench, where the Pacific Plate dives beneath the Okhotsk Plate.

Japan sits where four plates meet: the Pacific, Philippine Sea, Eurasian and Okhotsk (North American). Each boundary has its own kind of seismicity.

The Japan Trench (east of Honshu): the Pacific Plate subducts westward beneath the Okhotsk Plate. About half of the catalog's M7+ earthquakes (17 of 33) happened off Tohoku, and with the M9.1 Tohoku event among them they carry 93% of the M7+ energy. Of those 17, … sit on the plate boundary, within 20 km of the slab's top. The other … are outer-rise quakes, seaward of the trench in the incoming plate, which a box drawn around the trench would count with them.

The Nankai Trough (south of Shikoku and the Kii Peninsula): the Philippine Sea Plate subducts beneath the Eurasian Plate. NOAA's historical record has … great earthquakes here since …. Since …, when Part 5 argues the record becomes complete, they have come … to … years apart. The last was in …, … years before this catalog ends.

The Ryukyu Trench (Okinawa and the southwest islands): the same Philippine Sea Plate boundary, continuing toward Taiwan. Of the … shallow quakes along it, … lie within 20 km of the plate's top. Another … sit more than 20 km above it, mostly behind the island chain in the Okinawa Trough: crustal quakes of the arc, not plate-boundary quakes.

Deep-focus clusters: brush the map along the Izu-Bonin islands south of Tokyo, or across the Sea of Japan toward Vladivostok. The blue dots there, 300 to 680 km down, are inside the sinking Pacific slab, the Wadati-Benioff zone. Of the 522 events deeper than 300 km, 338 are under the Izu-Bonin arc. The deep events sit a median … km from the slab's top: … lie within 50 km but only … within 30 km, because they are inside the plate, not on its surface.

In the heatmap, the shallow row is pale for years and then goes dark all at once. March 2011 alone holds 13.5% of all M5+ events shallower than 70 km, and the variance of the monthly counts is 291 times their mean. For independent quakes arriving at a steady rate the two would be equal.

The 300+ row never goes dark. It had 0 events in March 2011 and 10 to 33 in every year, with a variance 1.3 times its mean, close enough to a steady random rate that a chi-square test finds nothing against it (p = 0.17). At M4.5 the same test does reject a steady rate, but mostly because the catalog changed, not the ground (see the note below).

What clustering cannot show

Clustering marks structure: a fault, a subduction interface, a volcanic arc. It shows where plates are slipping now, not where stress is building. The Nankai Trough is the boundary most expected to produce the next great earthquake, yet a box over its shallow part holds … M5+ quakes in 26 years, about … a year, where a box of the same size off Tohoku holds …. A locked fault does not slip, so it stays quiet while the strain builds.

Data: USGS Earthquake Catalog, M4.5+, 2000 to 2025. Its depth errors (median … km where given) can move a quake across the 20 km line between on the plate and inside it. Plate boundaries are Bird's PB2002 model (2003); slab surfaces and contours are USGS Slab2 (Hayes et al. 2018). Where two slabs overlap, under Kanto, the nearer is taken, for the quakes and the random control alike. The 2018 hold-out compares free depths only, against 2000 to 2016. Mechanisms are Global CMT solutions of Mw 5.5+ within 60 s and 100 km of a catalog event (… of … M5.5+ quakes), classed by P, T and B axis plunge with the limits of Frohlich and Apperson (1992); the match to a slab thrust is a Kagan angle. Seaward means no slab beneath and on the sinking-plate side of the nearest PB2002 subduction boundary. The Nankai box is 31.5 to 34.5°N, 132 to 137.5°E, the Tohoku box 36.5 to 39.5°N, 141.5 to 147°E, both shallower than 60 km. Dispersion tests are chi-square over 312 months. At M4.5 the deep row's variance ratio is 1.26 (p = 0.001), but its yearly count falls from about 23 in 2000 to 2010 to 17 since 2012 while M5+ goes from 7.5 to 6.3; the shallow M4.5 rate rises from about 320 to 530 a year while M5+ goes from 92 to 97. Tohoku aftershocks feed part of any rise, but most of the M4.5 change is the catalog.
