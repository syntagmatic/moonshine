Tsunami Propagation

The 2011 tsunami killed more than 15,000 people and crossed the Pacific in under a day. Its speed is set by ocean depth, and shallow coastal water makes it grow.

Part 5 showed tsunamis striking the Sanriku coast again and again, from Jogan in 869 to 2011. This part follows the 2011 tsunami to ask how an earthquake makes one, how fast it crosses an ocean, and why it grows so tall at the coast. At 14:46 local time on March 11, 2011, a magnitude 9.1 earthquake ruptured a 500-kilometer stretch of the Japan Trench, roughly 70 km off the coast of Miyagi Prefecture. The tsunami it generated killed more than 15,000 people and triggered the Fukushima Daiichi meltdown.

How a Tsunami Forms

A tsunami is a shallow water wave, a pulse that moves the entire water column from seafloor to surface. It starts when a large section of seafloor is abruptly displaced upward (or downward), shoving the water above it like a piston. The displaced water then radiates outward as a wave that carries a great deal of energy because the entire depth of the ocean is in motion.

Cross-section of tsunami generation. A shallow thrust fault lifts the seafloor, displacing the water column above it, which then radiates outward as waves.

The fault must be shallow (near the seafloor) and produce vertical displacement. The 2011 Tohoku earthquake began at a depth of about 29 km on a thrust fault, the Pacific Plate diving beneath the Okhotsk Plate. Portions of the seafloor shifted upward by as much as 5 to 8 meters over an area roughly the size of Kyushu.

Speed Depends on Depth

Tsunami speed follows from the shallow water approximation, which applies when the wavelength is much larger than the water depth, as it is for a tsunami.

where v is wave speed, g is gravitational acceleration (9.8 m/s²), and d is ocean depth. In the open Pacific, with average depths around 4,000 meters, this gives roughly 200 m/s, about 720 km/h. That is the speed of a commercial jet. Near the coast, where depths drop to 10 meters, the wave slows to about 36 km/h, roughly the speed of a bicycle.

This is also why you can't see a tsunami in the open ocean. Out in deep water it is perhaps 1 meter tall with a wavelength of 200 km or more, so the surface rises and falls by a meter over a distance far wider than Tokyo. A ship in the open Pacific would barely notice it passing beneath. It grows only as it enters shallow water.

Propagation Across the Pacific

The 2011 tsunami radiated outward from the epicenter at 38.3°N, 142.4°E. Within 30 minutes, the first waves reached the nearest coastline, the Sanriku coast of northeastern Honshu. Over the following hours, the wavefront crossed the Pacific Basin, reaching Hawaii about 7 hours after the quake, the U.S. West Coast around 10 hours, and the coast of Chile roughly 22 hours later.

The wavefront is not a perfect circle. Bathymetry (the shape of the ocean floor) bends and distorts it. Waves travel faster over deep ocean trenches and slower over ridges and seamounts. Ridges and seamount chains act as lenses, focusing energy toward some coasts and away from others. The map above ignores all of that and expands every ring at one speed, the speed at 5,000 m depth. At that one speed the arrivals at Hilo and Crescent City still come within about 3 percent of the observed ones, as the depth figure showed.

Shoaling

As a tsunami enters shallow water near the coast, it slows and grows taller, a process called shoaling. The front of the wave slows down (shallower water = lower speed), but the back of the wave is still in deeper water and moving faster. The wave compresses, and its wavelength shrinks from hundreds of kilometers to a few. The energy that was spread through 4 km of water column is concentrated into a few meters of depth. Shoaling alone makes the wave several times taller: by Green's law, about 5 to 8 times between 4,000 m of water and the last few meters. The bays and run-up described below do the rest.

As the tsunami moves from deep ocean to shallow coast, it slows down, compresses, and grows much taller.

On March 11, the Tohoku tsunami ran up parts of the Sanriku coast to heights exceeding 40 meters. The highest surveyed run-up, about 40.5 meters, was at Aneyoshi in Miyako. Shoaling explains only part of these heights. The shape of the coast amplified them further.

The Sanriku Coast

The Sanriku coast of northeastern Honshu has a distinctive ria topography: deep, narrow, V-shaped bays carved by rivers and then drowned by rising sea levels. When a tsunami enters one of these bays, the converging walls funnel and focus the wave energy into an increasingly narrow space. The wave height grows far beyond what open-coast shoaling alone would produce.

The same geography amplified tsunamis in 1896 (Meiji Sanriku, 38.2 m) and 1933 (Showa Sanriku, 28.7 m). The Sanriku coast has the highest tsunami risk in Japan because it faces the Japan Trench directly and has ria bays that act as natural amplifiers.

The Japan Trench lies about 200 km off the Pacific coast of Tohoku, and the 2011 rupture began only 70 km offshore. After a major trench earthquake, coastal communities may have as little as 15 to 30 minutes before the first tsunami arrives. Japan's early warning system issued an alert within 3 minutes of the 2011 quake, but the initial estimate severely underestimated the wave height (3 to 6 m predicted; 10 to 40 m actual). The system was later redesigned to better handle magnitude-9 events.

Why Not Every Earthquake Makes a Tsunami

Only some earthquakes produce dangerous tsunamis. Three conditions matter: the fault must be shallow, it must be a thrust (or normal) fault that produces vertical motion, and the earthquake must be large enough to displace a significant area of seafloor.

Three earthquakes compared. The shallow thrust moves the seafloor vertically and raises a tsunami; the deep rupture and the strike-slip fault barely move it. A shallow normal fault, not drawn here, can also raise one: the 1933 Sanriku tsunami came from a normal fault.

The 2011 Tohoku earthquake met all three: magnitude 9.1, shallow depth (29 km), thrust mechanism on the subduction interface, rupture area of roughly 500 × 200 km.

Deep earthquakes, even large ones, pose less tsunami risk because the rupture occurs far below the seafloor and the deformation is absorbed by the rock above before it reaches the ocean floor. Strike-slip faults (like the San Andreas) move rock horizontally past each other, which barely disturbs the water column.

Japan's Position

Japan sits at the intersection of four tectonic plates, but the tsunami hazard is concentrated on the Pacific coast. The Japan Trench and the Nankai Trough are both subduction zones where oceanic plates dive beneath Japan, producing the shallow thrust fault geometry that generates tsunamis. The Pacific coast of Tohoku faces the trench at close range. The Sea of Japan side has no comparable subduction trench and faces much lower tsunami risk.

The 2011 tsunami was the latest in a pattern on this coast that goes back centuries; what planners had underestimated was the maximum size. On the Tohoku coast the first waves can arrive within 15 to 30 minutes of a trench earthquake, and the shaking arrives sooner still. Part 7 looks at the warning Japan can give before the shaking.
