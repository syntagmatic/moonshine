How Often the Big Ones Come

Small earthquakes outnumber large ones at a steady ratio. Fitted to 26 years of Japan's catalog, that ratio estimates how often large earthquakes come, if the cutoff magnitude is chosen carefully, and a century of older earthquakes and Japan's official forecasts show where the line has to stop.

Part 3 showed one great earthquake dominating the catalog's energy. This part asks how often earthquakes that large come. Small earthquakes are common and large ones are rare, and the ratio between them is steady. In 1944 Beno Gutenberg and Charles Richter wrote it down: count every earthquake at or above magnitude M, and the count falls by roughly a factor of ten for each unit of magnitude. On a log scale that is a straight line, log10 N = a − bM. The slope b is called the b-value, and a b of 1 means exactly a factor of ten per magnitude unit.

A b-stability test (Cao and Gao 2002; Woessner and Wiemer 2005) asks whether b above a cutoff matches the average of b over the next half magnitude unit, to within one standard error, and the first cutoff that passes is M, marked on the axis. With the cutoff anywhere from M5.2 to M5.5 the prediction lands between and , close to the observed : a consequence of the choice, not the reason for it. The strip under the plot shows why the small end behaves differently. Below M5.0, % of events are measured with the body-wave magnitude mb, a quick estimate from the first seconds of the P wave. Above M5.5, % get a moment magnitude Mw, computed from the whole rupture. The two scales don't agree exactly for moderate earthquakes, so the lower part of the curve is measured on a different scale from the upper part.

A b-value is only as good as the cutoff and the magnitude scale behind it. With a consistent choice, the law gives a long-run average rate. It says nothing about when the next large earthquake will come, and the next section shows how far single years, and fits to single decades, stray from that average. Japan's official long-term forecasts for the great trench earthquakes are not built on this average either: the Headquarters for Earthquake Research Promotion (HERP) evaluates each source zone separately, with a renewal model where the history gives a series of dates and a plain Poisson rate where it does not (note 1 of its 2026 summary), and Part 5 shows one of them being built.

The line misses on both sides. It is low by about a quarter at M7 to M7.5 and high at M8.5 and above. The low side sits in the early decades: M7+ came a year in 1920–1963, in 1964–1999 and in 2000–2025, and the first of those eras holds the 1923 Kanto earthquake, the 1933 Sanriku earthquake, the 1944–46 Tonankai and Nankai pair, the 1952 Tokachi-oki earthquake and the Kuril earthquakes of 1958 and 1963. Older magnitudes may also run high. If their scatter is 0.2 units, counts above a threshold inflate by a factor of ; the per-event uncertainties that would settle it are not in the USGS file, so this is a lead, not a finding. Counting mainshocks only does not repair the tail. Their fit (b = ) predicts earthquakes of M8.5 or more in the 80 years against , and an M9 every years.

A fault system cannot give unlimited earthquakes, so the law has to taper. The tapered law (Kagan 2002) multiplies the line by a fall-off that sets in near a corner magnitude. Fitted to the 26 years, its best corner is M, and the 95% range runs from M upward with no upper end (the strip): the catalog says the corner is at least about M, not how big it is. Japan's official evaluation closes it from outside. HERP's summary of 1 January 2026 gives the Kuril Trench's 17th-century type (M8.8 or more) a mean interval of to years and the Japan Trench's Tohoku-oki type (M9.0) to years, both from tsunami deposits. Adding the two rates gives an M8.8 or more about every to years. That is a bracket, not a fit: each is one source's mean interval, HERP gives no rate for the whole box, and its 30-year probabilities ( for the Kuril source, almost 0% for the Japan Trench) are conditional on the time since the last event, unlike the Poisson chance in the readout. The plain law puts M8.8 or more every years. Each is a floor, since other sources add to the box's rate. So the catalog and the official intervals are consistent with a corner near M to M, and with an M9 as a once-in-several-centuries event, which is what 2011 was.
