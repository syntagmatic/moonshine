How Often the Big Ones Come

Small earthquakes outnumber large ones at a steady ratio. Fitted to 26 years of Japan's catalog, that ratio estimates how often large earthquakes come, if the cutoff magnitude is chosen carefully.

Part 3 showed one great earthquake dominating the catalog's energy. This part asks how often earthquakes that large come. Small earthquakes are common and large ones are rare, and the ratio between them is steady. In 1944 Beno Gutenberg and Charles Richter wrote it down: count every earthquake at or above magnitude M, and the count falls by roughly a factor of ten for each unit of magnitude. On a log scale that is a straight line, log10 N = a − bM. The slope b is called the b-value, and a b of 1 means exactly a factor of ten per magnitude unit.

The strip under the plot shows why the small end behaves differently. Below M5.0, % of events are measured with the body-wave magnitude mb, a quick estimate from the first seconds of the P wave. Above M5.5, % get a moment magnitude Mw, computed from the whole rupture. The two scales don't agree exactly for moderate earthquakes, so the lower part of the curve is measured on a different scale from the upper part.

A b-value is only as good as the cutoff and the magnitude scale behind it. With a consistent choice, the law gives a long-run average rate. It says nothing about when the next large earthquake will come, and the next section shows how far single years, and fits to single decades, stray from that average. The average is still what hazard maps, building codes and insurance models are built on.

Twenty-six years of instrumental data give average rates for moderate and large earthquakes. For the largest and rarest, the next part turns to Japan's written record, which reaches back more than thirteen centuries.
