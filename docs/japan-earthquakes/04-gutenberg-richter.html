How Often the Big Ones Come

Small earthquakes outnumber large ones at a steady ratio. Fitted to 26 years of Japan's catalog, that ratio gives a long-run rate for large earthquakes; a century of older quakes and Japan's official forecasts show where the line has to bend.

Small earthquakes are common and large ones rare, at a steady ratio. In 1944 Beno Gutenberg and Charles Richter wrote it down: count every earthquake at or above magnitude M, and the count falls by about a factor of ten for each unit of magnitude. On a log scale that is a straight line, log10 N = a − bM. The slope b is the b-value; b = 1 means exactly a factor of ten per unit.

The line misses on both sides: about a quarter low at M7 to M7.5, too high at M8.5 and above. The low side sits in the early decades. M7+ came a year in 1920–1963, in 1964–1999 and since 2000; the first era holds the 1923 Kanto, 1933 Sanriku, 1944–46 Tonankai and Nankai, and 1952 Tokachi-oki earthquakes. Older magnitudes may also run high (a 0.2-unit scatter would inflate counts by a factor of ), which the USGS file cannot settle. Counting mainshocks only makes the tail worse: their fit predicts earthquakes of M8.5 or more in the 80 years, against , and an M9 every years.

A fault system cannot make arbitrarily large earthquakes, so the law has to taper. The tapered law (Kagan 2002) multiplies the line by a fall-off that sets in near a corner magnitude. Fitted to the 26 years, its best corner is M, but the 95% range runs from M upward with no upper end (the strip): the catalog says the corner is at least about M, not how big it is.

Japan's Headquarters for Earthquake Research Promotion (HERP) gives an outside check. From tsunami deposits, its 1 January 2026 summary puts the Kuril Trench's 17th-century type (M8.8 or more) every to years on average and the Japan Trench's Tohoku-oki type (M9.0) every to . On that reading an M9 here is a once-in-several-centuries event, which is what 2011 was.
