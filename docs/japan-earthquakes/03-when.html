When the Ground Won't Stop Shaking

Twenty-six years of Japan's earthquakes played forward in time. Aftershock sequences burst out of a background of ordinary activity, and one great earthquake releases more energy than all the others combined.

Parts 1 and 2 looked at where Japan's earthquakes happen and why. This part looks at when. How earthquake sequences unfold

Some of Japan's seismicity is a steady background: earthquakes scattered across the map with no large shock before them, ordinary stress release along plate boundaries and faults. It is the baseline that sequences stand out from, but it is a smaller part of the catalog than it looks. From 2001 to 2010 the catalog averaged 1.01 new M4.5+ dots a day, and most of those followed an earlier earthquake: in the box around Tohoku used below, the model counts only about 26% of M5+ events as background.

A sequence starts when a major earthquake changes the stress field in the surrounding crust. Faults that were close to failure slip, and the result is a burst of aftershocks that can last weeks or months, and after a great earthquake, years. In the animation, the Tohoku M9.1 on March 11, 2011 produces a dense swarm of dots along the entire northeast coast of Honshu. The catalog holds 530 aftershocks of M4.5 or larger in the first 24 hours alone.

For comparison, the 2001–2010 catalog averaged 0.29 M5+ events a day, sequences included. Over the 30 days after the mainshock it counts 572, about 67 times that rate. The stress change also reached faults far from the rupture: within 15 hours a M6.2 struck near the Nagano-Niigata border and a M6.2 off Akita, on the opposite coast, and 4 days later a M6.0 shook the foot of Mount Fuji. The largest aftershock in the box was a M7.9, 29 minutes after the mainshock and 1.2 magnitude units below it. Båth's law, an old rule of thumb, says the largest aftershock is about 1.2 units smaller than the mainshock on average.

How aftershocks fade

The map's 30-day fade hides how long a sequence really lasts. To count Tohoku's aftershocks over years, the chart below uses a fixed box, 34.5° to 42°N and 139° to 146°E. It covers the rupture along the Japan Trench, the aftershocks under the coast to the west and the outer-rise events seaward of the trench to the east, and it holds 2,038 of the 2,080 catalog events in the first 30 days. Two choices need explaining. The chart counts M5.0 and above, not the map's M4.5 and above, because the catalog changed around 2010: it lists 2.5 events of M4.5–4.9 for every M5+ event in 2000–2009 and 4.4 from 2012, while M5+ events held at 107 and 115 a year. Counts at M4.5 therefore move with the catalog and not only with the ground, so fits and rates here start at M5 (the buttons let you see M4.5 and M5.5). The map keeps every quake. And the flat floor is the model's background, not a count of 2001–2010: those years include the 2003 Tokachi-oki sequence, so a raw count would overstate what happens anyway.

The rate falls roughly as 1/t. Fusakichi Omori found this in the aftershocks of the 1891 Nobi earthquake, and Tokuji Utsu later generalized it with an exponent p. Fitted from the mainshock on, the dashed curve gives p = 1.02 at M5, close enough to 1 that each tenfold stretch of time holds about as many events as the one before: days 1 to 10 hold 187 M5+ events in the box, days 10 to 100 hold 162. The constant c, where the curve bends flat at the start, is mostly the catalog. In the first tenth of a day, 99% of the box events the catalog lists are M5 or larger, against 34% in 2001–2010, because small events are lost in the coda of larger ones; by Helmstetter, Kagan and Jackson's formula the catalog is complete only above M5.35 at 0.1 day after an M9.1 and does not reach M4.5 until day 1.4. Fit the same curve to M4.5+, M5+ and M5.5+ events and c shrinks from 0.26 to 0.08 to 0.03 days: the old ten hours were mostly the catalog's. The ETAS fit, which leaves the incomplete minutes and hours out, puts c at 8 minutes and p at 1.07 ± 0.02, and its c is poorly determined (a factor of 1.4 either way, one standard error). Jalilian's fit of the same model to the JMA catalog of 1953–1990, which holds no M9, gives p = 1.15 and c = 43 minutes, and counts 55% of events as background.

How long does a sequence last? The model answers with a share instead of a date. Of the box's M5+ events, 69% in 2012, 46% in 2014 and 34% in 2016 descend from the Tohoku mainshock, falling to 18% in 2024; the rest are background or belong to other sequences. Counting only direct aftershocks, the mainshock accounts for about 31% of the events the model expects after the first day; the rest are aftershocks of aftershocks, other sequences and background. This replaces an earlier reading of the M4.5 counts, which had the aftershock rate falling below the old background after seven years and the box still at 1.3 times its 2001–2010 rate in 2023 and 2024. That was reporting: the box's M5+ rate was back to 26 a year over 2017–2024 against 30 in 2001–2010. The M4.5 button shows what happens otherwise: the model reads the later rise in small events as triggering and counts only 7% as background. The model also misses. Its branching ratio, the number of direct aftershocks per event averaged over magnitudes up to the mainshock's, comes out at 1.00, at the edge of what a stable sequence can have, which is a known bias when early aftershocks are missing and one sequence dominates. Its transformed times miss a uniform spread (KS distance 0.047 against 0.035 for 95%). And it follows time only, so triggering across the box's edges and in faraway places like Nagano, Akita and Fuji is not counted.

Energy and the magnitude scale

The magnitude scale is logarithmic: each whole number represents about 32 times more energy. Add up the energy of all 16,787 earthquakes on this map and the M9.1 Tohoku mainshock accounts for 86% of it, about 6.3 times everything else combined. In the sparkline, the cumulative energy line is essentially flat for most of the timeline, then jumps vertically on March 11, 2011.

Put another way, to match the energy of one M9.1 earthquake, you would need about 45,000 earthquakes of M6.0, or about 8 million of M4.5. The whole catalog holds 343 events of M6.0 or larger. Even its second-largest event, the M8.2 Tokachi-oki earthquake of 2003 (8.16 before rounding), released only about 1/26 of Tohoku's energy.

The full timeline has several other bursts: the 2003 Tokachi-oki earthquake off Hokkaido, the 2016 Kumamoto earthquakes (a foreshock on April 14, then a larger mainshock 28 hours later), the 2024 Noto Peninsula earthquake on New Year's Day, and recurring offshore swarms east of Honshu. Each has a different character. Kumamoto was shallow and destructive. Noto struck a region with many traditional wooden buildings. The offshore events mostly dissipate into the Pacific. Magnitudes on this page are the USGS catalog values, which can differ from the Japan Meteorological Agency's: USGS gives Kumamoto M7.0 and Noto M7.5, where JMA gives 7.3 and 7.6.

What this means for daily life

If you're living in Japan, the background seismicity is always present. The shindo scale matters more than magnitude for how it actually feels. A deep M6.0 hundreds of kilometers offshore might produce only a gentle sway in Tokyo, while a shallow M5.5 directly beneath the city is a sharp jolt that knocks things off shelves. The animation does not show intensity, but it does show spatial patterns: some regions are consistently more active than others, and the offshore trench east of Honshu is the single biggest source of earthquakes.

The plates are still moving, so another great earthquake will come. Knowing what the background looks like makes a major sequence easier to recognize. The next part asks how often large earthquakes come, by comparing how many small and large ones the catalog holds.
