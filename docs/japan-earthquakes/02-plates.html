Tectonic Plate Geometry of Japan

Four tectonic plates meet under Japan. In cross-section, earthquake depths trace the sinking plates and show why each region faces a different kind of earthquake.

The Four-Plate Junction

The Pacific Plate pushes in from the east and the Philippine Sea Plate from the south. Japan itself straddles the boundary between the Eurasian Plate (western Japan) and the North American Plate (eastern Japan, including Tohoku).

The mechanism is subduction: when an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate dives underneath. It does not slide smoothly. It locks, builds up stress for decades or centuries, then slips suddenly in an earthquake. The deeper the plate goes, the deeper its earthquakes, a sloping band that Kiyoo Wadati mapped in the 1930s and Hugo Benioff in the 1940s: the Wadati-Benioff zone.

The Pacific Plate and the Japan Trench

The Pacific Plate is the oldest and fastest of the four. At the Japan Trench it is 133 to 135 million years old and moves west-northwest at about 8.9 cm a year, diving under the North American Plate. Old plate is cold and dense, which suggests a steep dive. Slab2, the published slab model drawn in the figures, says age alone does not set the angle: the same plate dips about 28° under Tohoku and 62° under the Bonin Islands, off seafloor of nearly the same age (134 and 143 million years).

Select transect A–A′, across northern Tohoku. Near the trench the earthquakes are shallow, mostly 10 to 60 km. Westward the slab top, the blue line, sinks at 22 to 29 degrees, and the band of hypocenters follows it from inside: the median event below 70 km lies 23 km under the slab top. Between 250 and 350 km the line has only 2 events, but Slab2 carries the slab straight through at about 27 degrees, so the lull is a quiet stretch of slab, not a gap in it. The band returns at 390 to 570 km under the Sea of Japan and the Russian coast, more than 1,000 km from where the plate went down.

The 2011 Tohoku earthquake (M9.1) ruptured about 500 km of this interface. The locked contact slipped all the way to the trench and moved the seafloor there about 50 meters east. Slip that shallow lifts the seafloor directly, which is what made the tsunami so large.

South of Tokyo the Pacific Plate dives under the Philippine Sea Plate instead, at the Izu-Bonin Trench. Select transect D–D′, across the Bonin Islands at 28.5°N. Here the slab top falls from 100 to 400 km deep in just 157 km, an apparent dip of 62 degrees, and its true dip reaches 69 degrees at 300 to 400 km. The hypocenters lie inside the slab, a median 36 km under its top below 70 km and 42 km below 400 km, so a dip read off the dots overstates the slab's. At 420 to 520 km the band spreads westward. This one corridor holds 141 events deeper than 300 km, including the deepest in the catalog.

The Philippine Sea Plate and the Nankai Trough

The Philippine Sea Plate subducts under southwestern Japan along the Nankai Trough. At 17 to 30 million years old it is far younger than the Pacific Plate, which suggests a shallower dive. Select C–C′: near the trench the two slabs dip alike (a mean of 13° under A–A′ and 12° under C–C′ between 10 and 50 km), and from 100 to 200 km the Philippine Sea slab is the steeper, 64° against 29°. What the younger plate does differently is go quiet. Its M4.5+ events stop at about 140 km, though Slab2 follows the slab to 352 km, while the Pacific slab under Tohoku breaks down to 582 km. A young, warm slab should stop breaking sooner, which fits but is untested here. 7 of the 7 deep events at the far end of C–C′ lie inside the Pacific slab, which continues west beneath.

The Nankai Trough has produced some of Japan's most destructive earthquakes. The 1707 Hoei earthquake (estimated M8.4 to 8.6) ruptured its full length, and 49 days later Mt. Fuji began its most recent eruption. The government's Earthquake Research Committee put the chance of an M8–9 Nankai earthquake within 30 years at about 80% until September 2025, when it gave two estimates side by side, 60–90% or more under one model and 20–50% under another, without choosing between them.

The Kanto Triple Junction

Under Tokyo there are two slabs, one above the other. Select transect B–B′. The Philippine Sea Plate subducts under the North American Plate, and the Pacific Plate subducts under both of them. This is one of the few places on Earth where two oceanic plates go down beneath a third. Sorted by Slab2 surface, the 376 events deeper than 30 km under Kanto split into 239 in the Pacific slab only, 44 in the Philippine Sea slab only, 25 in both and 68 in neither. The sort is partial. Slab2's Philippine Sea slab stops at 139.9°E, and at M4.5+ the two layers merge into one cloud at 40 to 90 km. Japan's dense networks, which catch far smaller events, resolve the two.

The 1923 Great Kanto earthquake (M7.9), which destroyed much of Tokyo, ruptured the boundary between the Philippine Sea and North American plates.

Shallow and Deep Earthquakes

Shallow crustal earthquakes (under ~20 km) break the brittle upper crust of the overriding plate. They tend to be smaller, but they are close to the surface. The 1995 Kobe earthquake (M6.9) was only 16 km deep, directly beneath the city, and killed over 6,400 people.

Subduction zone earthquakes happen on the plate interface near the trench and, deeper, inside the sinking slab, down to 683 km in this catalog. Depth usually softens the shaking, but not always. In May 2015 an M7.8 earthquake 664 km below the Bonin Islands, about 870 km south of Tokyo, shook central Tokyo at JMA intensity 4 and parts of Kanagawa at 5-upper. It was felt in all 47 prefectures and did little damage.

The histogram does not simply thin out downward. 82% of the earthquakes are 60 km deep or shallower. Below that the counts fall to just 39 between 250 and 300 km, then rise again to 258 between 350 and 450 km, 200 of them under the Izu-Bonin arc. Intermediate-depth earthquakes are thought to happen where water driven out of the warming slab weakens it, and most of that water is gone by about 300 km, so the deeper population needs another way to break. Below 525 km the counts collapse: only 3 events lie deeper than 660 km.

The dashed lines at 410 and 660 km bound the mantle transition zone, where pressure repacks olivine, the upper mantle's main mineral, into denser crystal structures; at 660 km it breaks down into lower-mantle minerals. How rock breaks at these pressures is still debated. One leading idea is that the cold core of a fast-sinking slab carries olivine down before it can convert. When it finally does, the change runs away along thin sheets that slip like faults. Below 660 km no such olivine survives, and the stiffer lower mantle slows the slab, which thickens and bends instead of breaking. Deep earthquakes stop near the base of the transition zone worldwide, as they do here.

In every cross-section the earthquakes happen inside the cold slab, which stays strong enough to fail suddenly long after the mantle around it has grown too hot to. Of the 2,254 events at 70 km or deeper, 85 percent lie 0 to 50 km under the Slab2 top surface, and they sit farther in as they go down: the median event lies 20 km under the top at 70 to 150 km and 40 km under it at 450 to 600 km.

Slab2 is built partly from earthquake locations, so its agreement with the dots proves less than it seems. Volcanoes are an independent test: arc volcanoes are expected to stand over slab roughly 100 km deep. Under the 40 Holocene volcanoes of the Northeast Japan arc the Slab2 surface is a median 109 km deep, and under the volcanic front 88 km (range 84 to 130). The Izu, Kuril and Ogasawara arcs give 106, 97 and 134 km. The model passes this test.

The model and the catalog disagree in three places. The deepest earthquakes, the 2015 Bonin M7.8 and the other 2 events below 660 km, lie 169 to 182 km from the nearest point of the Izu-Bonin slab, which Slab2 ends at 591 km (648 km in its supplement for the overturned part). On transect C, 15 of the 17 events at 70 km or deeper near the Philippine Sea slab lie above its top, a median 10 km above. Slab2 gives an uncertainty of 15 to 21 km there, but nearly all the misses are on one side: either the slab is drawn too deep under Shikoku and the Bungo Channel, or the catalog's depths run shallow. And a 2023 earthquake 645 km under the North Korea–Russia border lies 76 km from the Pacific slab model.

What the Geometry Means for Preparedness

Japan's hazard maps, building codes and tsunami warnings are built on this three-dimensional picture. In Tohoku the main risk is the Pacific Plate interface: shallow, offshore and tsunami-generating. In Osaka and Shikoku it is the Nankai Trough. In Tokyo it is the crust and both slabs beneath it.

Data: USGS earthquake catalog, M4.5+, 2000–2025. The Kanto sort takes events deeper than 30 km in 35–36.5°N, 139.3–140.5°E, with a window of 10 km above to 40 km below each slab top. Dips are measured on the Slab2 surface: apparent dips along the section line, true dips along the direction of steepest descent. The volcanic front is the easternmost volcano in each half degree of latitude from 36° to 41°N. The 70 km cut used in the slab counts also keeps out events logged at the catalog's default depths of 10, 33 and 35 km.
