Tectonic Plate Geometry of Japan

Japan sits where four tectonic plates meet. Cross-sections through the catalog show the sinking plates traced by earthquake depths, and explain why different regions face different kinds of earthquake.

The map in Part 1 showed earthquakes bunched along the trenches, with the deepest ones under the Izu-Bonin arc. This part explains that pattern from the geometry of the four plates that meet under Japan, using cross-sections in which the earthquakes trace the plates as they sink.

The Four-Plate Junction

Japan sits on the boundaries of four tectonic plates. The Pacific Plate pushes in from the east, the Philippine Sea Plate from the south, and Japan itself straddles the boundary between the Eurasian Plate (western Japan) and the North American Plate (eastern Japan, including the Tohoku region). Each of these boundaries generates earthquakes in its own characteristic way.

The basic mechanism is subduction: when an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate dives underneath. The sinking plate does not slide smoothly. It locks, builds up stress for decades or centuries, and then slips suddenly in an earthquake. The deeper the plate goes, the deeper the earthquake. Kiyoo Wadati mapped this pattern in the 1930s and Hugo Benioff in the 1940s. We now call it the Wadati-Benioff zone.

The Pacific Plate and the Japan Trench

The Pacific Plate is the biggest, oldest, and fastest-moving of the four. It's about 130 million years old where it meets Japan, which makes it cold and dense, and so it sinks steeply into the mantle. It moves west-northwest at roughly 8–10 cm per year, diving under the North American Plate along the Japan Trench, which runs from off Hokkaido down past the Boso Peninsula.

Select transect A–A′ above, which crosses northern Tohoku. Near the trench the earthquakes are shallow, mostly 10 to 60 km, on and just above the plate interface. Moving west, the band of hypocenters angles downward at roughly 20 to 30 degrees: about 100 km deep under the Pacific coast, 150 to 200 km under the Sea of Japan coast. This is the Wadati-Benioff zone. Between about 250 and 350 km the catalog has few events, a lull seen in many slabs, and then the band reappears at 400 to 650 km beneath the Sea of Japan and the Russian coast, more than 1,000 km from where the plate went down.

The 2011 Tohoku earthquake (M9.1) ruptured about 500 km of this interface along the Japan Trench. It was a shallow subduction earthquake: the locked contact between the Pacific and North American plates slipped by as much as 50 meters, and the slip reached all the way to the trench. That shallow slip lifted the seafloor directly and is what made the tsunami so large.

South of Tokyo the Pacific Plate dives under the Philippine Sea Plate instead, at the Izu-Bonin Trench. Select transect D–D′, which crosses the Bonin Islands at 28.5°N. Here the slab goes down far more steeply than under Tohoku. About 200 km west of the trench the band of hypocenters is roughly 100 km deep; less than 100 km farther west it is at 400 to 450 km, a dip of about 75°. It then spreads westward at 400 to 530 km. This one corridor holds 141 events deeper than 300 km, including the deepest in the catalog.

The Philippine Sea Plate and the Nankai Trough

The Philippine Sea Plate comes from the south, subducting under southwestern Japan along the Nankai Trough. It's younger and warmer than the Pacific Plate, only about 15 to 50 million years old, so it subducts at a shallower angle. Select transect C–C′ to see the contrast. The Philippine Sea slab produces M4.5+ events only down to about 140 km, where the Pacific slab under Tohoku stays active to 650 km. The handful of deep events at the far end of C–C′ belong to the Pacific slab, which continues westward beneath the Philippine Sea Plate.

The Nankai Trough has produced some of Japan's most destructive historical earthquakes. The 1707 Hoei earthquake (estimated M8.4 to 8.6) ruptured the full length of the trough, and 49 days later Mt. Fuji began its most recent eruption. The subduction zone is currently locked and accumulating strain. The government's Earthquake Research Committee put the chance of an M8–9 Nankai Trough earthquake within 30 years at about 80% in January 2025. In September 2025 it split the estimate: 60–90% or more under its main model, 20–50% under an alternative one.

The Kanto Triple Junction

The plate geometry under Tokyo is unusually complex. Select transect B–B′ to see it. Here, the Philippine Sea Plate is subducting under the North American Plate, while the Pacific Plate subducts under both of them. The result is a double slab: two plates sinking at different angles, one on top of the other. At M4.5 and above the catalog cannot pull them apart; beneath Kanto their earthquakes merge into one cloud at 30 to 120 km. Japan's dense seismic networks, which record far smaller events, resolve them as two separate layers. This triple junction of the Pacific, Philippine Sea, and North American plates is one of the only places on Earth where two oceanic plates subduct beneath a third.

This complexity produces earthquakes at almost every depth: shallow crustal quakes in the compressed overriding plate, intermediate-depth quakes in the Philippine Sea slab, and deeper quakes in the Pacific slab below it. The 1923 Great Kanto earthquake (M7.9) that destroyed much of Tokyo occurred on the boundary between the Philippine Sea and North American plates.

Shallow and Deep Earthquakes

A map hides depth, one of the most important features of Japan's seismicity. The cross-sections separate the earthquakes into two broad categories.

Shallow crustal earthquakes (under ~20 km) occur in the brittle upper crust of the overriding plate. They tend to be smaller in magnitude but can be extremely damaging because they're so close to the surface. The 1995 Kobe earthquake (M6.9) was a shallow crustal event, only 16 km deep. It killed over 6,400 people despite being a "moderate" magnitude, because the rupture was directly beneath the city.

Subduction zone earthquakes occur on or near the plate interface and range from shallow (near the trench) to very deep, as far down as 683 km, the deepest event in this catalog. The deeper ones are generally less damaging at the surface because energy dissipates over distance, but they can be felt across huge areas, and deep does not always mean gentle. In May 2015 an M7.8 earthquake 664 km below the Bonin Islands, about 870 km south of Tokyo, shook central Tokyo at JMA intensity 4 and parts of Kanagawa at 5-upper, and was felt in all 47 prefectures, though it did little damage.

The histogram with the map counts the whole catalog by depth, and the counts do not simply thin out downward. 82% of the earthquakes are 60 km deep or shallower. Below that the counts fall steeply, to just 39 events between 250 and 300 km, and then rise again, to 258 between 350 and 450 km, 200 of them under the Izu-Bonin arc. Intermediate-depth earthquakes are thought to happen where water driven out of the warming slab weakens it, and most of that water is gone by about 300 km. The deeper population needs another way to break. Below 525 km the counts collapse, and only 3 events lie deeper than 660 km.

The dashed lines at 410 and 660 km bound the mantle transition zone. Within it, the pressure repacks olivine, the upper mantle's main mineral, into denser crystal structures, and at 660 km it breaks down into the minerals of the lower mantle. At these pressures rock should not fracture in the ordinary way, and how deep earthquakes happen is still debated. One leading idea is that the cold core of a fast-sinking slab carries olivine down before it has time to convert; when it finally does, the change runs away along thin sheets that slip like faults. No such olivine survives past 660 km, and the stiffer lower mantle below slows the slab, which thickens and bends there instead of breaking. So deep earthquakes stop near the base of the transition zone, worldwide as in this catalog, whose deepest lies at 683 km.

In the cross-sections, the sloping band of dots is the subducting plate. The earthquakes happen inside the cold slab, which stays strong enough to fail suddenly long after the mantle around it has grown too hot to do so. So the shape of the band traces the shape of the plate.

What the Geometry Means for Preparedness

This geometry has practical consequences. Japan's seismic hazard maps, building codes, and tsunami warning systems are all built on this three-dimensional picture of where the plates are and how they move. The type of earthquake you should prepare for depends on where you live relative to these subducting slabs.

If you're in Tohoku, the big risk is the Pacific Plate interface: shallow, offshore, tsunami-generating. If you're in Osaka or Shikoku, it's the Nankai Trough. Under Tokyo, earthquakes come from the crust, the Philippine Sea slab and the Pacific slab below it.
