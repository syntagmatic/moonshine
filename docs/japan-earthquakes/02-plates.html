Tectonic Plate Geometry of Japan

Why one of the most seismically active places on Earth sits where four tectonic plates collide

If you live in Japan, you already know earthquakes in your body. The phone alarm, the few seconds of deciding whether to move, the specific pitch of the shaking that tells you how far away it was. But the why comes down to geometry: Japan sits at one of the most complicated tectonic junctions on the planet. Four separate plates converge in a space smaller than California.

The Four-Plate Junction

Most countries sit on one tectonic plate. Japan sits on the boundary of four. The Pacific Plate pushes in from the east, the Philippine Sea Plate from the south, and Japan itself straddles the boundary between the Eurasian Plate (western Japan) and the North American Plate (eastern Japan, including the Tohoku region). Each of these boundaries generates earthquakes in its own characteristic way.

The basic mechanism is subduction: when an oceanic plate meets a continental plate, the denser oceanic plate dives underneath. This diving plate doesn't slide smoothly. It catches and locks, building up stress for decades or centuries, then lurches forward in an earthquake. The deeper the plate goes, the deeper the earthquake. Kiyoo Wadati mapped this pattern in the 1930s and Hugo Benioff in the 1940s. We now call it the Wadati-Benioff zone.

The Pacific Plate and the Japan Trench

The Pacific Plate is the biggest, oldest, and fastest-moving of the four. It's about 130 million years old where it meets Japan, which makes it cold and dense. Those are exactly the properties that cause it to plunge steeply into the mantle. It moves west-northwest at roughly 8–10 cm per year, diving under the North American Plate along the Japan Trench, a gash in the seafloor that runs from off Hokkaido down past the Boso Peninsula.

Select transect A–A′ above, which crosses northern Tohoku. Near the trench the earthquakes are shallow, mostly 10 to 60 km, on and just above the plate interface. Moving west, the band of hypocenters angles downward at roughly 20 to 30 degrees: about 100 km deep under the Pacific coast, 150 to 200 km under the Sea of Japan coast. This is the Wadati-Benioff zone. Between about 250 and 350 km the catalog has few events, a lull seen in many slabs, and then the band reappears at 400 to 650 km beneath the Sea of Japan and the Russian coast, more than 1,000 km from where the plate went down.

The 2011 Tohoku earthquake (M9.1) ruptured about 500 km of this interface along the Japan Trench. It was a shallow subduction earthquake: the locked contact between the Pacific and North American plates slipped by as much as 50 meters, and the slip reached all the way to the trench. That shallow slip lifted the seafloor directly and is what made the tsunami so large.

The Philippine Sea Plate and the Nankai Trough

The Philippine Sea Plate comes from the south, subducting under southwestern Japan along the Nankai Trough. It's younger and warmer than the Pacific Plate, only about 15 to 50 million years old, so it subducts at a shallower angle. Select transect C–C′ to see the contrast. The Philippine Sea slab produces M4.5+ events only down to about 140 km, where the Pacific slab under Tohoku stays active to 650 km. The handful of deep events at the far end of C–C′ belong to the Pacific slab, which continues westward beneath the Philippine Sea Plate.

The Nankai Trough has produced some of Japan's most destructive historical earthquakes. The 1707 Hoei earthquake (estimated M8.4 to 8.6) ruptured the full length of the trough, and 49 days later Mt. Fuji began its most recent eruption. The subduction zone is currently locked and accumulating strain. The government's Earthquake Research Committee put the chance of an M8–9 Nankai Trough earthquake within 30 years at about 80% in January 2025. In September 2025 it split the estimate: 60–90% or more under its main model, 20–50% under an alternative one.

The Kanto Triple Junction

The area around Tokyo is geologically outrageous. Select transect B–B′ and you'll see why. Here, the Philippine Sea Plate is subducting under the North American Plate, while the Pacific Plate subducts under both of them. You get a double slab: two plates diving at different angles, crossing at depth. This triple junction of the Pacific, Philippine Sea, and North American plates is one of the only places on Earth where two oceanic plates subduct beneath a third.

This complexity produces earthquakes at almost every depth: shallow crustal quakes in the compressed overriding plate, intermediate-depth quakes in the Philippine Sea slab, and deeper quakes in the Pacific slab below it. The 1923 Great Kanto earthquake (M7.9) that destroyed much of Tokyo occurred on the boundary between the Philippine Sea and North American plates.

Shallow vs. Deep: Two Kinds of Danger

A map alone hides one of the most important features of Japan's seismicity: depth. The cross-sections reveal that earthquakes fall into two very different categories.

Shallow crustal earthquakes (under ~20 km) occur in the brittle upper crust of the overriding plate. They tend to be smaller in magnitude but can be extremely damaging because they're so close to the surface. The 1995 Kobe earthquake (M6.9) was a shallow crustal event, only 16 km deep. It killed over 6,400 people despite being a "moderate" magnitude, because the rupture was directly beneath the city.

Subduction zone earthquakes occur on or near the plate interface and range from shallow (near the trench) to very deep, as far down as about 680 km, the deepest event in this catalog. The deeper ones are generally less damaging at the surface because energy dissipates over distance, but they can be felt across huge areas. A 500 km deep earthquake under the Sea of Japan will rattle buildings in Tokyo, but gently.

In the cross-sections, the sloping band of dots is the subducting plate. The earthquakes happen inside the cold slab, which stays strong enough to fail suddenly long after the mantle around it has grown too hot to do so. So the shape of the band traces the shape of the plate.

What the Geometry Means for Preparedness

This geometry has practical consequences. Japan's seismic hazard maps, building codes, and tsunami warning systems are all built on this three-dimensional picture of where the plates are and how they move. The type of earthquake you should prepare for depends on where you live relative to these subducting slabs.

If you're in Tohoku, the big risk is the Pacific Plate interface: shallow, offshore, tsunami-generating. If you're in Osaka or Shikoku, it's the Nankai Trough. If you're in Tokyo, it's everything at once.
