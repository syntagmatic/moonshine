Earthquake Early Warning

The damaging S wave travels slower than the P wave ahead of it. Japan's early warning system turns that gap into seconds of warning, with real limits.

Part 6 ended with tsunamis that reach the nearest coast 15 to 30 minutes after a trench earthquake. The shaking arrives far sooner, and Japan's system tries to warn people before it does. If you've lived in Japan you know the alert: a warbling tone from your phone, tididididi, tididididi, sometimes seconds before the ground starts to shake. This part covers where those seconds come from and what they can be used for.

P Waves and S Waves

When a fault ruptures, it sends out two kinds of seismic waves. P-waves (primary, or compression waves) move fast, about 6 km/s through the crust. They're the first to arrive, but they're relatively gentle: a jolt, a rattling of dishes. S-waves (secondary, or shear waves) are slower, around 3.5 km/s, but they cause the strong shaking that throws people off their feet and brings down buildings.

Early warning lives in the time between the two arrivals, but not the gap at your own house. Seismometers near the source catch the P wave first. Once two of them have it, the system works out where the quake is and how big, and an alert goes out to everyone at once. Your warning is the time from that alert until the S wave reaches you.

At these speeds the S wave falls behind the P wave by about 12 seconds for every 100 km, so a seismometer 100 km from the epicenter sees the two arrive about that far apart. The alert doesn't wait for the P wave to reach you, though. Once it is out, each extra 100 km buys about 29 more seconds, the time the S wave needs to cross that distance. From the 2011 Tohoku epicenter, Tokyo's own P and S waves arrive 44 s apart, but in the model below an ideal alert, out 22.6 s after the rupture, reaches Tokyo 84 s ahead of the S wave, and Sendai, 131 km away, 16 s ahead. In 2011 itself Tokyo got no public warning at all; a later section explains why.

How JMA's System Works

Japan's Earthquake Early Warning system, operated by the Japan Meteorological Agency, is among the fastest in the world. The process goes roughly like this:

1. Detect. A dense network of over 1,000 seismometers blankets Japan. When the P-wave from an earthquake reaches the nearest station, it's detected within a fraction of a second.

2. Locate. Once two or more stations have picked up the P-wave, the system can locate the epicenter and estimate the depth. This happens within about 3–5 seconds of the first detection.

3. Estimate. Using the initial waveform data, algorithms estimate the earthquake's magnitude. This is the hardest step. Early estimates can be significantly off, especially for very large events. In 2011, the initial estimate was M7.2; the earthquake turned out to be M9.1 (9.0 on JMA's scale).

4. Predict shaking. Based on magnitude, depth, and distance, the system predicts the seismic intensity at locations across the country. Areas expected to experience shaking of intensity 4 or above on the JMA scale receive warnings.

5. Broadcast. Alerts go out simultaneously through TV, radio, cell phones (that distinctive sound), factory control systems, train operations centers, and elevator controllers. The whole chain from detection to broadcast can take as little as 3 seconds.

On March 11, 2011, the public warning went out about 8 seconds after the first station detected the P wave. Because it was based on the first estimate of M7.2, it covered only the Tohoku prefectures nearest the rupture: Miyagi, Iwate, Fukushima, Akita and Yamagata. Kanto was never in the warned area. Some Tokyo residents saw the Tohoku alert on national television before the shaking reached them, but no warning was issued for Tokyo itself. In this page's model, an ideal alert would have come 16 s before the S wave reached Sendai, and only 0.7 s before it reached the tip of the Oshika Peninsula, the land closest to the epicenter. Separately, the Tohoku Shinkansen's own coastal seismometers cut power to its trains, and none of the trains in passenger service derailed.

Limitations

The most basic limit is distance. The alert can't go out until the P wave has reached two stations and been processed, and by then the S wave has already covered some ground. Inside that blind zone the S wave arrives before the alert. For the shallow quakes near land in the chart below, it reaches a median 27 km from the epicenter. For a shallow earthquake directly beneath a city, the phone alert might arrive after the shaking has already started.

Shaking comes from the Si and Midorikawa (1999) peak-velocity attenuation relation, from a point source at the hypocenter, amplified 1.4 times and converted to JMA intensity. A point source underpredicts shaking along a long fault, and the ground it misses lies mostly outside the blind zone, so for large quakes these no-warning shares lean high. Magnitude estimation is another weak point. The system uses the first few seconds of P-wave data to guess how big the earthquake is, but very large earthquakes don't reveal their full size immediately. The initial M7.2 estimate for the 2011 Tohoku event meant early warnings significantly underestimated the shaking intensity. JMA has since updated its algorithms to better handle cascading ruptures.

False alarms happen too, though JMA has worked hard to reduce them. A false alarm that sends factory workers scrambling or stops trains costs money and erodes trust in the system.

Early Warning Elsewhere

Japan's earthquake early warning system grew out of repeated disasters and large public investment. Other countries run or are building similar systems: Mexico's SASMEX, ShakeAlert on the US West Coast, and systems in China and Taiwan.
