Earthquake Early Warning

The damaging S wave travels slower than the P wave ahead of it. Japan's early warning system turns that gap into seconds of warning, with real limits.

If you've lived in Japan you know the alert: a warbling tone from your phone, tididididi, tididididi, sometimes seconds before the ground starts to shake. Those seconds come from the gap between two kinds of seismic wave, and close to the epicenter they run out.

P Waves and S Waves

When a fault ruptures it sends out two kinds of seismic wave. P waves (compression) travel about 6 km/s through the crust and arrive first, as a jolt or a rattle of dishes. S waves (shear) travel about 3.5 km/s and carry the strong shaking that throws people off their feet and brings down buildings.

Early warning lives between the two arrivals, but not in the gap at your own house. Seismometers near the source catch the P wave first; once two have it, the system estimates where the quake is and how big, and an alert goes out to everyone at once. Your warning runs from that alert until the S wave reaches you, about … s more for each extra 100 km. In 2011 Tokyo got no public warning at all.

How JMA's System Works

The Japan Meteorological Agency's system works in five steps.

1. Detect. More than 1,000 seismometers cover Japan, and the nearest one picks up the P wave within a fraction of a second.

2. Locate. Once two or more stations have the P wave, the system fixes the epicenter and depth. In JMA's records the first report comes … to … s after the first detection (median …).

3. Estimate. The magnitude comes from the first seconds of waveform, and this is the hardest step: those seconds don't reveal the size of a very large earthquake. In 2011 the first report said M…, the public warning M…, and the estimate never passed M…, reached … s after the origin time, for a quake that was M9.1 (9.0 on JMA's scale). JMA has since updated its algorithms to handle cascading ruptures.

4. Predict shaking. From magnitude, depth and distance it predicts intensity across the country, and areas expected to reach intensity 4 or more on JMA's scale are warned.

5. Broadcast. Alerts go out at once over TV, radio and phones, and to factory controls, train operations centers and elevators.

Limitations

A few seconds is enough to get under a desk; ten or more lets people move away from windows, elevators stop and trains slow. The basic limit is distance. The alert can't go out until the P wave has reached two stations and been processed, and inside that blind zone the S wave arrives first. False alarms happen too, and an alert that stops trains or sends factory workers running for nothing costs money and trust.

Early Warning Elsewhere

Other countries run or are building similar systems: Mexico's SASMEX, ShakeAlert on the US West Coast, and systems in China and Taiwan.
