Inflation

Cutting every tile into smaller tiles by a fixed rule gives the same Fibonacci and Penrose patterns as cut-and-project. In internal space the rule is a multiplication that shrinks the window into itself.

The first two articles built aperiodic patterns by projection: pick lattice points whose hidden coordinate falls in a window. There is an older and more hands-on way to build them. Start with one tile, cut it into smaller tiles by a fixed rule, blow the result back up to the original tile size, and repeat. Rules of this kind are called substitutions, and the blow-up step is inflation. Penrose found his tilings this way, and much of the early theory of aperiodic tilings was written in this language.

This part asks whether the two methods give the same patterns for the Fibonacci chain and the Penrose tiling. They do, and the window explains why.

Substitution in the plane

Penrose rhombs have a substitution too. It is easiest to state for half-rhombs, the triangles you get by cutting each rhomb along a diagonal, often called Robinson triangles. A thin half cuts into one thin and one thick half. A thick half cuts into two thick halves and one thin one. Each new edge is times the old one, and pairs of halves glue back into rhombs.

Start from a wheel of ten thin halves around a point and apply the rule a few times. The vertices of every tile you get are sums of the unit vectors once you rescale the edges back to length 1, because every edge points in one of the ten pentagon directions. Walking the edges from the center recovers an integer 5-vector for every vertex, and with it the internal image and the index from the previous article. So the substitution tiling can be tested against the pentagon windows directly.

Every vertex lands in its window, and the old vertices fill exactly the shrunk, inverted copy. The substitution is one more view of the same model set. One detail needs care. The wheel is fivefold symmetric about its center, so its windows are centered at the origin of internal space. The walk fixes the vector of the center only up to adding the same integer to every entry, which leaves the center's index undetermined, so the figure tries the five possible indices and keeps the one under which the other vertices land in their windows. From two steps on, exactly one choice works.

Substitution is not special to Penrose and Fibonacci. It is also at the center of the 2023 proof by David Smith, Joseph Samuel Myers, Craig Kaplan and Chaim Goodman-Strauss that a single polygon, the "hat", tiles the plane but never periodically: copies of the hat group into clusters that obey a substitution rule, and every hat tiling is forced to be hierarchical. Not every substitution tiling is a cut-and-project set with a nice window, though, and when it is, proving it takes work. The Fibonacci and Penrose cases are the clean ones.
