Five Grids and the Penrose Tiling

Five families of parallel lines, read the right way, become a Penrose tiling. Read another way, they are a slice through the five-dimensional integer lattice.

In 1974 Roger Penrose published a small set of tiles that can cover the plane but only without ever repeating. One version uses two rhombs with unit sides: a thick rhomb with a 72° corner and a thin rhomb with a 36° corner, plus marks on their edges that say which edges may meet. Following the marks, you can keep tiling forever, and the result is never periodic.

For a few years these tilings were understood through their edge rules and through subdivision, the subject of the next article. In 1981 the Dutch mathematician N. G. de Bruijn found a direct formula for them. His construction is the Fibonacci strip from the first article, moved up from a line to a plane, and it explains where the five-fold symmetry comes from.

De Bruijn proved that every Penrose rhomb tiling arises this way, from shifts that add to zero or as a limit of such choices, so the edge-matching rules and the pentagrid describe the same objects.

Where the window went

In the Fibonacci chain, the membership test lived on a second axis, the conjugate coordinate. Here it is hiding in the integers . A vertex is a point of the five-dimensional lattice , and the physical position is its shadow on a plane. The other three directions of five-dimensional space form the internal space. and the third is the index , which turns out to be 1, 2, 3 or 4 at every vertex. The window is the shadow of the unit five-cube on this three-dimensional internal space, just as the Fibonacci window was the shadow of the unit square. Sliced at each value of the index, that shadow is a pentagon. So the vertices with index 1 have inside one small pentagon, those with index 2 inside a larger one, those with index 3 inside the larger one turned upside down, and index 4 inside the small one upside down. The larger pentagons are times the size of the small ones. The shifts move all four pentagons together.

Drag the handle a short way and the red tiles in Figure 2 come in rows of hexagons. Each hexagon holds three tiles, and a flip swaps them for a different three that fill the same hexagon, moving one interior vertex. In internal space, that moved vertex is one point leaving a pentagon through an edge while its replacement enters one. These are the Penrose tiling's phason flips. Unlike in one dimension they do not come one at a time. A pentagon's straight edge can sweep over a whole line of internal images at the same instant, and those points sit along a ribbon in physical space, so a row of hexagons flips together.

The window knows the neighborhood

In the Fibonacci chain, the position of inside the window decided which tile came next. The same is true here, one dimension up. Around each vertex of a Penrose tiling, the tiles meeting there form one of a small number of configurations. Where the vertex's internal image sits inside its pentagon decides which one.

Figure 3 puts the whole construction in one place: a lattice, a plane at an irrational angle, and a window cut into polygonal pieces. Every local question about the tiling, such as what a vertex looks like, how often a patch occurs, or where the next copy of it is, becomes a question about where points of land inside those pieces. Because the pentagons are unchanged by a rotation of 144° about their common center, which matches a rotation of 72° in physical space, every patch and its rotations by 72° occur equally often. That is the sense in which a Penrose tiling has five-fold symmetry even though no single tiling needs to have a center of rotation.
