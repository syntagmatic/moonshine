Diffraction

A periodic crystal cannot have five-fold symmetry, but a cut-and-project set can and still diffracts into sharp spots. Shechtman saw such a pattern in an aluminum-manganese alloy in 1982.

Crystallographers do not look at atoms directly. They send a beam of X-rays or electrons through a sample and record where it scatters. A periodic arrangement of atoms scatters into a pattern of sharp, isolated spots, the Bragg peaks, and the positions of those spots give the lattice. A liquid or a glass scatters into diffuse rings. For seventy years "sharp spots" and "periodic" went together so reliably that the first was treated as proof of the second.

This part asks why the Fibonacci chain and the Penrose tiling fall on the "sharp spots" side even though they are not periodic, computing their diffraction straight from the point sets of the earlier parts. It ends with the experiment that raised the question.

With the random sequence the peaks decay as grows and only diffuse humps remain. With the Fibonacci chain they stay, at heights the window formula predicts. The chain is also unlike the periodic sequence in one visible way. Between the strong peaks there are weaker ones, and between those, weaker still. Peak positions are dense on the line, so in principle there is a peak in every interval, though almost all of them are too faint to see. A theorem of A. Hof (1995) shows the same holds for every model set with a reasonable window: the diffraction is made entirely of Bragg peaks, at the shadows of a dual lattice, with heights set by the window's Fourier transform.

The Penrose pattern has ten-fold symmetry, not five-fold. A diffraction pattern of real scatterers is always symmetric under , and combining that half-turn with a five-fold rotation gives a ten-fold one. Many of the spots also sit on rings whose radii differ by factors of , the same scaling that inflation produced in the previous article. This kind of calculation had been done before any quasicrystal was found. In 1982 Alan Mackay made an optical diffraction pattern of the vertices of a Penrose tiling and reported that it was made of discrete spots.

Shechtman's alloy

On 8 April 1982, Dan Shechtman, visiting the National Bureau of Standards (now NIST) near Washington from the Technion in Israel, was examining a rapidly cooled alloy of aluminum and manganese in an electron microscope. The diffraction pattern for exposure 1725 had sharp spots arranged with ten-fold symmetry. His notebook entry for it reads "10 Fold ???". Tilting the sample, he found further axes of five-fold, three-fold and two-fold symmetry, at the angles of an icosahedron.

The standard explanation for apparent five-fold spots was twinning: several ordinary crystals grown together at angles, whose patterns overlap. Shechtman was familiar with twinning and argued that this was not it, and he spent more than two years persuading colleagues. According to NIST's account, a first paper with Ilan Blech was rejected by the Journal of Applied Physics, and John Cahn later recalled that his own first reaction was that the patterns were twins. The paper that announced the discovery, by Shechtman, Blech, Denis Gratias and Cahn, appeared in Physical Review Letters on 12 November 1984, under the title "Metallic phase with long-range orientational order and no translational symmetry". Five weeks later, on 24 December 1984, Dov Levine and Paul Steinhardt published a theory of such structures in the same journal and gave them the name quasicrystals.

The icosahedral alloy is the three-dimensional version of the construction in this series: a slice through the six-dimensional integer lattice, projected to three physical dimensions, with a three-dimensional window. Its diffraction peaks are indexed by six integers rather than three. Resistance lasted for years, notably from Linus Pauling, who proposed twinning-based explanations until his death in 1994. In 1992 the International Union of Crystallography changed its definition of a crystal to "any solid having an essentially discrete diffraction diagram", which puts periodic crystals and quasicrystals in the same class. A natural quasicrystal, icosahedrite, was identified in 2009 in a mineral sample from the Khatyrka River in Chukotka, Russia. Shechtman received the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery of quasicrystals.

The mathematics and the metal are separate things. A model set is exact, and its diffraction is a theorem. A real quasicrystal has defects, thermal motion and phason disorder, and whether a given alloy is well described by a particular model set is an experimental question. What the model sets supplied was a proof that sharp, ten-fold diffraction and a well-defined structure are compatible, and a working method for describing the alloys.
