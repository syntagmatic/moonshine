Quasicrystals

Ordered patterns that never repeat, built by slicing a periodic lattice in a higher dimension and projecting the slice down.

A crystal is periodic, and for most of the twentieth century periodicity was taken to be what order in a solid meant. It comes with a restriction: a periodic pattern can only have two-, three-, four- or six-fold rotational symmetry. In 1982 an aluminum-manganese alloy produced a diffraction pattern with sharp spots and ten-fold symmetry, which no periodic crystal can give.

The mathematics that explains it is older than the experiment. Take a periodic lattice in a higher dimension, keep the lattice points that fall in a thin slab around a plane placed at an irrational angle, and project them onto the plane. The result is perfectly ordered and never periodic. This construction, cut-and-project, is the thread through these four articles. The first builds it in the smallest case, a line cut from the square grid. The second builds the Penrose tiling from five grids, or equivalently from the five-dimensional integer lattice. The third shows that cutting tiles into smaller tiles gives the same patterns, and why. The fourth computes their diffraction and tells the story of the discovery.

Every figure computes from the construction it illustrates: lattice points, windows, substitution rules and Fourier sums, evaluated in the page as you move the controls.
