The Fibonacci Chain

A square grid, a tilted line and a window give a row of points that is completely ordered and never repeats. It is the smallest case of cut-and-project, the construction behind the whole series.

A crystal repeats. Shift it by the right vector and every atom lands on another atom. For most of the twentieth century that property, periodicity, was treated as what "ordered solid" meant. The diffraction patterns of crystals were explained by it, and a theorem built on it says a periodic pattern in the plane or in space can only have rotational symmetry of order 1, 2, 3, 4 or 6. Five-fold symmetry is ruled out.

This series is about point sets that are ordered but not periodic, and about the one construction that produces most of the ones anyone understands well: take a periodic lattice in a higher dimension, cut a slice through it at an irrational angle, and project the slice down. It is called cut-and-project. The smallest example, built in this part, is a line cut from the square grid, and it already has every moving part that the later articles use in two and three dimensions.

A strip through the square grid

Start with the integer lattice in the plane, every point with integer coordinates. Draw a line through the origin with slope , and a strip of fixed width around it. The strip's cross-section, measured in the direction perpendicular to the line, is the window. Keep the lattice points that fall inside the strip and drop each one straight onto the line. What lands on the line is a row of points, and the gaps between neighbors are the tiles of a one-dimensional tiling.

Figure 1 raises two questions. The first is why, at the "shadow of the square" width, only two gap lengths ever appear. At that width the kept points form a staircase: from each kept point, the next one along the line is either one step right or one step up. The window is exactly as wide as the unit square seen edge-on, so it has room for one staircase and no more. A step right projects to one length and a step up to another. Widen the window and some points get a second neighbor inside the strip, and a third gap length appears.

The second is when the row repeats. At slope the line passes exactly through the lattice point . Sliding the whole picture along the line by that vector maps the lattice to itself and the strip to itself, so the row of kept points repeats with a period of eight tiles, five long and three short. Any rational slope gives a period this way. At an irrational slope no lattice vector lies along the line, and that is enough to rule out any period. A period would have to be the shadow of some lattice vector . Shifting by it moves every point's perpendicular coordinate by the same nonzero amount, and since those coordinates fill the window densely, some point near the window's edge would be pushed out of it.

Second, every interval has positive length, so every word that occurs once occurs again, with a bounded gap between occurrences. So although the chain has no period, every finite piece of it recurs. The count of distinct words of length is the smallest count any non-periodic sequence can have. Sequences with exactly this count are called Sturmian, and the Fibonacci word is the standard example.

Sliding the window

So far the window was pinned at , but it can move. Slide it by an offset to and you get another chain. As the window moves, lattice points cross its edges. A point leaving at the top edge is always replaced by a point entering at the bottom edge whose lattice coordinates differ by . In the chain, that pair sits apart, and the local effect is that an LS swaps into SL, or the reverse. These swaps are called phason flips.

Two things stay fixed while the window slides. The tile lengths never change, and neither do the frequencies, because they depend only on interval lengths inside the window. What changes is where the swaps are. For a small offset only a few points sit close enough to an edge to cross it, so the flips are sparse, and every finite stretch of one chain still appears somewhere in the other. The chains for different offsets are different sequences that cannot be told apart by looking at any bounded piece. That is why a quasicrystal's order is described by the whole family of offsets rather than by one chain.

Physicists use the word phason for a related thing in real quasicrystals: a degree of freedom in which atoms hop between nearby sites in the pattern, the physical counterpart of these flips. In the mathematics the offset is a parameter. The next article raises the dimension. Five grids of lines in the plane, or equivalently a slice through the five-dimensional integer lattice, produce the Penrose tiling, and the window becomes four pentagons.
