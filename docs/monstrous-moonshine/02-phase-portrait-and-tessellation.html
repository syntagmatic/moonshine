Phase Portrait and Hyperbolic Tessellation

Part 2 of 10. The j-function lives in four real dimensions and cannot be plotted directly. We use the next best thing: a visual trick that turns the upper half-plane into an Escher-like tessellation whose infinite self-similarity is the SL₂(ℤ) invariance from explainer 1.

In the previous explainer we described the j-function analytically: it is a meromorphic function on the upper half-plane ℍ, invariant under the fractional linear action of SL₂(ℤ), with a Fourier expansion in the variable q = e2πiτ. We wrote down the first dozen coefficients and drew a bar chart. Now we are going to see it.

The obstacle is dimensionality. A complex-valued function of a complex variable is a map ℂ → ℂ, so graphing it in the sense of "draw the graph in ℝ4" requires four real dimensions. We don't have four axes on a flat screen. The standard escape is to give up on showing the full graph and instead encode part of the output as colour, using the two screen dimensions for the two real dimensions of the input. This technique is called domain colouring, and it turns the SL₂(ℤ)-invariance of j from a formal property into something you can see.

The fundamental domain

Before we colour anything, we need to understand the space j lives on. The quotient ℍ/SL₂(ℤ), the space of distinct elliptic-curve shapes, is a 2-dimensional orbifold homeomorphic to a sphere with one puncture. To parameterise it, we pick a single representative tile inside ℍ that SL₂(ℤ) can copy to fill the entire half-plane. The standard choice is

a region bounded by the vertical lines Re τ = ±½ and the unit semicircle |τ| = 1, stretching upward to Im τ → ∞. It has two special interior points:

  • τ = i, the midpoint of the arc, where j(i) = 1728. This is the square lattice (ℤ + ℤi) and the elliptic curve with an extra ℤ/4 symmetry.
  • τ = ρ = e2πi/3, the left corner of the arc where it meets the wall, with j(ρ) = 0. This is the hexagonal lattice (ℤ + ℤρ) and the elliptic curve with an extra ℤ/6 symmetry.

At the cusp τ → i∞ (the top of ℱ), j has a simple pole: j(τ) ∼ q−1 → ∞ as Im τ → ∞. This pole is the reason the quotient ℍ/SL₂(ℤ) is a sphere, not a more complicated surface: j maps ℱ bijectively onto the Riemann sphere ℂ ∪ {∞}, with 1728 at i, 0 at ρ, and ∞ at the cusp. This is what it means to say j is a Hauptmodul, a uniformiser for the genus-zero quotient ℍ/Γ, where Γ = SL₂(ℤ). In explainer 5 this word will become the central noun of the Conway–Norton conjecture.

Figure 1. The standard fundamental domain ℱ of SL₂(ℤ). The purple region is ℱ: the closed half-strip −½ ≤ Re τ ≤ ½, |τ| ≥ 1, stretching upward. The two special points are ρ (the cube root of unity, j(ρ) = 0) and i (the square lattice point, j(i) = 1728). All of ℍ is tiled by copies of ℱ under SL₂(ℤ).

The tessellation

Every point τ ∈ ℍ has a unique image in ℱ under some element of SL₂(ℤ) (up to identifications on the boundary). Equivalently, the translates γ(ℱ), for γ ranging over all of SL₂(ℤ), tile the entire upper half-plane with no overlaps and no gaps. The figure below draws these tiles for all γ whose matrix entries are at most 6 in absolute value.

What you see is an Escher-like pattern: each tile is a curvilinear triangle bounded by circular arcs and vertical line segments, shrinking hyperbolically as you approach the real axis. Near the real axis the tiles pile up in a self-similar fractal pattern; near the cusp (high Im τ) they degenerate into tall thin vertical strips. The entire picture is an artefact of the group SL₂(ℤ), and the invariance of j means: j takes the same values at corresponding points of every tile.

Figure 2. The SL₂(ℤ) tessellation of ℍ. Each curvilinear triangle is the image of ℱ under one element of SL₂(ℤ). Near the real axis, tiles shrink exponentially — this is the hyperbolic metric at work. The tessellation encodes the full symmetry group of the j-function: any modular-invariant function takes identical values at corresponding points of every tile. Use the depth selector to add or remove smaller tiles near the real axis.

Hyperbolic geometry. The tessellation looks like an Escher woodcut because ℍ with the metric ds² = (dx² + dy²)/y² is a model of the hyperbolic plane. In hyperbolic measure, every tile has the same area (π/3 steradians) even though in Euclidean measure the tiles near the real axis are tiny. SL₂(ℤ) acts by hyperbolic isometries, so translating ℱ around preserves hyperbolic shape and size; it is only the Euclidean projection to the screen that makes some tiles look smaller. This is exactly the same geometric effect that Escher exploited in Circle Limit.

Domain colouring

Now we can attach colour. In domain colouring, we paint each point τ of the input domain with a colour that encodes the output j(τ). The standard recipe:

  • Hue encodes the argument (phase angle) of j(τ). Red at argument 0, cycling through orange, yellow, green, cyan, blue, magenta, and back to red at 2π.
  • Brightness encodes the modulus. Large |j| goes toward white (the pole at the cusp); small |j| goes toward black (the zero at ρ). A logarithmic brightness scale keeps both visible.

To compute j(τ) at each pixel, we first reduce τ to the fundamental domain (where the q-expansion converges quickly), evaluate the first ten terms of the q-series, and paint the pixel. For τ near the real axis with small Im τ, the reduction step lifts Im τ into the fundamental domain where |q| is small and the series converges in a handful of terms.

Figure 3. Domain colouring of j(τ) on the upper half-plane. Hue = arg j(τ), brightness = log |j(τ)|. The bright white region at the top is the pole (j → ∞ at the cusp). The dark spots at the corners of the fundamental domain are the zeros (j(ρ) = 0). The pattern is SL₂(ℤ)-periodic: every tile is a colour-identical copy of every other, because j takes the same values on corresponding points. The grid toggle overlays the tessellation for orientation.

What the picture shows

Three features of the domain-coloured image are worth noting explicitly.

Self-similarity. Every tile is a shrunken, Möbius-transformed copy of the fundamental domain, and the colouring within it is an identically transformed copy of the colouring in ℱ. This self-similarity is not approximate: it is exact, forced by the modular invariance j(γτ) = j(τ).

The zeros at ρ. At the two lower corners of ℱ, j = 0. In the domain-coloured picture, zeros show up as dark spots where all hues converge (because the argument of 0 is undefined). Every copy of ρ in the tessellation (and there are infinitely many, clustering toward every rational point on the real axis) produces another dark spot.

The pole at the cusp. At the top of ℱ, j has a simple pole. In the picture, the cusp direction washes out to bright white as |j| → ∞. Every tile has its own "cusp direction" (a vertex that abuts the real line), and near that vertex the colouring goes white.

In explainer 5 we will see that 196884 = 1 + 196883 is not a coincidence: the j-function, painted across this tessellation, has somehow smuggled information about the Monster into its Fourier coefficients. The tessellation and the domain colouring will still be here when we need them.

What we take with us

  • The fundamental domain ℱ of SL₂(ℤ) is the region |τ| ≥ 1, |Re τ| ≤ ½, together with its boundary identifications. It contains two special points: ρ with j(ρ) = 0 and i with j(i) = 1728.
  • The SL₂(ℤ) tessellation tiles ℍ with copies of ℱ that shrink hyperbolically toward the real axis. It is the visible form of modular invariance.
  • Domain colouring encodes arg j as hue and |j| as brightness. The resulting picture is self-similar across tiles, with dark zeros at images of ρ and bright poles at images of the cusp.
  • j is a Hauptmodul: it maps ℱ bijectively to the Riemann sphere. This bijectivity (the fact that the quotient ℍ/SL₂(ℤ) has genus zero) is the geometric property that Conway and Norton will demand of every McKay–Thompson series.
