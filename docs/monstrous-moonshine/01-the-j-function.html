The Modular j-Function

Part 1 of 10. A single complex-valued function on the upper half-plane, invariant under a discrete group of symmetries, with Fourier coefficients that a hundred years later turned out to know about a 53-digit finite group nobody had noticed yet.

The story of Monstrous Moonshine has two protagonists. One is a finite group so large that its order is 808,017,424,794,512,875,886,459,904,961,710,757,005,754,368,000,000,000. The other is the complex function we meet in this explainer: the modular j-function, a meromorphic map from the upper half-plane to the complex line whose Fourier coefficients on inspection turn out to decompose, grade by grade, into sums of dimensions of representations of that finite group. We will not meet the group for three more explainers. First we need to be fluent in its partner.

We are going to do three things with j. We will describe the setting it lives in (the upper half-plane with a discrete group acting on it), we will write down its Fourier expansion (the one whose coefficients do all the mischief), and we will look at how fast those coefficients grow. By the end you should be comfortable with the phrase "the first non-trivial coefficient of j is 196884", which is the hinge the whole series swings on.

The upper half-plane and the modular group

The natural home of j is the upper half-plane

a two-dimensional real manifold sitting inside ℂ. Points of ℍ are complex numbers with strictly positive imaginary part. A letter you will see over and over is τ (tau), which always denotes a point of ℍ; we will freely write τ = x + iy with y > 0.

Why the upper half-plane? One short answer: it parameterises shapes of lattices in ℂ. Every lattice L ⊂ ℂ of rank 2 is the image of the standard lattice ℤ + ℤi under some real-linear change of basis, and two lattices give rise to the same complex torus ℂ/L exactly when their bases differ by a rotation and a scaling. Up to that rescaling, a lattice is determined by the ratio τ of its two generators, and we can always choose this ratio to live in ℍ. So points of ℍ are exactly shapes of elliptic curves over ℂ, and j will turn out to be a function that records which shape you're looking at.

But the representation "lattice as a τ in ℍ" is not unique. The basis of the lattice can be changed, and every change of integer basis with determinant 1 produces a different τ for the same physical lattice. The group of such basis changes is the modular group

which acts on ℍ by the fractional linear transformation

Two generators of SL₂(ℤ) are enough to reach everything: the translation T: τ ↦ τ + 1, which nudges the real part, and the inversion S: τ ↦ −1/τ, which flips the upper half-plane through the unit semicircle. Every element of SL₂(ℤ) is a finite word in S and T.

The point of the modular group. Two values τ, τ' ∈ ℍ related by an element of SL₂(ℤ) represent the same lattice up to rotation and rescaling, and therefore the same elliptic curve up to isomorphism. A function on ℍ that respects this equivalence (that takes the same value at τ and τ') is really a function on the space of elliptic curves.

So the functions that matter on ℍ are the ones invariant under SL₂(ℤ), and the j-function is the simplest non-trivial one.

Defining the j-function

Among all meromorphic functions on ℍ, the ones we want are those that satisfy

for every γ = ((a, b), (c, d)) ∈ SL₂(ℤ). Such a function is called a modular function of weight 0 and level 1. The full definition includes a mild growth condition at infinity (meromorphy at the cusp), which rules out pathologies. The space of all such functions is a field, and a remarkable theorem of classical complex analysis says that field is generated by a single function. Every modular function of weight 0 and level 1 is a rational function of one specific protagonist, and that protagonist is j.

We normalise j by requiring it to have a simple pole at the cusp τ → i∞ with residue 1 and a very specific constant term. Concretely, there are elegant analytic constructions (via the Eisenstein series E₄ and E₆, or as the discriminant quotient E₄³/Δ), all of which land on the same function up to a shift. Why q, not τ? The variable q = e2πiτ is how j's SL₂(ℤ)-invariance cashes in. Under the generator T: τ ↦ τ + 1, q is unchanged, because e2πi(τ+1) = e2πiτ. So any function that is T-invariant admits a Fourier expansion in powers of q, and j is exactly such a function. The remaining generator S: τ ↦ −1/τ is harder to read in q-coordinates, and is the reason we will later bring in the fundamental domain and the phase portrait.

Every integer in the middle column is about to become a sum of dimensions of representations of a finite group we haven't met yet.

Two things to notice. First, the coefficients are growing fast: by n = 10 we are already up to roughly 2 × 1016, and they will continue to grow exponentially. Second, they are integers, and surprisingly interesting integers.

Why the coefficients are integers

A priori there is no reason the Fourier coefficients of an arbitrary modular function should be integers. They could be arbitrary complex numbers; and in fact, the higher-weight analogues (weight-k modular forms for k ≥ 2) usually have rational but non-integer coefficients when normalised naturally. The j-function is special.

The reason j's coefficients are integers is that j can be constructed as a ratio of two modular forms whose coefficients are all integers. Specifically, Δ(τ) (the discriminant modular form, the one whose coefficients are Ramanujan's τ-function) and E₄(τ) (the weight-4 Eisenstein series) both have integer q-expansions, and the normalisation j = 1728 E₄³/Δ has integer coefficients as a consequence of Δ being a normalised cusp form. We don't need the construction to state the fact; we just need to know that it holds and that it forces the coefficients to carry meaningful arithmetic.

Integer coefficients matter because they make j into an object of arithmetic geometry, not merely complex analysis. When we evaluate j at quadratic-irrational values of τ, the resulting j(τ) turns out to be an algebraic integer (the famous "singular moduli" theorem, the starting point of complex multiplication). The coefficients of the q-series are the first hint that the series knows about algebra.

Ramanujan's constant

Before we move on, one cultural digression. Put τ = (1 + i√163)/2 into j. This is a quadratic irrational for the imaginary quadratic field ℚ(√−163), whose ring of integers has class number 1 (the largest class number among such fields; no larger one exists). The theory of complex multiplication forces j(τ) to be not just an algebraic integer but a rational integer in this case, and evaluating gives

Now j(τ) = q−1 + 744 + (things of size at most roughly q), and q = e2πiτ. For our τ, 2πiτ = πi − π√163, so q = −e−π√163, which is extremely small. Therefore q−1 = −eπ√163 is a very large negative real number, and

is forced to be an integer. Rearranging, eπ√163 must itself be very close to an integer: specifically, to 6403203 + 744, differing from it by about 10−12. This is Ramanujan's constant, and the apparent near-integrality is sometimes advertised as a piece of mathematical "moonshine" of its own. It isn't magic: the Hardy–Ramanujan asymptotic we just wrote down makes the tail of the q-series exponentially small when q is exponentially small, so the constant term 744 dominates. But it is a nice reminder that the integer 744 in the q-series is not decoration. It is fixed by demanding that j(i∞) be a specific value, and that specific value turns out to pay off at every reasonable evaluation point.

The nine Heegner numbers 1, 2, 3, 7, 11, 19, 43, 67, 163 are the full list of d for which ℚ(√−d) has class number 1, and they are the full list at which this near-integrality trick works at all.

Takeaways

  • The upper half-plane ℍ = { τ ∈ ℂ : Im τ > 0 } is the moduli space of shapes of 2-dimensional lattices in ℂ, and equivalently of complex elliptic curves up to isomorphism.
  • SL₂(ℤ) acts on ℍ by the fractional linear transformations γτ = (aτ + b)/(cτ + d), generated by the two elements S: τ ↦ −1/τ and T: τ ↦ τ + 1.
  • The j-function is the unique generator, normalised by j(τ) = q−1 + 744 + 196884 q + …, of the field of all meromorphic SL₂(ℤ)-invariant functions on ℍ. Its Fourier coefficients are positive integers.
  • Those coefficients grow as c(n) ∼ e4π√n/(√2·n3/4), which is the Hardy–Ramanujan asymptotic applied to modular forms. The smooth envelope has no finite-group information in it; the Monster hides in the deviations.
  • Ramanujan's constant eπ√163 ≈ 6403203 + 744 is an almost-integer because j evaluated at the Heegner point (1 + i√163)/2 must be an algebraic integer of class number 1, i.e. a rational integer. The constant 744 in the q-series is carrying arithmetic content.
