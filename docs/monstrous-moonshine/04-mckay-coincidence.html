McKay's Coincidence

Part 4 of 10. In 1978 John McKay noticed that 196884 = 1 + 196883. One Fourier coefficient, two representation dimensions. The observation that launched a conjecture, a Fields Medal, and a new branch of mathematics.

A number theorist reading the ATLAS and a group theorist reading the Fourier analysis both encounter the integer 196884 in one context and 196883 in the other. In 1978, John McKay noticed the near-miss and asked: is there a reason that these two numbers, from two subjects that should have nothing to do with each other, are one apart?

The first three decompositions

McKay's observation was that c(1) = 196884 = 1 + 196883 = dim ρ1 + dim ρ2. The addend 1 is the trivial representation. When McKay communicated this to John Thompson, Thompson looked at the next coefficient:

c(1) = 196 884 = 1 + 196 883 = dim ρ₁ + dim ρ₂
c(2) = 21 493 760 = 1 + 196 883 + 21 296 876 = dim ρ₁ + dim ρ₂ + dim ρ₃
c(3) = 864 299 970 = 2·1 + 2·196 883 + 21 296 876 + 842 609 326
c(3) = 864 299 970 = 2·dim ρ₁ + 2·dim ρ₂ + dim ρ₃ + dim ρ₄

The pattern is striking: each j-coefficient decomposes as a sum of Monster irrep dimensions with small non-negative integer multiplicities. Not just any integer linear combination: one where every multiplicity is 0, 1, or 2, and the higher irreps enter one at a time. This is far too structured to be numerical coincidence.

Why this is not a coincidence

The first decomposition, c(1) = 1 + 196883, could conceivably be an accident. The second, c(2) = 1 + 196883 + 21296876, is a mild extension. But by c(3), we are fitting a 9-digit integer as a specific linear combination of four Monster irrep dimensions with small multiplicities, and by c(4) and c(5) the pattern extends to five and seven irreps respectively. The probability of this happening by accident is vanishingly small: the j-coefficients grow like e4π√n, and fitting each one as a non-negative integer combination of a fixed set of basis dimensions (the 194 irrep dimensions of the Monster) with small multiplicities is a Diophantine constraint that has no reason to be satisfiable, unless there is an actual representation hiding behind it.

This is the insight Thompson drew from the data: the decompositions are exactly what you would see if there existed an infinite-dimensional graded vector space

where each Vn♮ is a finite-dimensional representation of 𝕄, meaning 𝕄 acts on it by matrices, and dim Vn♮ = c(n). The decomposition of c(n) into irrep dimensions is then simply the decomposition of Vn♮ into its irreducible summands.

The Conway–Norton conjecture

In 1979, John Conway and Simon Norton published a paper titled "Monstrous Moonshine" that upgraded Thompson's observation from a guess to a precise, falsifiable conjecture. The conjecture says:

The Moonshine Conjecture (Conway–Norton 1979). There exists an infinite-dimensional ℤ-graded 𝕄-module V♮ = ⊕n=−1∞ Vn♮ with dim V−1♮ = 1, dim V0♮ = 0, dim V1♮ = 196884, such that for every element g ∈ 𝕄 the McKay–Thompson series

is a Hauptmodul, a generator of the function field of a genus-zero discrete subgroup Γg ⊂ SL₂(ℝ).

This is a lot of words. Let us unpack them.

Graded module. V♮ is an infinite-dimensional vector space, but it is organised as a direct sum of finite-dimensional pieces (the Vn♮), one per integer n ≥ −1. The Monster acts on each piece by finite matrices, so the graded trace Tr(g | Vn♮) is a well-defined complex number for every g and every n. The "graded dimension" ∑ dim Vn♮ qn is the j-function (minus 744), so the dimensions are accounted for.

McKay–Thompson series. For each g ∈ 𝕄, collecting the traces ∑ Tr(g | Vn♮) qn produces a formal power series in q. For the identity element g = 1, the trace is just the dimension, and the series is j − 744 itself. For other elements, the series is a different modular function, determined by the conjugacy class of g.

Hauptmodul. This is the most surprising part. A Hauptmodul is a function that generates the field of all modular functions for a given discrete subgroup of SL₂(ℝ), which can only happen if the quotient of ℍ by that subgroup is a Riemann sphere (genus zero). We saw in explainer 2 that j is a Hauptmodul for SL₂(ℤ). The conjecture says that for every element of the Monster (all 194 conjugacy classes, each giving its own McKay–Thompson series) the resulting series is also a Hauptmodul, each for its own genus-zero group Γg.

Conway and Norton verified the conjecture numerically for all 194 conjugacy classes, using the character table computed by Fischer, Livingstone, and Thorne. For each class, they identified which genus-zero group Γg the series matched. The fact that all 194 series land on genus-zero groups, rather than some landing on higher-genus curves, is the deep arithmetic content of the conjecture. Conway called it "moonshine" because it felt like madness.

Why "genus zero" matters

The genus-zero condition is the mathematical knife edge that separates a meaningful conjecture from a tautology. Without it, the conjecture would just say "there exists a graded Monster module whose graded dimensions equal the j-coefficients," which is true by construction (you can always cook up an artificial module). The genus-zero condition says something much sharper: it says the graded traces, one per Monster element, are not arbitrary power series but are all Hauptmoduls. This constrains the multiplicities at every grade, locks them in, and makes the conjecture falsifiable.

To put it differently: the identity element gives j − 744, which is a Hauptmodul for SL₂(ℤ). For the 193 remaining conjugacy classes, the graded traces must each be a Hauptmodul for a different genus-zero group. This is 193 additional constraints on the module V♮, one per conjugacy class. The fact that a single module can satisfy all of them simultaneously is the miracle.

What we take with us

  • McKay's observation (1978): c(1) = 196884 = 1 + 196883 = dim ρ₁ + dim ρ₂.
  • Thompson's extension: c(2) = dim ρ₁ + dim ρ₂ + dim ρ₃, c(3) = 2·dim ρ₁ + 2·dim ρ₂ + dim ρ₃ + dim ρ₄. Every coefficient decomposes into Monster irrep dimensions with small integer multiplicities.
  • Conway–Norton conjecture (1979): there exists a graded Monster module V♮ = ⊕ Vn♮ with dim Vn♮ = c(n), such that for every g ∈ 𝕄 the graded trace Tg(τ) = ∑ Tr(g | Vn♮) qn is a Hauptmodul for a genus-zero subgroup of SL₂(ℝ).
  • Hauptmodul = genus zero: the conjecture constrains not just the dimensions (which follow from the j-function) but the traces, locking in every multiplicity through all 194 conjugacy classes.
  • This concludes Act I. The reader now knows what the coincidence is, what objects it connects, and what the precise conjecture claims. Acts II and III will explain why it is true.
