Monstrous Moonshine

How the largest sporadic simple group talks to a classical modular function, and what the conversation tells us. Ten interactive explainers, from John McKay's 1978 coincidence to Richard Borcherds' 1992 proof that it was no coincidence at all.

In 1978 John McKay was reading a paper on the modular function j(τ). The first surprising coefficient of its Fourier series was the number 196884. A few days earlier John Thompson had told him about a group called the Monster 𝕄, predicted by Bernd Fischer and Robert Griess and not yet constructed, whose smallest non-trivial representation had dimension 196883. Two off-by-one coincidences, from two subjects that should have had nothing to do with each other. McKay wrote to Thompson.

Thompson looked at the next few j-coefficients and found that they also decomposed, with non-negative integer multiplicities, into sums of dimensions of Monster representations. Conway and Norton collected the data into a table, saw patterns no numerical accident could produce, and published "Monstrous Moonshine" in 1979 as a precise conjecture: there must exist an infinite-dimensional graded module for the Monster whose graded characters are a specific family of classical modular functions, one per conjugacy class. Conway named it moonshine because it felt too strange to be real, and because Thompson had called the first coincidence "moonshine".

Richard Borcherds proved the conjecture in 1992. He built a specific infinite-dimensional Lie algebra (now called the Monster Lie algebra), imported the no-ghost theorem from bosonic string theory as a technical lemma, and extracted from it a denominator identity rigid enough to lock in every graded character. The proof connected the Monster to vertex operator algebras, the Leech lattice, and 26-dimensional string theory, and won him the 1998 Fields Medal.

This series tells that story as a three-act arc. Act I introduces the j-function, the Monster, and the coincidence itself. Act II builds the structural bridge: E8, the Leech lattice, and the graded-module framework that Conway and Norton needed to state the conjecture at all. Act III runs Borcherds' proof: vertex operator algebras, the moonshine module V♮, generalised Kac–Moody algebras, and the denominator identity that finishes the job.

The companion E8 lattice and Lie algebras series are soft prerequisites for Act III. Everything else we build from scratch.
