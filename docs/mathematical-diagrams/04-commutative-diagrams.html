Commutative Diagrams: The Language of Category Theory

A commutative diagram is a directed graph of objects and morphisms where every pair of paths with the same endpoints composes to the same morphism. It is the fundamental notation of modern mathematics.

Why diagrams?

Category theory does not study objects in isolation. It studies the maps between them: functions between sets, homomorphisms between groups, continuous maps between spaces. When you have several objects and several maps, writing equations like g' f = f' g quickly becomes opaque. A commutative diagram makes the same statement visible: draw the objects as nodes, the maps as arrows, and assert that every way of walking from one node to another yields the same composite map.

This is not a convenience. It is the working language of algebra, topology, and theoretical computer science. A mathematician who says "the diagram commutes" is making a precise, checkable assertion about composition of morphisms.

Categories, objects, morphisms

A category consists of a collection of objects and, for every pair of objects, a set of morphisms (arrows) between them. Every object has an identity morphism, and morphisms compose associatively. The category of sets has sets as objects and functions as morphisms. The category of groups has groups as objects and group homomorphisms as morphisms. The category of topological spaces has spaces and continuous maps.

A commutative diagram is a finite directed graph embedded in this framework. Nodes are objects. Directed edges are morphisms. The diagram commutes if for every pair of directed paths from the same source to the same target, the compositions of the morphisms along each path are equal.

Commutativity is about paths, not shapes. A square commutes when the two-step path across the top and down the right equals the two-step path down the left and across the bottom. A triangle commutes when the direct edge equals the two-step path through the third vertex. The geometric shape is secondary; the path algebra is the content.

The fundamental shapes

The commutative square

The most common diagram in mathematics. Four objects, four morphisms forming a square. Commutativity says g' f = f' g. Pullbacks, pushouts, and naturality squares are all instances. When you see a square in a textbook, the author is asserting that two different compositions agree.

The factorisation triangle

Three objects, three morphisms. The direct morphism f : A → B factors through an intermediate object C as f = g h. Every factorisation in mathematics, from prime decompositions to canonical forms, is a triangle diagram.

Exact sequences

A chain of morphisms where the image of each map equals the kernel of the next. A short exact sequence 0 → A → B → C → 0 says that A injects into B, the quotient is C, and nothing is lost. The arrow styles encode the type of map: a hooked arrow for a monomorphism (injective), a double-headed arrow for an epimorphism (surjective).

The snake lemma

Two parallel exact sequences connected by vertical morphisms, with a "connecting homomorphism" snaking from the kernel of one map to the cokernel of another. It is the engine of homological algebra, the tool that lets you extract long exact sequences from short ones.

Universal properties

The product of two objects A and B is defined by a diagram: for every object X with morphisms to A and B, there exists a unique morphism from X to A×B making the diagram commute. The dashed arrow signals existence-and-uniqueness. Every universal construction in category theory, from products to limits to adjunctions, is stated as a commutative diagram with a unique-fill-in arrow.

Functors are diagram-to-diagram maps. A functor F from category C to category D sends objects to objects and morphisms to morphisms, preserving composition and identities. If a diagram commutes in C, its image under F commutes in D. This is why category theory can transfer theorems between completely different areas of mathematics: a commutative diagram in algebra maps to a commutative diagram in topology.

Arrow styles and their meaning

The style of an arrow is not decoration. It is a statement about the morphism:

  • Plain arrow (→) a morphism with no additional claim.
  • Hooked arrow (mono) a monomorphism: injective, no information is lost.
  • Double-headed arrow (epi) an epimorphism: surjective, every target element is hit.
  • Dashed arrow a morphism whose existence (and often uniqueness) is being asserted, not assumed.

Diagram chasing is a proof technique. You assume an element exists at one node, follow the arrows, use injectivity or surjectivity at each step, and arrive at the conclusion. The snake lemma was once considered so mechanical that, in the film It's My Turn (1980), Jill Clayburgh proved it on a blackboard in the opening scene.

What the diagram sees

Every commutative diagram encodes a specific algebraic statement. Here is what each shape reveals:

  • Square — two different compositions of morphisms are equal: the fundamental naturality condition.
  • Triangle — a morphism factors through an intermediate object: decomposition and uniqueness of factorisation.
  • Exact sequence — the image of each map is the kernel of the next: nothing is gained or lost between consecutive maps.
  • Product diagram — a universal construction defined by a unique fill-in: the product, the limit, the adjunction.
  • Snake lemma — two linked exact sequences produce a connecting homomorphism: the engine that drives long exact sequences in homological algebra.
  • Path agreement — commutativity is not about shapes but about the path algebra: every pair of directed routes between two nodes composes to the same morphism.
