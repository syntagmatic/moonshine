Penrose Graphical Notation: Tensors as Diagrams

Part 14 of 14. Roger Penrose's 1971 insight that tensor algebra becomes clearer when drawn as diagrams — wires for indices, nodes for tensors, and contraction as connection.

In 1971, Roger Penrose proposed a graphical notation for tensor calculus in "Applications of Negative Dimensional Tensors." Each tensor becomes a box, its indices become lines, and contraction (the summing operation) is just connecting lines. The structure of an algebraic expression becomes visible in the shape of its diagram.

What is a tensor?

A tensor is a multilinear map. Where a matrix takes one vector and returns another, a general tensor can accept and produce several vectors at once. The key measure of complexity is the tensor's type: a tensor of type has p contravariant (upper) indices and q covariant (lower) indices. Its rank is .

A scalar is type (0,0) — no indices at all. A vector is (1,0) — one upper index. A covector (linear functional) is (0,1) — one lower index. A matrix, with one input slot and one output slot, is (1,1). The Riemann curvature tensor of general relativity is (1,3): one contravariant index and three covariant ones, for a total rank of four.

Upper and lower indices transform oppositely under change of basis, so a contraction always pairs an upper index with a lower one to keep the result basis-independent. Penrose notation makes the geometric content visible: upward and downward wires are different, and only opposite types may connect.

The graphical convention

Penrose's notation replaces index-heavy expressions with diagrams. Each tensor becomes a node — a labelled shape. Its indices become lines (or wires): upper indices extend upward from the top of the node, lower indices extend downward from the bottom.

A matrix is drawn as a node with one line going up (the output index i) and one going down (the input index j). A vector has only an upward line. A covector has only a downward line. The number and direction of lines tell you the tensor's type at a glance, without consulting any formula.

The Riemann tensor, with its four indices, becomes a node with one line going up and three going down. Traditional index notation requires careful tracking of which indices are up and which are down. In the Penrose diagram, that bookkeeping is handled by the picture itself.

Contraction: connecting wires

The power of the notation lies in contraction. When an upper index of one tensor matches a lower index of another, we sum over that index. In Penrose's diagrams, this summation is simply connecting the corresponding wires.

Matrix–vector multiplication connects the lower wire of A to the upper wire of v; the remaining free upper wire is the resulting vector's index. The topology of the wiring encodes the summation.

Matrix multiplication connects the lower wire of A to the upper wire of B. The result has one free upper wire (from A) and one free lower wire (from B): another matrix. Longer chains are longer chains of connected nodes, and associativity is visible: the topology of A–B–C is the same regardless of parenthesisation.

Trace: the self-loop

The trace of a matrix connects an upper wire back to a lower wire of the same tensor, forming a loop. Since there are no free indices left, the result is a scalar:

Every closed loop represents a summation ; a closed wire is a contraction with nothing left free.

Contracting any pair of indices on a higher-rank tensor reduces the rank by two. The Ricci tensor is obtained from the Riemann tensor by a single contraction: one self-loop on the (1,3) node leaves a (0,2) tensor. Contract again with the inverse metric to get the Ricci scalar.

The trace rule generalises: any contraction pairing an upper and lower index of the same tensor is a partial trace. For a type (2,2) tensor, two possible traces give generally different results, and the diagram makes the choice explicit through the topology of the self-loop.

Special tensors

Some tensors have distinguished graphical forms. The metric tensor is drawn as a cup or a symmetric bar with two lower wires — it lowers an index by converting a contravariant slot into a covariant one. Its inverse does the reverse, raising indices. In Penrose notation the metric is often drawn as a small cup connecting two downward wires, so that "lowering an index" literally means bending a wire from pointing up to pointing down.

The Kronecker delta is the simplest possible diagram: a straight wire from top to bottom, with no node at all. It is the identity map on a single index — doing nothing, just passing the index through. The Levi-Civita symbol is drawn as an antisymmetrizer node: a horizontal bar with n wires whose ordering matters. Swapping any two wires changes the sign, making the antisymmetry of the Levi-Civita visible in the diagram's structure.

Connection to string diagrams

Penrose notation is a special case of string diagrams from monoidal category theory (Part 7). Tensor products correspond to placing wires side by side; contraction corresponds to composition. The category of finite-dimensional vector spaces is the archetypal example. Generalising to any monoidal category gives the diagrammatic grammar shared by quantum circuits, logical proofs, and signal-flow graphs.

Any equation between Penrose diagrams is a theorem in the category of finite-dimensional vector spaces, and conversely. The compact closure of (every finite-dimensional space has a dual) is what lets wires bend between upper and lower. The cup and cap of a compact closed category are the metric and its inverse. Diagrammatic reasoning is a proof technique, not a heuristic.

Applications

Penrose notation was developed for general relativity, where expressions like the Riemann curvature tensor involve four indices and the traditional notation becomes unwieldy. Penrose diagrams tame the complexity: the Bianchi identity becomes a simple symmetry of a diagram, and the Einstein field equations can be stated as a relationship between two diagrams with two free lower wires each.

In quantum information, tensor networks describe entangled many-qubit states. A matrix product state (MPS) is a chain of tensors contracted in sequence: a Penrose diagram read left to right. The DMRG algorithm, the workhorse of condensed-matter physics, is tensor contraction along a one-dimensional chain. Entanglement entropy corresponds to cutting wires and counting the remaining degrees of freedom.

In machine learning, tensor decompositions (Tucker, CP, tensor train) are all expressible as Penrose diagrams. The tensor train decomposition is structurally identical to MPS, linking theoretical physics to compression of high-dimensional data. Tensor network methods have grown into a bridge between these communities, with Penrose's 1971 notation as the shared visual language.

Topology, not geometry. Node positions carry no algebraic information; only connections matter. Dragging a tensor node changes the picture but not the expression. Two diagrams with the same connectivity are the same tensor expression.

Einstein summation is wiring. The Einstein convention "sum over repeated indices" is a notational shortcut. Penrose notation replaces repeated indices with the physical act of connecting wires: the summation is the wire. Every connected wire is a sum; every free wire is an uncontracted index.

From Penrose to quantum circuits. A quantum circuit diagram is a Penrose tensor diagram. Qubits are wires, gates are tensors, a CNOT gate is a (2,2) tensor, measurement collapses a wire into a scalar. The framework of quantum computation reads as tensor contraction made visible; Penrose's 1971 notation anticipated the graphical calculus of quantum information by three decades.

What the diagram sees

  • Nodes — tensors, each labelled with a name and drawn as a rounded box. The number of ports on top and bottom gives the type.
  • Upper wires — contravariant indices, extending upward from the top edge. Free ones end in open circles and represent output slots.
  • Lower wires — covariant indices, extending downward from the bottom edge. Free ones end in open circles and represent input slots.
  • Connected wires — contracted indices, drawn as curves between tensors. Each connection is a summation over one index, performed automatically.
  • Self-loops — trace operations, where a tensor's upper wire curves back to its own lower wire, collapsing two indices into a sum.
  • No wires at all — a node with no free wires is a scalar. The tensor has been fully contracted.
  • Index labels — letters along the wires (i, j, k ...) name the indices. Shared labels on a connected wire show which index is being summed.
  • Topology over geometry — dragging a node changes the picture but not the algebra. The meaning lives in the connections, not the positions.

Penrose notation is the fourteenth and final entry in this series. From Hasse diagrams to Penrose diagrams, each translates algebraic structure into visual form.

Traditional tensor notation forces you to name every index, even those summed away. The diagram shows only what matters: nodes (tensors), connections (contractions), and dangling wires (free indices). Dummy index names, summation signs, and term ordering are absorbed into the topology.
