The Core Concept

A plane tropical curve can be introduced without much machinery. Start with finitely many affine-linear functions on . Take their pointwise maximum. The result is a convex piecewise-linear surface. The tropical curve is the projection of the creases of that surface down to the -plane.

In the figure, those affine-linear functions come from the six lattice monomials of a quadratic polynomial:

At a point , one monomial may have the largest value. That point lies in a two-dimensional region labelled by the winner. If two or more monomials have the same largest value, and no other monomial is larger, the point lies on the tropical curve.

The curve is a tie set. A classical zero asks when a sum cancels to zero. A tropical zero asks when the leading term is not unique. The diagram therefore draws competition between monomials, not sign-changing crossing through an axis.

Why Amoebas Become Lines

The tropical picture is not only a formal game with max and plus. One route to it starts with ordinary complex curves inside the algebraic torus. Send a point to the logarithms of the absolute values of its coordinates. The image is called an amoeba. When that logarithmic picture is rescaled, many families of amoebas approach piecewise-linear spines.

You will also see a min-plus convention in the literature, where . It gives lower envelopes instead of upper envelopes. For the planar pictures in this article, the two conventions differ mostly by signs: replacing every value by its negative turns maxima into minima. What matters for the diagram is the same combinatorial event, a minimum or maximum being attained at least twice.

Balancing and the Newton Polygon

A tropical curve is not just any drawing made of straight segments. The edges have rational slopes, integer weights, and a local conservation law. Near a vertex, take each outgoing edge direction as a primitive integer vector and multiply it by the edge weight. The sum of those weighted vectors is zero. This is the balancing condition.

Try the balance check: Select a local vertex type. The left side draws the outgoing weighted rays. The right side places those weighted primitive vectors head to tail, so a balanced vertex closes up.

Balancing inspector. Smooth trivalent vertices close the vector polygon with three unit directions. Higher weights and higher valence still use the same conservation law.

For a plane tropical hypersurface coming from a polynomial, the balancing law is reflected in the Newton polygon. The exponents form lattice points. Their convex hull is the Newton polygon. Coefficients lift those points to heights . The upper convex hull, for the max-plus convention, projects back to a regular subdivision of the Newton polygon.

The tropical curve and that subdivision are dual to one another. A region of the plane corresponds to an exponent point. An edge of the curve corresponds to an edge in the Newton subdivision, with perpendicular direction. A vertex of the curve corresponds to a polygonal cell in the subdivision. In the small Newton panel in the figure, the drawn edges record which exponent regions meet in the current viewing window.

The usual smooth plane tropical curve is the case where the subdivision is a unimodular triangulation. The word "smooth" here is tropical language: it says the local Newton cells are as small as possible in the lattice, not that the polyline has no corners.

Degree, Ends, and Genus

The Newton polygon also controls coarse topological data. For ordinary plane curves of degree , the Newton polygon is the triangle with vertices . Its boundary lattice points correspond to unbounded ends of the tropical curve. Its interior lattice points count the genus of a smooth curve with that Newton polygon.

This is one place where the diagram language changes viewpoint. A plane tropical curve is drawn as a balanced graph in , but an abstract tropical curve is a metric graph with ends. The embedding remembers slopes and Newton data. The abstract graph remembers lengths, cycles, and genus.

For higher genus, not every metric graph appears as a smooth plane tropical curve with a chosen Newton polygon. The polygon and its regular unimodular subdivisions constrain which graph shapes and edge lengths can occur. This is why tropical plane curves sit between two worlds: they are combinatorial graphs, but graphs with extra lattice geometry attached.

Concept Summary

  • Tropical addition is max or min. This article uses max-plus: . Min-plus is the sign-reversed lower-envelope convention.
  • A tropical polynomial is piecewise-linear. Each monomial becomes an affine-linear function, and the polynomial takes the maximum of those functions.
  • The curve is the corner locus. It is the set of points where the winning monomial is not unique.
  • Amoebas explain the limit intuition. Logarithmic images of classical curves can shrink toward tropical spines under rescaling.
  • Regions remember monomials. Each complementary region is labelled by the exponent vector of the monomial that wins there.
  • Balancing is local conservation. Weighted primitive outgoing directions at a vertex sum to zero.
  • The Newton polygon is the dual picture. Coefficients define a regular subdivision of the exponent polygon, and the tropical curve is dual to that subdivision.
  • Interior lattice points count genus in the smooth plane case. The embedded curve's Newton polygon constrains the abstract metric graph beneath the drawing.
