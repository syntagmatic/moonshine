Cayley Graphs: Groups as Geometry

Part 3 of 7. A group's entire multiplication table, drawn as a directed graph. One node per element, one coloured edge per generator. The geometry you see is the algebra.

A multiplication table is a grid: look up the row for g, the column for h, and read off gh. It works, but it hides the geometry. Cayley's graph encodes exactly the same information as a directed graph, and structure that was invisible in the table jumps out: cyclic subgroups are loops, cosets are clusters, the centre is the set of nodes where differently-coloured paths commute.

Groups and generators

A group is a set G together with an associative binary operation, an identity element e, and inverses. A set S of generators is a subset of G such that every element of the group can be written as a product of elements of S and their inverses. A group may have many generating sets, and the choice of generators changes the graph.

Given a group G and a generating set S, the Cayley graph Cay(G, S) is a directed graph whose vertices are the elements of G. For each element g and each generator s in S, there is a directed edge from g to gs, coloured by s. If the group has two generators, the graph has two families of edges, one blue and one red. Three generators, three families.

Vertex-transitivity. Every node in a Cayley graph looks the same: from any vertex, you see the same pattern of outgoing edges. This is because left multiplication by any group element is a graph automorphism. The graph has no "special" vertex, until you pick one as the identity.

What the graph encodes

The Cayley graph is an exact encoding of the group's multiplication table. Given any node g, following a blue edge takes you to ga and following a red edge takes you to gb, where a and b are the generators. To compute any product, start at the identity and follow the corresponding sequence of coloured edges. The element you land on is the answer.

This turns multiplication into navigation. A word in the generators is a sequence like aba-1b; it corresponds to a directed path in the graph starting at the identity. Two words that represent the same group element correspond to paths with the same endpoint, regardless of how differently the paths meander through the graph.

The word metric. The distance between two elements g and h in the Cayley graph is the length of the shortest word w such that g = hw. This metric depends on the choice of generators, but the large-scale geometry does not: different generating sets for the same group produce quasi-isometric Cayley graphs. This is the starting observation of geometric group theory.

Cosets as clusters

If H is a subgroup of G, the left cosets gH partition the group into equal-sized blocks. In the Cayley graph, each coset forms a cluster of nodes connected internally by the generators belonging to H. The edges that cross between cosets are exactly the generators not in H.

When H is a normal subgroup, the quotient pattern is especially clean: the cosets themselves form a group G/H, and the between-coset edges are uniform. You can visually "collapse" each coset to a single node and recover the Cayley graph of G/H.

Seeing normality. In S3, the subgroup {e, (123), (132)} is normal: its two cosets (the rotations and the reflections) tile the graph symmetrically. The subgroup {e, (12)} is not normal: its three cosets do not tile uniformly. Try toggling generators in the explorer to see the difference.

A gallery of small groups

Each finite group has a characteristic shape in its Cayley graph. Here are the ones available in the explorer above:

Cyclic groups Zn. A single generator produces a cycle of length n. The Cayley graph is a directed polygon. Simple, but it already shows something: adding the inverse generator doubles every edge, turning the directed cycle into an undirected one.

Klein four-group V4. Two generators of order 2, commuting with each other. The Cayley graph is a square (or rectangle), the simplest non-cyclic graph. Every pair of differently-coloured paths commutes: blue-then-red lands on the same node as red-then-blue.

Symmetric group S3. Generated by the transpositions (12) and (23). Six nodes, twelve edges. The graph has a triangular prismatic shape: three rotations form an inner triangle, three reflections form an outer triangle, and the red edges connect them.

Dihedral group D4. Symmetries of the square, generated by a rotation r and a reflection s. Eight nodes. The rotation generator produces a 4-cycle among the rotations and another 4-cycle among the reflections; the reflection generator connects the two rings.

Quaternion group Q8. Generated by i and j, with the relation ij = k and every element squaring to −1 (except ±1 themselves). The Cayley graph is striking: it looks similar to D4 but the edge pattern differs, reflecting the fact that Q8 and D4 are non-isomorphic groups of the same order.

Same order, different geometry. D4 and Q8 both have order 8, both are non-abelian, and both are generated by two elements. But their Cayley graphs are not isomorphic: the quaternion group has no element of order 2 other than −1, while D4 has five. The graph sees this difference immediately.

Vertex-transitivity and the absence of a centre

A Cayley graph is always vertex-transitive: for any two vertices g and h, left multiplication by hg−1 is a graph automorphism sending g to h. There is no distinguished vertex. When you click a node in the explorer and "re-root" the graph, you are not changing the graph; you are changing which element you call e.

This is why the Cayley graph depends on the choice of generators but not on the choice of identity. The graph is the same object regardless of which node you stand on. The structure is global, not local.

Connections

Cayley graphs appear wherever groups act. The Weyl group of a Lie algebra (see the Lie algebras series) has a Cayley graph that is the 1-skeleton of a Coxeter complex. Cayley graphs of free groups are infinite trees, the starting point for Bass-Serre theory. The word problem, whether two words represent the same group element, is equivalent to deciding whether two paths in the Cayley graph share an endpoint.

What the diagram sees

  • Multiplication. Following a coloured edge from g lands on gs. Every product is a path.
  • Subgroups. A subgroup generated by a subset of the generators appears as a connected subgraph through every node.
  • Cosets. Left cosets of a subgroup partition the graph into isomorphic clusters.
  • Commutativity. Two generators commute if and only if every blue-red square closes. In an abelian group, every such square closes.
  • The word metric. Shortest-path distance in the graph gives a left-invariant metric on the group.
  • Group isomorphism. Two groups are isomorphic if and only if (for appropriate generators) their Cayley graphs are isomorphic as coloured directed graphs.
