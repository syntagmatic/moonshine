The Associahedron

An associahedron is the polytope whose vertices are the triangulations of a convex -gon and whose edges are flips. A flip takes the two triangles on either side of a diagonal, which together form a quadrilateral, and swaps in the quadrilateral's other diagonal. Conventions vary; here the -gon gives dimension , so the pentagon gives a pentagon and the hexagon a solid with 14 vertices. The triangulations that keep one diagonal form a facet: a pentagon when the diagonal cuts off a triangle and leaves a pentagon to flip in, a square when it splits the hexagon into two quadrilaterals that flip independently.

Parenthesizations and Triangulations

The name comes from associativity. Label the sides of the polygon a, b, c, ... in order and let the remaining side, drawn thick in Figure 1, stand for the whole product. The triangle on that side is the last multiplication: it splits the factors into the sides to its left and the sides to its right, and each block is a smaller polygon triangulated the same way. So a triangulation of an -gon is a full parenthesization of factors, drawn as a binary tree, and a flip is one use of .

Cluster Algebra Connection

A cluster algebra is built from clusters, sets of compatible variables, and mutation, which trades one variable of a cluster for a new one through an exchange relation; its exchange graph has clusters as vertices and mutations as edges. In finite type there is a polygon model: cluster variables are the diagonals of a convex -gon, clusters are triangulations, and mutation is a flip. So the exchange graph of type is the pentagon and that of is the associahedron of Figure 1.

The exchange relation is Ptolemy's. Give every side of the polygon the value 1, the coefficient-free case. Flipping diagonal inside a quadrilateral with sides in order produces with . Quiver Mutation

The polygon model exists only in type A. What carries over to other cluster algebras is a quiver, a directed graph that records how the variables of one cluster interact. A triangulation gives one directly. Put a vertex on each diagonal. Inside every triangle with two diagonal sides, draw an arrow between them, turning the same way around every triangle.

Flipping diagonal changes that quiver by a rule stated without the polygon. Fomin and Zelevinsky's quiver mutation at adds an arrow for every path , reverses every arrow at , and then deletes opposite pairs of arrows two at a time. Because the rule needs only the quiver, it applies with no polygon behind it. Fomin and Zelevinsky proved in 2003 that a cluster algebra built from a quiver has finitely many clusters exactly when its mutation class contains an orientation of a Dynkin diagram of type A, D or E. Type gives the associahedron of the -gon, and types D and E give the generalized associahedra of Chapoton, Fomin and Zelevinsky. The same list turns up in Gabriel's 1972 theorem, which says a connected quiver has finitely many indecomposable representations exactly when its underlying graph is an ADE Dynkin diagram. The Exceptional Atlas covers where Dynkin diagrams come from, and its build-your-own diagram sandbox tests which graphs are finite type.
