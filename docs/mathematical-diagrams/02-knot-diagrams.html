Knot Diagrams

Part 2 of 10. A knot can be written as a drawing with crossings, a braid word, or a twisted tangle named by a fraction. One state sum, the Kauffman bracket, computes an invariant from all three.

A knot is a closed loop in space. Project it onto the plane and, wherever the shadow crosses itself, record which strand passes over. That record is a knot diagram, and unlike the diagrams of Part 1 it holds all of its information in the drawing.

Pick a preset and click a crossing to swap over and under, or press Draw your own and click out a loop. Try to unknot the trefoil by flipping crossings; the bracket says when you have.

Figure 1. Knot workspace. Dashed amber circles are clickable crossings: the solid strand passes over, the pale stub passes under, and the small +/− is the crossing's sign. Figures 2 and 3 read whatever diagram is here.

A drawn curve gets alternating crossings: walking along it you go over, under, over. Tait conjectured that a reduced alternating diagram (no removable kinks) uses the fewest crossings its knot allows, and Kauffman, Murasugi and Thistlethwaite proved it in 1987 with the polynomial computed here. So almost anything you draw is knotted. To undo that, make the diagram descending: from some starting point the strand goes over at its first visit to every crossing. Read height as time and the strand spirals steadily down, so it is the unknot, and X(D) drops to 1.

Summing over states

Each crossing can be smoothed away in two ways, called A and B. Choosing one at every crossing gives a state s, a set of disjoint loops, and n crossings give 2n states. With σ(s) the number of A-smoothings minus B-smoothings, |s| the number of loops and δ = −A2 − A−2,

The all-A cell, first in the grid, carries the highest power of A. In a reduced alternating diagram every state one smoothing away from it has one loop fewer, so nothing else reaches that power and it cannot cancel. The same holds for all-B at the bottom, and the gap between the two ends is what the 1987 proof uses to count crossings.

When two diagrams are the same knot

One knot has infinitely many diagrams. Reidemeister proved in 1927 that two diagrams show the same knot exactly when a sequence of three local moves turns one into the other: add or remove a kink (R1), pull one strand fully across another (R2), or slide a strand across a crossing (R3). An invariant is anything unchanged by all three.

The bracket survives R2 and R3 but not R1. Neither does the writhe w, the sum of crossing signs. Orient the knot; a crossing is +1 when turning the over strand counterclockwise, by less than a half turn, lines it up with the under strand, and −1 otherwise. A kink changes w by ±1 and multiplies the bracket by −A±3, so the two failures cancel in X(D) = (−A³)−w⟨D⟩.

Substituting A = t−1/4 in X gives the Jones polynomial. The trefoil preset has writhe −3 and X = A4 + A12 − A16, which is V = t−1 + t−3 − t−4: this is the left-handed trefoil. Its mirror image has the exponents negated, so the bracket tells the two apart.

Braids

Pin n strings to a bar at the top and a bar at the bottom and let them cross on the way down. Write σi when the string in position i crosses over its right neighbour and σi−1 when it crosses under. Reading the crossings from top to bottom gives a word, and stacking braids concatenates words. Artin showed in 1925 that these words form a group, the braid group Bn, and that two words give the same braid exactly when the relations

connect them. The first says crossings far apart commute; the second is R3 for braids. Join each bottom end to the top end in the same position and you get the braid's closure. Alexander proved in 1923 that every knot and link is a closure, so a word is enough to name any knot.

The two trefoil presets are different words with the same X, the left-handed trefoil of Figure 1. (With this picture σi is a −1 crossing, so σ1³ has writhe −3; some books draw σi the other way and get the right-handed trefoil from the same word.) Markov's theorem (1936) says two braids have the same closure exactly when the group relations, conjugation and stabilisation connect them. Try the two Markov buttons on any word: the bracket changes under stabilisation, X never does.

Forget over and under and a braid becomes a permutation, with one closure component per cycle. The Borromean preset permutes nothing, so it has three loops. Delete any one of them and the readout reports a two-loop unlink: no two of the rings are linked, and yet the three are.

Rational tangles

Draw a circle that meets a knot diagram in exactly four points. The part inside is a tangle: two arcs whose ends sit at the corners NW, NE, SW, SE. Start from the tangle [0], two horizontal arcs. A horizontal twist turns the right-hand ends NE and SE about each other, adding a crossing on the right; a vertical twist turns the bottom ends SW and SE, adding a crossing below. Tangles built this way are rational.

Conway gave each rational tangle a fraction F. The tangle [0] has F = 0, a horizontal twist adds ±1 to F, and a vertical twist adds ±1 to 1/F, so a twist sequence gives a continued fraction. His theorem (1970) is that two rational tangles can be deformed into each other, ends fixed, exactly when their fractions are equal. Join NW to NE and SW to SE outside the circle and the tangle closes into a two-bridge knot or link N(T).

Figure 5. Rational tangle builder. Dashed amber arcs are the closure N(T). The fraction comes from twist arithmetic. The bracket comes from the crossings: every state of a tangle leaves some loops and one of the crossing-free tangles [0] or [∞], so ⟨T⟩ = f⟨0⟩ + g⟨∞⟩, and each twist updates f and g. Evaluated at A = eiπ/4, the bracket's absolute value is the determinant |V(−1)|.

Whatever you build, the determinant equals |p|, the fraction's numerator, and the two numbers come from computations that never see each other. The preset 3/7 is a trefoil, with the [3] preset's bracket times (−A³)2, the factor two extra kinks would contribute. Schubert showed in 1956 why: N(p/q) and N(p′/q′) are the same knot or link, allowing mirror images, exactly when p = p′ and q′ ≡ ±q±1 (mod p), and 7 ≡ 1 (mod 3).
