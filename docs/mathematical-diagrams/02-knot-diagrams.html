Knot Diagrams

Part 2 of 10. A knot can be written as a drawing with crossings, a braid word, or a twisted tangle named by a fraction. One state sum, the Kauffman bracket, computes an invariant from all three.

In Part 1 the equations came from the author rather than the picture. A knot diagram holds all its information in the drawing, and this part computes an invariant from it and asks when two drawings show the same knot.

A knot is a closed loop in space. To work with one on paper, project it onto the plane and, at each place where the shadow crosses itself, record which strand passes over. That record is a knot diagram. The figure below computes on diagrams you choose or draw.

Pick a preset and click any crossing to swap which strand goes over. The crossing count, writhe and Kauffman bracket update as you do. Or press Draw your own, click out any loop you like, and close it: every self-intersection becomes a crossing, and the same invariants are computed on the spot. Try to unknot the trefoil by flipping its crossings; the bracket shows when you have.

Figure 1. Knot workspace. Dashed amber circles are clickable crossings: the solid strand passes over, the pale stub passes under, and the small +/− is the crossing's sign. The panel shows the Kauffman bracket ⟨D⟩ and its writhe-corrected form X(D), recomputed from all 2n states after every change.

In draw mode each click drops a point and a smooth curve threads through them. Click near your first point, double-click, or press Close curve to close the loop. Every place the curve crosses itself becomes a crossing, and over and under are assigned alternately: walking along the strand you go over, under, over, under. This default makes most drawings knotted. Tait conjectured in the nineteenth century that an alternating diagram with no removable kinks already uses the fewest crossings any diagram of its knot can have, and Kauffman, Murasugi and Thistlethwaite proved it in 1987 using the polynomial computed here. A diagram like that with at least one crossing cannot be the unknot, which needs none. So almost anything you draw is a nontrivial knot, and the bracket confirms it.

Now flip crossings so that the strand always goes over on its first visit to each crossing, starting from any point. A diagram like that is called descending, and a descending diagram of one loop is always the unknot: read the strand's height as time, and it spirals steadily downward, so it can be pulled apart layer by layer. Once your diagram is descending, X(D) on the verdict line drops to 1, the unknot's value.

When two diagrams are the same knot

One knot has infinitely many diagrams. Reidemeister proved in 1927 that two diagrams show the same knot exactly when a sequence of three local moves turns one into the other: add or remove a kink (R1), pull one strand fully across another (R2), or slide a strand across a crossing (R3).

The three Reidemeister moves. Each changes the diagram only inside the small disc shown; the rest of the knot is untouched.

An invariant is anything unchanged by all three moves. Give each crossing a sign of +1 or −1 by the direction the over strand turns, and sum the signs to get the writhe:

The writhe survives R2 and R3 but not R1, which changes it by ±1. The Kauffman bracket is subtler. Each crossing can be smoothed away in two ways, called A and B. Choosing one at every crossing gives a state s, a set of disjoint loops; a diagram with n crossings has 2n states. With σ(s) the number of A-smoothings minus B-smoothings and |s| the number of loops,

The bracket also survives R2 and R3, and it also fails R1, picking up a factor of −A±3 per kink. The two failures cancel: X(D) = (−A³)−w⟨D⟩ survives all three moves, and substituting A = t−1/4 gives the Jones polynomial. That is the sum Figure 1 evaluates, state by state.

The state sum uses only the crossings, so it applies to any picture with crossings, including pictures built by rules very different from free drawing. The next two sections build knots from a word and from a fraction, and hand the result to the same sum.

Braids

Pin n strings to a bar at the top and a bar at the bottom and let them cross on the way down. Write σi when the string in position i crosses over its right neighbour and σi−1 when it crosses under. Reading the crossings from top to bottom gives a word, and stacking braids concatenates words. Artin showed in 1925 that these words form a group, the braid group Bn, and that two words give the same braid exactly when the relations

connect them. The first says crossings far apart commute. The second is the braid version of R3.

Join each bottom end of a braid to the top end in the same position, around the side, and you get a knot or link, the braid's closure. Alexander proved in 1923 that every knot and link arises this way. So a word is enough to specify any knot, and the figure below closes your braid and runs the bracket on it.

Strands are coloured by the component of the closure they belong to. The readout computes the closure's bracket over all 2n states and, when X matches one of a few small knots and links, names it.

Load the two trefoil presets. The words σ1³ on two strands and (σ1σ2)² on three are different words but close to the same knot, with the same X as the trefoil you flipped in Figure 1. Markov's theorem (1936) says exactly when this happens: two braids have the same closure if and only if one can be reached from the other by the group relations, by conjugation (moving a crossing from the bottom of the word to the top, which the closure cannot see), and by stabilisation (adding a strand with one crossing σn±1, an R1 kink in the closure). The word σ1σ2 on three strands is the empty braid on one strand, stabilised twice, and the readout calls it the unknot.

Forget over and under and a braid becomes a permutation of the positions. The closure has one component for each cycle of that permutation, which is how the builder colours the strands. The Borromean preset permutes nothing, so its closure has three loops, and X shows they are linked even though any two of them alone are not.

Rational tangles

Draw a circle that meets a knot diagram in exactly four points. The part inside is a tangle: two arcs whose ends sit at the corners NW, NE, SW, SE. Start from the tangle [0], two horizontal arcs with no crossings. A horizontal twist turns the two right-hand ends NE and SE about each other, adding a crossing on the right. A vertical twist turns the two bottom ends SW and SE, adding a crossing below. Tangles built by any sequence of these twists are called rational.

Conway gave each rational tangle a fraction F. The tangle [0] has F = 0. A horizontal twist adds ±1 to F, and a vertical twist adds ±1 to 1/F, so the fraction of a twist sequence is a continued fraction. His theorem (1970) is that two rational tangles can be deformed into each other, ends held fixed, if and only if their fractions are equal. The fractions range over ℚ ∪ {∞}.

Join NW to NE and SW to SE outside the circle and the tangle closes into a knot or link N(T), a two-bridge knot. The figure below computes two things about N(T) independently. The fraction p/q comes from the twist arithmetic alone. The bracket ⟨N(T)⟩ comes from the state sum: every state of a tangle leaves some loops and one of the two crossing-free tangles [0] or [∞], so ⟨T⟩ = f⟨0⟩ + g⟨∞⟩ for two Laurent polynomials f and g, and each twist updates f and g by a small rule. Then it evaluates the bracket at A = eiπ/4, where its absolute value is the knot's determinant |V(−1)|.

Try any sequence you like: the determinant always comes out equal to |p|, the numerator of the fraction. This is a theorem, and the figure checks it with two independent computations. The fraction also says which knot you have. Schubert showed in 1956 that N(p/q) and N(p′/q′) are the same knot or link, allowing mirror images, exactly when p = p′ and q′ ≡ ±q±1 (mod p). The preset 3/7 is a trefoil for that reason, since 7 ≡ 1 (mod 3), and its bracket is the [3] preset's bracket times (−A³)², exactly the factor two extra kinks would contribute. Both are the mirror image of the trefoil in Figures 1 and 2, as the readout reports; three H −1 twists give that one. The figure-eight is 5/2, and [2] gives 2/1, the Hopf link.
