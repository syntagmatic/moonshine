Ternary Diagrams: Three Numbers on a Triangle

Why a triangle

The constraint turns three numbers into two degrees of freedom. Whatever and happen to be, is forced. So even though the data looks three-dimensional, it lives on a flat 2D slice of : the triangular face of the simplex with vertices at the three pure compositions .

An equilateral triangle is the natural canvas because of Viviani's theorem: from any interior point, the three perpendicular distances to the sides sum to a constant equal to the triangle's altitude. Set that altitude to 1 and the three perpendicular distances are the three coordinates. Reading the diagram is reading three altitudes from a single point.

There is no preferred axis. Each vertex is the "pure" point for one component (the other two are zero), each side is the locus where the opposite component is zero, and the centre is the uniform mixture . Symmetry across the three components becomes a symmetry of the picture.

The point of the picture. A ternary diagram does not show three independent numbers. It shows one point in a two-dimensional space whose geometry encodes a constraint: the three components are coupled by their sum. Once you see this, ternary plots stop looking like a stylistic choice and start looking like the only honest way to draw a three-component composition.

A short history of three on a triangle

The same picture has been re-discovered in several disciplines, often without cross-citation. The throughline is that whenever a quantity decomposes into three coupled parts, someone eventually draws this triangle.

  • 1718 · Newton · In Opticks, draws a colour circle and discusses three-primary mixing. The conceptual seed for treating colour as a three-component composition.
  • 1857 · Maxwell · Constructs the colour triangle: an equilateral triangle with red, green, and blue at the vertices, the first explicit ternary plot of perceptual data. The triangle is the ancestor of the modern CIE chromaticity diagram.
  • 1873 · J. Willard Gibbs · Graphical Methods in the Thermodynamics of Fluids introduces the ternary phase diagram for three-component mixtures. The triangle becomes the standard canvas for chemical equilibrium.
  • 1894 · H. W. B. Roozeboom · Standardises the ternary phase diagram in physical chemistry, with conventions for tie-lines, miscibility gaps, and eutectic points that are still in use.
  • 1926 · Bruno de Finetti · Adopts the triangle for population genetics. The de Finetti diagram plots genotype frequencies with the Hardy-Weinberg locus appearing as a parabola.
  • 1938 · USDA · Adopts the soil texture triangle. Sand, silt, and clay percentages classify a soil into one of twelve named regions. Still the standard agricultural classifier.
  • 1982 · John Aitchison · Reframes compositional data as living natively on the simplex, not in . Introduces the log-ratio transformation and the Aitchison geometry on the triangle, the modern foundation of compositional data analysis.

The closure problem

Compositional data has a peculiar property that ordinary multivariate methods get wrong. Consider two soil samples:

  • Sample A: 30 g sand, 50 g silt, 20 g clay (100 g total)
  • Sample B: 60 g sand, 100 g silt, 40 g clay (200 g total)

As raw mass vectors in these are different points: one is twice the length of the other. As compositions they are identical: . Compositional data lives on the triangle, not in , and the operation that takes raw counts to compositions is called closure:

Closure is what makes the data live on the simplex in the first place, and it is what trips up naive analyses. Two compositions with the same ratios sit on the same point of the triangle even if their raw scales differ by orders of magnitude. Conversely, increasing one component must, by closure, decrease the others. Components cannot move independently; correlations are baked into the geometry.

John Aitchison's response, in 1982, was to stop pretending compositional data was Euclidean. He defined a different geometry on the simplex (perturbation as the group operation, log-ratio coordinates as the chart) under which the simplex is a flat Hilbert space. In Aitchison geometry the standard tools (means, distances, principal components) work as expected, just on transformed coordinates. The triangle stays the picture; the metric on it is not the one your ruler measures.

The probability simplex

The same triangle is the natural object for a different question: what is the space of categorical probability distributions over three outcomes? A point with and all is a probability distribution over three categories, and its space is the simplex . The vertices are the deterministic distributions; the centre is the uniform distribution; the sides are distributions that put zero mass on one outcome.

This is not a metaphor for compositional data: it is the same object, reached from a different direction. Information geometry studies the simplex as a Riemannian manifold equipped with the Fisher metric, under which two distributions are "close" when they assign similar likelihoods to the same observations. The Fisher metric on turns out to be (a constant multiple of) the round metric on the positive octant of the unit sphere in : square-root the coordinates and the simplex maps to a spherical octant.

That mapping is why the iso-entropy contours in the Entropy preset above are not concentric circles around the centre but pulled towards the vertices: entropy is a function of the distribution, and the Fisher metric measures distances in distribution space, not in raw probability space.

The three Amari α-connections add a one-parameter family of straight-lines-on-the-simplex: at a "straight line" is a mixture of two distributions, at it is a Fisher geodesic, at it is an exponential interpolation. The same triangle carries three different notions of "between," and they agree only at the metric midpoint. The triangle is geometric scaffolding; the geometry on it is a choice.

Variants

The triangle is the dominant convention but not the only option. Three departures are common enough to recognise.

Right-triangle ternary
Drop the equilateral constraint. Two coordinates become Cartesian; the third is read off the hypotenuse. Loses Viviani's symmetry; gains rectangular grids and easier overlay with other Cartesian plots.
Quaternary tetrahedron
Four components on the 3-simplex. Now a 3D solid; usually rendered as a wireframe with a draggable rotation. Beyond four components, projection or sliced views replace the simplex itself.
Ternary contour
A scalar function defined on the simplex (yield, viscosity, entropy) drawn as iso-curves over the triangle. Common in alloy design and chemical engineering.
Tie-lines and gaps
In phase diagrams, a miscibility gap is a region the system does not occupy as a single phase. Tie-lines connect the two coexisting phases on the boundary. Reading the gap is reading which mixtures separate.

Concept summary

  • Three numbers that sum to a constant have only two degrees of freedom. The triangle is the natural 2D canvas for that constraint.
  • Viviani's theorem makes the equilateral triangle work: from any interior point, the three perpendicular distances to the sides sum to the altitude. Set the altitude to 1 and those distances are the three coordinates.
  • Vertex = pure component, side = component is zero, centre = uniform mixture. Symmetry of the components is symmetry of the picture.
  • Closure is the operation that takes raw counts to compositions. Compositional data lives on the simplex, not in , and naive Euclidean methods misbehave on it.
  • Aitchison geometry equips the simplex with a Hilbert-space structure via the log-ratio transformation, so the standard statistical machinery works on the triangle's "true" metric rather than the apparent Euclidean one.
  • The probability simplex is the same triangle, viewed as the space of categorical distributions. Fisher geometry pulls it onto a spherical octant; the α-connections give three notions of "straight line" that agree only at the metric midpoint.

Where the triangle shows up next

The probability simplex is the entry point to the Information Geometry series, where the same triangle becomes the central object: distributions are points, the Fisher metric is the local ruler, and α-connections are the choices of "straight line." The Hardy-Weinberg parabola on the de Finetti triangle re-appears in a different language as a one-parameter exponential family on the simplex.

For the connection to graphical notation more broadly, the Penrose tensor diagram in the previous explainer treats a tensor of type (p,q) as a node with p upper and q lower wires. The probability simplex of categorical distributions over n outcomes is exactly the space of normalised covariant 1-tensors with non-negative components: a categorical distribution is a "probability covector," and the triangle is the geometry of that constraint.
