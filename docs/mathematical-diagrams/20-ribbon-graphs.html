Core Concept

A ribbon graph, also called a fatgraph in much of the topology literature, is a finite graph equipped with a cyclic order on the half-edges incident to each vertex. The word "fat" is meant quite literally. Replace each vertex by a small oriented disk, replace each edge by a narrow rectangular ribbon, and attach the ribbons to the disks in the prescribed circular order. The result is an oriented surface with boundary that deformation retracts onto the original graph.

The underlying graph alone does not know this surface. A theta graph, for example, can thicken to a pair of pants or to a once-punctured torus, depending on how the three half-edges are ordered at the two vertices. Ribbon graphs package that extra topological information without requiring a drawn embedding on a page.

The extra datum is local. A ribbon graph does not store coordinates for vertices or curves for edges. It stores only the circular order of incident half-edges at each vertex. From those local rotations, the boundary cycles and genus are recovered combinatorially.

Cyclic Orders

It is useful to label the half-edges, or darts. In the figure, the three edges are pairs (a1,b1), (a2,b2), and (a3,b3). The edge-pairing is a fixed-point-free involution, usually written . The cyclic orders at the vertices form another permutation, often written .

For a vertex of valence three there are only two cyclic orders once the first dart is fixed: (a1 a2 a3) and (a1 a3 a2). For higher valence, the same idea applies. The order is cyclic, so (a1 a2 a3), (a2 a3 a1), and (a3 a1 a2) describe the same local rotation.

When a graph is already embedded in an oriented surface, the surface orientation gives these cyclic orders automatically. A ribbon graph reverses the direction of explanation: it starts with the cyclic orders and builds the surface from them.

Relation to Surfaces and Dessins

Ribbon graphs are a combinatorial language for graphs embedded in oriented surfaces. If the complement of the embedded graph is a union of disks, the embedding is often called a map. The cyclic order at each vertex records the same information as the local rotation system of that map.

Dessins d'enfants sit very close to this picture. A dessin is a bipartite graph embedded in an oriented surface with disk faces, usually studied together with a Belyi map. From the combinatorial side, its embedding can be encoded by permutations and cyclic orders, much like a ribbon graph. Conversely, an ordinary ribbon graph can be made bipartite by inserting a new vertex in the middle of every edge, which is why clean dessins and ribbon graphs often appear in the same conversations.

Metric ribbon graphs add positive lengths to the edges. Through Strebel differentials, such metric fatgraphs give a combinatorial model for moduli spaces of punctured Riemann surfaces. This statement has hypotheses and choices: the boundary components correspond to marked points or punctures, and the edge lengths record metric data. The important point for diagrams is that the same local cyclic-order data becomes a coordinate chart for families of surfaces.

Concept Summary

  • Half-edges: A ribbon graph is most cleanly described using darts, one at each end of every edge.
  • Cyclic order: At each vertex, the incident darts are arranged in a circular order. This is the fatgraph structure.
  • Boundary cycles: Alternating "cross the edge" and "turn around the vertex" produces the boundary components of the thickening.
  • Genus: For a connected ribbon graph, v - e + b = 2 - 2g. Changing cyclic order can change b, and therefore g.
  • Surfaces: A ribbon graph is a graph with enough local data to thicken canonically into an oriented surface with boundary.
  • Dessins and moduli: Dessins use closely related permutation data, while metric ribbon graphs appear in cell decompositions of moduli spaces via Strebel theory.
