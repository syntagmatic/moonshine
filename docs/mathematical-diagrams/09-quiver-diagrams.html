Quiver Diagrams: Arrows, Algebras, and Mutation

Part 9 of 12. Quivers encode the structure of algebras and their representations as directed graphs. Their combinatorics reaches from Gabriel's classification of finite representation type to Fomin and Zelevinsky's cluster algebras.

A quiver is a directed graph: a finite set of vertices and a finite set of arrows between them. Multiple arrows between the same pair of vertices are allowed, and so are loops. Despite the simplicity of the definition, quivers carry a remarkable amount of algebraic structure. Every finite-dimensional algebra over an algebraically closed field is Morita equivalent to one built from a quiver, and the representation theory of these path algebras connects graph combinatorics to homological algebra, Lie theory, and cluster algebras.

What is a quiver?

Formally, a quiver consists of a set of vertices , a set of arrows , and two maps assigning to each arrow its source and target. In practice, we simply draw dots and directed edges. Unlike ordinary graphs, quivers are explicitly directed and permit multiple edges between the same pair of vertices (the Kronecker quiver has two parallel arrows).

The path algebra

Given a quiver and a field , the path algebra is the algebra whose basis consists of all directed paths in the quiver (including a trivial path of length zero at each vertex). Multiplication is concatenation of paths when the endpoint of one path matches the starting point of the next, and zero otherwise:

The trivial paths act as orthogonal idempotents: they are the "local identities" at each vertex. When the quiver is acyclic (has no oriented cycles), the path algebra is finite-dimensional and every indecomposable module corresponds to a representation of the quiver.

Quiver representations

A representation of a quiver assigns a vector space to each vertex i and a linear map to each arrow . The collection of all representations forms an abelian category, and the central question of quiver representation theory is: how many indecomposable representations are there?

Gabriel's theorem (1972). A connected quiver has only finitely many indecomposable representations (up to isomorphism) if and only if its underlying undirected graph is an ADE Dynkin diagram — one of . The indecomposable representations are in bijection with the positive roots of the associated root system.

This is a striking bridge between combinatorics and Lie theory. The Dynkin diagrams that classify simple Lie algebras also classify quivers of finite representation type. The dimension vectors of indecomposable representations are exactly the positive roots, and the number of indecomposables equals the number of positive roots: 3 for , 6 for , 12 for , and so on.

Cluster algebras and mutation

Fomin and Zelevinsky introduced cluster algebras in 2002, partly motivated by total positivity and canonical bases. The combinatorial engine behind a cluster algebra is quiver mutation. Given a quiver without loops or 2-cycles and a vertex , the mutation produces a new quiver by three steps:

  1. For every pair of arrows , add a new arrow .
  2. Reverse all arrows incident to k.
  3. Cancel any resulting 2-cycles (pairs of opposite arrows between the same two vertices).

Mutation is an involution: mutating twice at the same vertex returns the original quiver. The mutation class of a quiver — the set of all quivers reachable by sequences of mutations — is a fundamental invariant. Quivers with finite mutation class are classified and correspond to triangulations of surfaces.

Mutation as a discrete dynamical system. Quiver mutation generates a groupoid acting on the space of quivers. In the finite-type case (ADE Dynkin diagrams), the mutation class is finite and the dynamics is periodic. For other quivers the mutation class can be infinite, and the resulting cluster algebra has infinitely many cluster variables. The Laurent phenomenon guarantees that every cluster variable is a Laurent polynomial in the initial variables — a surprising positivity result whose combinatorial proof occupied the field for a decade.

What the diagram sees

  • Vertices — basis idempotents of the path algebra, or simple modules.
  • Arrows — generators of the radical: each arrow is an irreducible morphism between simples.
  • Paths — composition of morphisms: the path algebra is spanned by all directed walks.
  • Orientation — algebra structure: different orientations of the same underlying graph yield non-isomorphic algebras with equivalent derived categories.
  • Mutation — tilting equivalences: mutating a quiver corresponds to a change of tilting object in the derived category.
  • Finite type — Dynkin classification: finitely many indecomposables iff the graph is ADE.
  • Cluster variables — Laurent polynomials: each mutation step produces a new rational function that is miraculously a Laurent polynomial.
