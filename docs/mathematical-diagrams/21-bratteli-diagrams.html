Core Concept

A Bratteli diagram is a graph arranged in levels. There is usually one root vertex at level 0, a finite set of vertices at each later level, and edges only between adjacent levels. The edges may be repeated. Repetition is not decoration: it records multiplicity.

The same drawing appears in more than one part of algebra. In operator algebras, Bratteli introduced these diagrams for inductive limits of finite-dimensional -algebras, now called AF algebras. In representation theory, a similar diagram records how irreducible representations branch as one moves through a tower such as .

The common idea is modest and useful. If a finite object at level decomposes inside, restricts to, or embeds into objects at level , put an edge for each copy. The graph keeps the local rules visible while paths remember their iterated consequences.

Some authors draw the edges without arrowheads because the levels already give the direction. The important convention is that edges do not skip levels. If one wants to compare level 2 directly to level 5, the diagram is usually telescoped: all paths between those levels become the new edges.

Path Counts and Dimension Vectors

The most immediate calculation on a Bratteli diagram is path counting. Let be the number of paths from the root to vertex at level . If is the incidence matrix from level to level , then the next vector of path counts is obtained by multiplying by that incidence matrix.

These vectors are often called height vectors or dimension vectors, depending on context. The word "dimension" is not accidental in the Young-lattice example. This is a useful warning about what a Bratteli diagram does and does not show. The picture does not draw every path separately once levels become large. Instead, it stores local branching, and the path counts accumulate the global information.

AF Algebra Interpretation

For AF algebras, a finite-dimensional algebra can be written as a direct sum of matrix algebras, for example . When one finite-dimensional algebra embeds into the next, each simple matrix summand can appear with some multiplicity inside a summand at the next level.

The Bratteli diagram records those multiplicities. A vertex represents a simple summand, often labeled by its matrix size. The diagram is not merely a sketch of the construction: up to the usual equivalences such as telescoping, it carries classification data through the ordered dimension group. That statement belongs to operator algebra rather than elementary graph theory, but the visual mechanism is still the same recurrence of multiplicities.

Branching Interpretation

For a tower of split semisimple algebras or finite groups, a Bratteli diagram can be read through restriction. Put the irreducible modules for level on the -th row. If an irreducible at level restricts to a direct sum containing an irreducible at level with multiplicity , draw edges.

For the symmetric groups over the complex numbers, irreducibles are indexed by partitions, and the branching rule removes one corner box. Reversing that direction gives the add-a-box edges in the interactive. Since the branching is multiplicity-free, every edge there is single. Other towers can have repeated edges, and those repetitions matter in the path counts.

Concept Summary

  • Levels: The diagram is graded. Vertices at level represent the finite pieces available at that stage.
  • Edges: Edges connect only adjacent levels and record multiplicities of embedding, restriction, induction, or branching.
  • Incidence matrices: The edge multiplicities form matrices. Products of these matrices count paths across several levels.
  • Dimension vectors: Starting from the root, path counts propagate by a linear recurrence. In representation examples, these counts often become actual module dimensions.
  • AF algebras: For inductive systems of finite-dimensional -algebras, the diagram records how matrix summands are included in later matrix summands.
  • Branching graphs: For towers such as the symmetric groups, the same diagram records how irreducibles restrict or induce between neighboring levels.
