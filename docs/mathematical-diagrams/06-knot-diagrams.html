By a century-old conjecture of Tait — proved in the 1980s using exactly the polynomial computed below — an alternating diagram with no removable kinks is as knotted as it looks, so almost anything you draw is a genuine knot, and the bracket will say so.

Unknotting by click: Now flip crossings so the strand always goes over on its first visit to each crossing. A diagram like that is called descending, and a descending diagram is always the unknot, however tangled it looks. Watch the verdict line: the moment your diagram becomes descending, X(D) collapses to 1, the unknot's value. You have untied your scribble without moving a single strand.

The Core Concept

A knot is a closed loop in 3-space. To study it, project the loop onto the plane and mark which strand crosses over which. The resulting knot diagram, together with Reidemeister moves, is a complete combinatorial way to represent the same knot type.

Knots versus links: A knot is a single closed loop embedded in 3-space. A link is a collection of such loops, possibly intertwined.

Crossings and writhe: At each crossing, one strand passes over and one passes under. Each crossing has a sign (+1 or −1). The crossing number is the minimum number of crossings over all possible diagrams, and the writhe is the sum of all crossing signs:

Reidemeister moves: Reidemeister's theorem says two diagrams represent the same knot if and only if one can be obtained from the other by a sequence of three local moves:

R1 (Twist): A single loop can be added or removed. R2 (Poke): Two strands can be pushed across each other. R3 (Slide): A strand can slide over or under a crossing.

The Kauffman bracket: A more powerful invariant computed from the diagram. Each crossing can be smoothed in two ways, called A and B; choosing one smoothing at every crossing gives a state s, and a diagram with n crossings has 2n states. This is exactly the sum the workspace evaluates, state by state, each time you close a curve or flip a crossing. A normalised version gives the Jones polynomial.

An invariant is a quantity that survives all three Reidemeister moves. The writhe is not quite one: an R1 twist changes it by ±1. The bracket fails R1 in exactly the same way, picking up a factor of −A±3 per twist. The trick is to play the two failures against each other: X(D) = (−A³)−w⋅⟨D⟩ survives all three moves. Substituting A = t−1/4 turns it into the Jones polynomial — an invariant you can compute by hand, or by click, in the workspace above.

Concept Summary

  • Crossings: Encode the 3D over/under information as a finite combinatorial structure.
  • Reidemeister moves: The complete set of diagram operations that correspond to continuous deformations of the knot.
  • The crossing number: A coarse but fundamental measure of knot complexity.
  • The writhe: Counts signed crossings and feeds into more powerful invariants.
  • The Kauffman bracket: Turns crossing data into a polynomial, connecting the visual diagram to algebra.
  • The normalised bracket X(D): Cancelling the writhe's R1 misbehaviour against the bracket's turns it into a genuine invariant — the Jones polynomial up to a change of variable.
  • Recognition: The core problem of determining when two different-looking diagrams encode the same knot.
