Knot Diagrams

Part 6 of 7. Crossings, invariants, and the Reidemeister moves that tell us when two tangles are secretly the same knot.

Take a piece of string, tangle it up, and glue the ends together. That closed loop in 3-space is a knot. But to study it, we need a picture: project the loop onto the plane and mark which strand crosses over which. The result is a knot diagram, and it captures everything we need to know, because any question about the 3D knot can be answered by manipulating its diagram.

The central problem is recognition. Two diagrams can look completely different and yet represent the same knot. Kurt Reidemeister proved in 1927 that two diagrams represent the same knot if and only if they are related by a finite sequence of three local moves. Those moves, together with the invariants that survive them, are what this explainer is about.

Knots versus links

A knot is a single closed loop embedded in 3-space. A link is a collection of such loops, possibly intertwined. The simplest knot is the unknot: a loop with no crossings at all, equivalent to a circle. The simplest nontrivial link is the Hopf link: two circles linked once.

Why diagrams? A knot lives in three dimensions, but a diagram is a 2D object with extra data at each crossing. This reduction from 3D to 2D-with-crossing-info is what makes knot theory combinatorial, and what makes it computable.

Crossings and writhe

At each crossing, two strands meet. One passes over (drawn as a continuous arc) and one passes under (drawn with a gap). Each crossing also has a sign: orient the knot, and the sign is +1 or −1 depending on the handedness of the crossing. The crossing number of a knot is the minimum number of crossings over all possible diagrams, and the writhe is the sum of all crossing signs.

Reidemeister moves

Reidemeister's theorem says two diagrams represent the same knot if and only if one can be obtained from the other by a sequence of three local moves, together with planar isotopy (smooth deformation that does not change crossings).

Figure 2. The three Reidemeister moves. R1 adds or removes a twist. R2 adds or removes two crossings between the same pair of strands. R3 slides a strand across a crossing.

R1: Twist

A single loop (a "kink") can be added to or removed from a strand. This changes the crossing number by 1 and the writhe by ±1. Because R1 changes the writhe, the writhe is not a knot invariant by itself, but it becomes one when combined with other information (for instance, in the Jones polynomial).

R2: Poke

Two strands can be pushed across each other, creating or destroying two crossings at once. The crossing signs of the two new crossings are opposite, so the writhe is unchanged.

R3: Slide

A strand can slide over or under a crossing. This move changes the arrangement of three crossings but preserves both the crossing number and the writhe.

Reidemeister completeness. These three moves are complete: any deformation of the 3D knot that does not cut the string corresponds to a finite sequence of R1, R2, and R3 moves on the diagram. The diagram is a faithful combinatorial proxy for the topological object.

The crossing number

The crossing number of a knot type is the minimum number of crossings over all diagrams of that knot. The unknot has crossing number 0. The trefoil has crossing number 3; no diagram of the trefoil can have fewer than three crossings. The figure-eight knot has crossing number 4.

Computing the crossing number is hard in general: you need to prove that no simpler diagram exists. But it gives us a coarse classification. Knots are often named by their crossing number: the trefoil is 31 (the first knot with 3 crossings), the figure-eight is 41.

The Kauffman bracket

A more powerful invariant comes from the Kauffman bracket, a polynomial computed from the diagram. At each crossing, there are two ways to "smooth" it (replace the crossing with two non-crossing arcs). The bracket sums over all 2n possible smoothings, weighted by powers of a variable A.

The bracket is not quite a knot invariant (it changes under R1), but a normalised version gives the Jones polynomial, one of the most important invariants in knot theory. The Jones polynomial distinguishes the trefoil from the unknot, the figure-eight from the trefoil, and many more pairs that simpler invariants cannot tell apart.

From crossings to algebra. The Kauffman bracket converts the visual data of a crossing diagram into a polynomial. Different diagrams of the same knot yield the same polynomial (after normalisation). The diagram has been distilled into an algebraic invariant, and the visual language of crossings has become a computation.

Famous knots

The unknot

The simplest knot: a plain circle. Crossing number 0, writhe 0. Every knot diagram with crossing number 0 is the unknot, but recognising a complicated diagram as secretly the unknot is a deep problem (it is in NP and co-NP).

The trefoil

The simplest nontrivial knot, with crossing number 3. All three crossings have the same sign, so the writhe is ±3 depending on orientation. The trefoil is chiral: it is not equivalent to its mirror image (the left trefoil and right trefoil are distinct knots).

The figure-eight knot

Crossing number 4, with two positive and two negative crossings, giving writhe 0. Unlike the trefoil, the figure-eight knot is amphichiral: it equals its mirror image.

The Hopf link

Two circles linked once, with 2 crossings. The simplest nontrivial link. Its linking number is ±1.

What the diagram sees

  • Crossings encode the 3D over/under information as a finite combinatorial structure.
  • Reidemeister moves are the complete set of diagram operations that correspond to continuous deformations of the knot.
  • The crossing number is a coarse but fundamental measure of knot complexity.
  • The writhe counts signed crossings and feeds into more powerful invariants.
  • The Kauffman bracket turns crossing data into a polynomial, connecting the visual diagram to algebra and the Jones polynomial.
  • Recognition is the core problem: determining when two different-looking diagrams encode the same knot.
