Commutative Diagrams: The Language of Category Theory

Part 1 of 11. A commutative diagram lays out equations between composite maps as paths in a graph. Which paths agree is asserted by the author; the picture alone never shows it.

This series is about pictures you can compute with. The commutative diagram comes first because it is the plainest case: a directed graph whose meaning is a list of equations between composite maps. Pick two objects below and the figure composes every path between them, then asks whether the listed equations make those composites agree.

Click two nodes to check commutativity.

A drawn diagram is a presentation: the arrows are generators and the declared equalities are relations. Nothing in the picture forces g′ ∘ f to equal f′ ∘ g. “This diagram commutes” is a claim the author adds, which is why papers have to say it.

Pasting squares

A category has objects and, between any two, a set of morphisms that compose associatively, with an identity on each object. A commutative diagram is a directed graph of objects and morphisms in which paths with the same ends have equal composites. The fact used most is pasting: if every small square commutes, so does the outer rectangle. The proof rewrites one route into the other a square at a time.

Figure 2. Left: click a square (or Tab to it and press Enter) to declare or withdraw its equality. Right: all ten routes from the top-left corner to the bottom-right, ordered by how many squares lie above and to the right of them. Two routes are joined when one declared square turns one into the other. The target route is outlined; click another to change it. With all six squares declared, the two outer routes are six rewrites apart. Withdraw any one square and they fall into separate pieces, because each rewrite flips exactly one square and getting from one outer route to the other must flip all six.

Arrows that carry claims

Arrow shapes make claims too. A hooked arrow ↪ marks a monomorphism, a map m that cancels on the left: m ∘ x = m ∘ y forces x = y. A double-headed arrow ↠ marks an epimorphism, which cancels on the right, and a dashed arrow asserts that a map exists, often uniquely. In sets the cancellation tests come out as injective and surjective, but that is a fact about sets: in rings the inclusion ℤ → ℚ is an epimorphism, because a ring map out of ℚ is fixed by what it does on ℤ, yet it is far from surjective.

For mono the figure tries every pair of maps x, y from a one-point set T into A and looks for m ∘ x = m ∘ y with x ≠ y. For epi it tries every pair u, v from B into a two-point set S and looks for u ∘ m = v ∘ m with u ≠ v. In sets, test objects of one and two points are enough. The default map is neither: it sends 0 and 1 to the same place and misses c.

Exactness

Some claims are not equalities of paths at all. A sequence A → B → C is exact at B when the image of the first map is exactly the kernel of the second. That is why the short exact sequence in Figure 1 declares nothing. Take all three groups to be ℤ/12, with maps that multiply by s and by t.

Figure 5. ℤ/12 as a clock. Outer ring: the image of x ↦ sx. Inner ring: the kernel of y ↦ ty. The composite is zero when the image lies inside the kernel, and the sequence is exact when they coincide, which happens exactly when gcd(s, 12) · gcd(t, 12) = 12. The default s = 4, t = 6 has image {0, 4, 8} inside the kernel {0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10}: the composite is zero, but the kernel is twice as big, so the sequence is not exact.
