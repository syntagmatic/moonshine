String Diagrams: Wiring Algebra

Part 7 of 7. A graphical calculus for monoidal categories: objects become wires, morphisms become boxes, and the topology of the picture encodes every algebraic equation.

Most mathematical notation is one-dimensional. g ∘ f means "first f, then g," but the composition is invisible: nothing in the formula says how the output of f connects to the input of g. String diagrams make the wiring explicit. Every wire carries a type (an object in a monoidal category), every box carries a morphism, and the geometry of the picture is the algebra.

Monoidal categories

A monoidal category is a category equipped with a tensor product and a unit object , satisfying coherence conditions (associativity and unit isomorphisms). The archetypal example is the category of finite-dimensional vector spaces, where the tensor product is the usual tensor product of vector spaces and is the ground field.

The key insight of Joyal and Street (1991) is that every equation in a monoidal category can be stated as a topological deformation of a string diagram. Two diagrams represent the same morphism if and only if one can be continuously deformed into the other, preserving the connectivity of wires to boxes.

The visual dictionary

In string diagrams, reading flows top to bottom:

  • Objects are wires — vertical lines labelled by type.
  • Morphisms are boxes — rectangles sitting on wires, with input wires entering from the top and output wires exiting at the bottom.
  • Composition (applying f then g) is vertical stacking: connect the output wires of f to the input wires of g.
  • Tensor product (f ⊗ g) is horizontal juxtaposition: place f and g side by side.
  • Identity is a bare wire with no box.

Why it works. The interchange law of monoidal categories — — is built into the geometry for free. Sliding a box up or down without changing the wiring does not change the morphism.

Braiding and symmetry

In a braided monoidal category, objects can cross past each other. The braiding swaps two wires, drawn as a crossing. In a symmetric monoidal category (like vector spaces), the two crossings are equal — — so the diagram has no over/under distinction.

In a genuinely braided (non-symmetric) category, the two crossings are distinct: the Yang-Baxter equation governs their interaction, and we recover the braid group relations from the previous explainer.

Duals: cups and caps

When an object A has a dual A*, we gain two special morphisms: the unit (cup) and the counit (cap) . These satisfy the snake identities (also called zig-zag equations):

Graphically, the snake identities say that an S-bend in a wire can be straightened out. The trace of a morphism is constructed by bending a wire from an output back to an input using a cup and a cap. In the category of finite-dimensional vector spaces, this recovers the ordinary matrix trace: the cup creates a "loop" that sums the diagonal entries.

A category where every object has a dual is called compact closed. The paradigmatic example is the category of finite-dimensional vector spaces with the standard dual. In a compact closed category, every morphism corresponds uniquely to a morphism called its name, obtained by bending the input wire into a cup. This is the categorical version of currying.

Worked examples

Linear algebra. In the category of finite-dimensional vector spaces, a morphism is a matrix. Composition is matrix multiplication. The tensor product is the Kronecker product. The trace of an endomorphism corresponds to the cup-cap construction, recovering .

Quantum circuits. A quantum gate is a unitary morphism on a tensor product of qubit spaces. The CNOT gate acts on two qubits: the first (control) wire passes through unchanged if the control qubit is |0>, while the second (target) wire gets flipped if the control is |1>. In the string diagram this is simply a box with two input wires and two output wires. Composing gates is vertical stacking; running independent gates in parallel is horizontal juxtaposition. The entire circuit-model of quantum computation is a string diagram in the dagger compact category FdHilb.

Signal flow. In control theory, string diagrams model signal-flow graphs. Bonchi, Sobocinski, and Zanasi showed that the full algebra of signal-flow diagrams arises from a symmetric monoidal theory with a Frobenius structure. The braiding lets signals cross, and the trace construction models feedback loops: the same cup-cap that gives matrix trace in linear algebra gives the transfer-function feedback formula in control theory. The graphical calculus makes it impossible to accidentally drop or duplicate a signal without an explicit copy or discard morphism.

Natural language. The DisCoCat model of Coecke, Sadrzadeh, and Clark uses string diagrams in the category of finite-dimensional vector spaces to compose word meanings into sentence meanings. Nouns are objects, transitive verbs are morphisms with two inputs (subject and object) and one output (sentence), and composition follows the syntactic parse tree. The string diagram makes the flow of meaning visible.

Coherence as topology. Mac Lane's coherence theorem says every diagram of canonical isomorphisms (associators, unitors) commutes. In string diagrams, every equation between canonical isomorphisms is a trivial deformation of wires; the topology handles coherence automatically.

Beyond monoidal categories. String diagrams extend to bicategories (2-cells become regions between wires), to traced monoidal categories (feedback), and to compact closed categories (every object has a dual). The formalism underpins modern approaches to quantum computing, linguistics (DisCoCat), and database theory.

What the diagram sees

  • Composition — vertical connection: outputs of one box feed inputs of the next.
  • Tensor product — horizontal juxtaposition: independent processes side by side.
  • Braiding — wire crossings: the swap isomorphism made visible.
  • Duals — cups and caps: wires bend to create units and counits.
  • Trace — feedback loops: output wires routed back to inputs via cup-cap pairs.
  • Equations — topology: two diagrams represent the same morphism iff one deforms into the other.
  • Functoriality — structure preservation: a monoidal functor maps one wiring diagram to another, preserving boxes and connections.
  • Coherence — invisible isomorphisms: associators and unitors are absorbed into the geometry, so they never appear as explicit boxes.
