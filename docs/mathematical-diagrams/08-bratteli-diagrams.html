Definition

A Bratteli diagram is a graph arranged in levels. There is usually one root vertex at level 0, a finite set of vertices at each later level, and edges only between adjacent levels. Edges may be repeated, and the repetition records multiplicity.

The same drawing appears in more than one part of algebra. In operator algebras, Bratteli introduced these diagrams for inductive limits of finite-dimensional -algebras, now called AF algebras. In representation theory, a similar diagram records how irreducible representations branch as one moves through a tower such as .

In both cases, if a finite object at level decomposes inside, restricts to, or embeds into objects at level , put an edge for each copy. The edges record the rule between adjacent levels, and paths compose those rules across many levels.

Some authors draw the edges without arrowheads because the levels already give the direction. The convention that matters is that edges do not skip levels. If one wants to compare level 2 directly to level 5, the diagram is usually telescoped: all paths between those levels become the new edges.

The Hook Length Formula

For Young's lattice the recurrence has a closed form. The hook of a box in a shape is the box itself, the boxes to its right in the same row, and the boxes below it in the same column. The formula does not show why the quotient is an integer or why it counts paths. Greene, Nijenhuis and Wilf gave a probabilistic proof in 1979. A random "hook walk" that starts at a uniformly chosen box and jumps to a random box of its current hook, other than itself, ends at a corner, and it ends at each corner with probability equal to the fraction of paths whose last step adds that corner. Summing over corners is the recurrence run backwards one level.

AF Algebra Interpretation

For AF algebras, a finite-dimensional algebra can be written as a direct sum of matrix algebras, for example . When one finite-dimensional algebra embeds into the next, each simple matrix summand can appear with some multiplicity inside a summand at the next level.

The Bratteli diagram records those multiplicities. A vertex represents a simple summand, often labeled by its matrix size. If there are copies of the -th summand at level inside the -th summand at level , draw edges between the corresponding vertices. Passing to the limit gives an approximately finite-dimensional algebra. Bratteli showed in 1972 that, up to equivalences such as telescoping, the diagram determines the algebra. Elliott showed in 1976 that the ordered dimension group computed from the diagram, together with its scale, already determines it. Those are theorems of operator algebra, but the part the diagram plays in them is the same recurrence of multiplicities.

Branching Interpretation

For a tower of split semisimple algebras or finite groups, a Bratteli diagram can be read through restriction. Put the irreducible modules for level on the -th row. If an irreducible at level restricts to a direct sum containing an irreducible at level with multiplicity , draw edges.

For the symmetric groups over the complex numbers, irreducibles are indexed by partitions, and the branching rule removes one corner box. Reversing that direction gives the add-a-box edges in the interactive. Since the branching is multiplicity-free, every edge there is single. Other towers have repeated edges, which enter the path counts.
