Bratteli Diagrams: Levels, Branching, and Limits

Part 8 of 10. A Bratteli diagram is a leveled graph whose edges record how finite algebras or representations embed or branch. Counting its paths gives dimensions, such as those of the symmetric groups' representations.

A Bratteli diagram is read by counting paths: its vertices sit in levels, each edge records one copy of a piece at one level inside a piece at the next, and the number of paths down to a vertex is a size, such as the dimension of a representation. This part asks how those counts are computed and what they measure.

Definition

A Bratteli diagram is a graph arranged in levels. There is usually one root vertex at level 0, a finite set of vertices at each later level, and edges only between adjacent levels. Edges may be repeated, and the repetition records multiplicity.

Bratteli introduced these diagrams in 1972 for inductive limits of finite-dimensional -algebras, now called AF algebras. Representation theory draws the same picture for how irreducible representations branch along a tower such as . Either way an edge is one copy of a level- piece inside a level- piece, and a path composes those copies across many levels.

The Hook Length Formula

For Young's lattice the recurrence has a closed form. The hook of a box in a shape is the box itself, the boxes to its right in the same row, and the boxes below it in the same column. Its hook length is the number of boxes in the hook. The formula does not show why the quotient is an integer or why it counts paths. Greene, Nijenhuis and Wilf's 1979 proof does. Start at a uniformly random box and keep jumping to a random other box of the current hook. The walk stops at a corner with probability , the fraction of paths whose last step adds that corner, so summing over corners is the recurrence run backwards one level.

Branching

For a tower of finite groups or split semisimple algebras, put the irreducible modules of level on row . When an irreducible at level restricts to a sum containing an irreducible at level with multiplicity , draw that many edges.

AF Algebras and the Limit Shape

A finite-dimensional -algebra is a sum of matrix algebras, . When it sits inside the next one, the -th summand appears times inside the -th, and those multiplicities are the edges. For a unital inclusion the matrix sizes obey the path recurrence, which is why Figure 2's readout can name the algebra at each level.

Bratteli showed in 1972 that the diagram, up to telescoping, determines the limit algebra. Elliott showed in 1976 that the ordered dimension group computed from the diagram, with its scale, already does.

The group algebra joins the two readings.
