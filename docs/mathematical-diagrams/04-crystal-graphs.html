Two colors

In higher rank every simple root gets its own color, and the local patterns between colors encode the Cartan matrix, the same root geometry a Dynkin diagram records (see Simple Roots and Dynkin Diagrams in the Exceptional Atlas). A weight diagram only says how many basis vectors sit at each weight. The crystal also says where the operators send each one, so vertices that share a weight can differ in their arrows.

Highest and lowest weights

A highest-weight crystal has a vertex killed by every raising operator, for all , and lowering operators generate the rest of its component from there. In finite type the module is finite-dimensional, so the crystal is finite and has a single lowest vertex, killed by every lowering operator; in Figure 2 these are the top and bottom tableaux. (In affine type the module and its crystal are infinite and there is no lowest vertex.) Which weight counts as highest depends on a choice of positive roots, and once that is fixed the highest weight determines the irreducible representation.

Tableau connection

The vertices in Figure 2 are semistandard Young tableaux, and in type A that realization works for every highest weight. The Bratteli diagram article counts standard tableaux; here entries may repeat. The shape records the highest weight, the entries record a basis element, and the weight is the content: how many 1s, 2s and 3s appear.

The operators on tableaux come straight from the tensor rule. Read the tableau as a word, rows from top to bottom and each row right to left, and treat the word as a tensor product of single letters. For color a letter is a factor that can lower and a letter is one that can raise, so the rule turns into bracket matching: each opens, each closes, and matched pairs cancel. Kashiwara and Nakashima gave explicit tableau models for all the classical types. Tableaux, words, Littlewood-Richardson coefficients and character calculations all fit into this operator picture, which is why crystal graphs appear in combinatorics.
