Dessins d'Enfants: Children's Drawings and the Absolute Galois Group

Part 10 of 12. Grothendieck's astonishing insight: simple bipartite graphs drawn on surfaces encode the deepest symmetries of number theory.

In 1984, Alexandre Grothendieck wrote a research proposal that would reshape the landscape between combinatorics, algebraic geometry, and number theory. He described his amazement that something as elementary as a child's drawing — a graph with vertices coloured black and white, edges connecting only different colours — could encode the action of the absolute Galois group, the most mysterious symmetry group in all of mathematics. He called these drawings dessins d'enfants.

A dessin d'enfant is a bipartite graph embedded in a compact oriented surface. The vertices are coloured in two colours (traditionally black and white), and every edge connects a black vertex to a white vertex. The surface is usually the Riemann sphere, but higher-genus surfaces appear naturally. What makes dessins remarkable is not the drawing itself but what it represents: a combinatorial shadow of deep algebraic and arithmetic structure.

The idea sits at a striking junction. Graph theory, one of the most concrete branches of mathematics, meets algebraic geometry, one of the most abstract. The translation between them is mediated by a single analytic object: the Belyi function.

Belyi's theorem

The bridge between dessins and arithmetic is Belyi's theorem (1979). A compact Riemann surface X is defined over a number field — meaning its defining equations have coefficients in some finite extension of the rationals — if and only if there exists a meromorphic function

that is ramified only over three points: 0, 1, and ∞. Such a function is called a Belyi function. The preimage of the real interval [0, 1] under a Belyi function is precisely a dessin d'enfant: the preimages of 0 are the black vertices, the preimages of 1 are the white vertices, and each connected arc of the preimage is an edge.

Belyi's theorem says that every algebraic curve over the algebraic numbers carries at least one such function, and therefore at least one dessin. The passage from the curve to the dessin loses geometric information but preserves the arithmetic — the field of definition, the Galois orbit, the ramification data. Grothendieck later wrote that this theorem, "so simple that it could be stated to a child," had left him "confounded."

From functions to drawings

The construction is concrete. Given a Belyi function of degree d, mark the d preimages of 0 as black dots and the d preimages of 1 as white dots on the surface. The preimage of the open interval (0, 1) consists of d disjoint arcs, each connecting a black dot to a white dot. These arcs are the edges of the dessin. The complement of the graph on the surface decomposes into simply connected faces, giving the dessin its cell structure.

Conversely, every bipartite graph embedded in an oriented surface (with all faces simply connected) determines a unique Belyi function up to isomorphism. The correspondence is bijective: dessins are Belyi functions, seen from a combinatorial angle.

The passport

Each dessin carries a combinatorial invariant called the passport (or partition triple). If the Belyi function has degree d, then the passport records the ramification at each of the three branch points as a partition of d:

The black partition lists the degrees of the black vertices (how many edges meet each one), the white partition lists the degrees of the white vertices, and the face partition describes the cell decomposition of the surface. All three partitions sum to d, the total number of edges.

Two dessins with the same passport may still be combinatorially distinct — the passport classifies only up to a coarse equivalence. The number of non-isomorphic dessins sharing a given passport can be large, and understanding this multiplicity is one of the central problems in the theory.

Why "children's drawings"? Grothendieck chose the name deliberately. The objects are literally as simple as a child's drawing: dots connected by lines, some filled and some open. What astonished him was that this preschool simplicity was enough to capture the full action of the most powerful symmetry group in number theory. He wrote: "This discovery, which is technically so simple, made a very strong impression on me, and it represents a decisive turning point in the course of my reflections."

The absolute Galois group

The deepest consequence of Belyi's theorem is that the absolute Galois group

acts on the set of all dessins. An element of this group permutes the algebraic numbers while fixing the rationals. Since a Belyi function is defined over the algebraic numbers, applying a Galois automorphism to its coefficients produces a new Belyi function — hence a new dessin. This action preserves the passport but can change the combinatorial structure of the graph.

Grothendieck's key insight was that this action is faithful: distinct elements of the Galois group always produce distinct dessins on some surface. The group is thus embedded, in its full complexity, into the combinatorics of children's drawings. No finite collection of dessins suffices to detect every element — the group's structure is reflected only in the totality of all dessins.

This faithfulness is what makes the theory so striking. The absolute Galois group is a profinite group of enormous complexity — understanding it is essentially equivalent to understanding all of algebraic number theory. Yet it acts on objects that a child could draw with a pencil.

Grothendieck's Esquisse d'un Programme. In his 1984 research proposal, Grothendieck outlined a programme to study the absolute Galois group through its action on dessins and on the "Teichmüller tower" — the system of moduli spaces of curves with marked points. He wrote of being "struck by the incredible richness" of this action, calling it "a deep and in many ways mysterious phenomenon." The Esquisse was never formally published during his lifetime but circulated widely, seeding an entire field at the crossroads of combinatorics, topology, and arithmetic geometry. Its vision — that the Galois group could be understood through topology and combinatorics — remains one of the most ambitious programmes in modern mathematics.

The moonshine connection

The j-invariant, the fundamental modular function that classifies elliptic curves, is itself a Belyi function. It maps the modular curve X(1) to the Riemann sphere, ramified only over 0, 1728, and ∞. After normalising so that the branch points become {0, 1, ∞}, the j-function becomes a Belyi function of degree 1728, and its preimage of [0, 1] is a dessin with 1728 edges.

The passport of this dessin encodes the ramification: the black vertices have degrees dictated by the order-3 elliptic point (each of degree 3, giving 576 black vertices), and the white vertices by the order-2 elliptic point (each of degree 2, giving 864 white vertices). These numbers — 1728, the orders 2 and 3, the partition structure — are precisely the numerology that appears in monstrous moonshine, the unexpected connection between the Monster group and modular functions.

The Monster group, the largest sporadic simple group with roughly 8 × 1053 elements, was conjectured by Conway and Norton (1979) to govern the Fourier coefficients of the j-function. This "monstrous moonshine" conjecture, proved by Borcherds in 1992, means that the dessin of the j-function encodes not just the arithmetic of elliptic curves but also the representation theory of the Monster. The drawing is a combinatorial gateway to one of the deepest coincidences in mathematics.

The moonshine numerology. The j-function's Fourier expansion begins j(q) = q−1 + 744 + 196884q + …, and the coefficient 196884 equals 196883 + 1 — the sum of the two smallest dimensions of irreducible representations of the Monster group. The dessin of the j-function sits at the intersection of three vast subjects: the combinatorics of bipartite graphs, the arithmetic of number fields, and the representation theory of the largest sporadic simple group. That a single children's drawing could touch all three is, as Grothendieck might have said, "worthy of deep meditation."
