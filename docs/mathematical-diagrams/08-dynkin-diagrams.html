The Core Concept

In the 1890s, Wilhelm Killing set out to classify all simple Lie algebras over the complex numbers. The result, refined by Cartan and later distilled into graphical form by Dynkin, is one of the great classification theorems in mathematics.

Every finite-dimensional simple complex Lie algebra corresponds to exactly one connected Dynkin diagram: a small graph whose nodes are simple roots and whose edges encode angles and relative root lengths. Semisimple Lie algebras correspond to disjoint unions of these diagrams. The complete connected list is short: four infinite families and five exceptional cases.

Simple roots and the Cartan matrix: A semisimple Lie algebra has a root system. A subset of simple roots forms a basis. The geometry of the simple roots is captured by the Cartan matrix. The diagram reconstructs the full Cartan matrix and hence the Lie algebra.

From matrix to diagram:

  • Each node represents a simple root.
  • Two nodes are unconnected if the roots are orthogonal.
  • A single bond means the angle between the roots is 120°.
  • A double bond means the angle is 135°.
  • A triple bond means the angle is 150°.

ADE: the simply-laced types: A Dynkin diagram is simply-laced if every bond is single — equivalently, all roots have the same length. These are the types An, Dn, E6, E7, E8. The simply-laced condition turns up far beyond Lie theory, in surface singularities, quiver representations, and conformal field theory.

Why the List Stops: Build Your Own

The catalog looks arbitrary until you try to extend it. Why does the D family fork exactly once? Why does the E family stop at E8? The reason is a positivity condition. Each node claims a vector, and the bonds fix the angles between adjacent vectors (120°, 135°, 150°). The question is whether vectors at those angles can actually exist as a basis of Euclidean space. They can precisely when the Gram matrix of the configuration is positive definite, and almost every graph fails that test.

The classification sandbox. The verdict comes from the symmetrized Gram matrix: 2 on the diagonal, −√m for a bond of multiplicity m. Symmetrizing discards the arrow on double and triple bonds, so the direction does not affect the verdict and Bn and Cn are reported together. Watch det B: it hits zero exactly when a diagram crosses from finite to affine, and goes negative-or-mixed beyond.

Every diagram in the catalog is one edit away from the edge.

The classification theorem is a positivity test. A Dynkin diagram is shorthand for a quadratic form, and the theorem says the form stays positive definite only for the A–G shapes. The affine diagrams are the boundary cases, positive semidefinite with a kernel; everything past them is indefinite. The five exceptional algebras exist because five special shapes fit just inside the boundary.

Concept Summary

  • Roots: Each node is a simple root in the root system.
  • Angles: Bonds encode the angle between adjacent roots.
  • Root lengths: In non-simply-laced types, the double or triple bond carries an implicit arrow pointing toward the shorter root.
  • The Cartan matrix: Recoverable directly from the diagram's adjacency and bond multiplicities.
  • The Weyl group: One generator per node, relations encoded by the edges.
  • Classification: The finiteness theorem shows only A, B, C, D, E, F, G shapes are possible.
  • ADE universality: The simply-laced types reappear across mathematics and physics.
  • McKay correspondence: Finite subgroups of SU(2) map bijectively to ADE types via representation graphs.
