Dynkin Diagrams: Classifying Simple Lie Algebras

Part 8 of 12. A handful of dots and lines classify every semisimple Lie algebra — four infinite families and five exceptional cases, and nothing else.

In the 1890s, Wilhelm Killing set out to classify all simple Lie algebras over the complex numbers. The question sounds impossibly broad: a Lie algebra is an infinite-dimensional object defined by a bracket operation, and there is no obvious reason the possibilities should be finite. Yet the result, refined by Elie Cartan and later distilled into a clean graphical language by Eugene Dynkin, is one of the great classification theorems in mathematics.

Every simple Lie algebra corresponds to exactly one Dynkin diagram — a small graph whose nodes represent simple roots and whose edges encode the angles between them. The entire structure of the algebra, its root system, its representations, its Weyl group, is determined by this picture. What could have been an unruly zoo turns out to be a short, elegant list: four infinite families and five exceptional cases.

Simple roots and the Cartan matrix

A semisimple Lie algebra has a root system: a finite set of vectors in a Euclidean space that encode the algebra's adjoint representation. The roots are the "eigenvalues" of the adjoint action of a maximal abelian subalgebra (the Cartan subalgebra), and they organise the algebra into a direct sum of one-dimensional root spaces.

Among these roots, a subset of simple roots forms a basis with two properties: every root is a linear combination of simple roots with coefficients that are either all non-negative or all non-positive, and no simple root is a positive combination of others. The number of simple roots is the rank of the algebra.

From matrix to diagram

The Dynkin diagram encodes the Cartan matrix visually:

  • Each node represents a simple root .
  • Two nodes are unconnected if the roots are orthogonal ().
  • A single bond means the angle between the roots is 120° (both off-diagonal entries are −1).
  • A double bond means the angle is 135° (off-diagonal entries −1 and −2, one root is longer).
  • A triple bond means the angle is 150° (off-diagonal entries −1 and −3, as in ).

The diagram is enough to reconstruct the full Cartan matrix and hence the entire Lie algebra. Two diagrams determine isomorphic Lie algebras if and only if the diagrams are identical as graphs (including bond multiplicities). This is a remarkable fact: the enormous complexity of an infinite-dimensional representation theory is governed entirely by a picture with at most eight nodes.

ADE: the simply-laced types

A Dynkin diagram is simply-laced if every bond is single — equivalently, all roots have the same length. These are the types . The simply-laced condition may seem like a technical restriction, but ADE diagrams turn up far beyond Lie theory:

  • Surface singularities: du Val singularities of algebraic surfaces are classified by ADE types.
  • Quiver representations: Gabriel's theorem (1972) says a connected quiver has finitely many indecomposable representations if and only if its underlying graph is an ADE Dynkin diagram. (We explore quivers in Part 9.)
  • Conformal field theory: the ADE classification governs modular invariant partition functions of the WZW model. Cappelli, Itzykson, and Zuber showed that the allowed modular invariants are labelled by ADE Dynkin diagrams.
  • McKay correspondence: finite subgroups of are classified by ADE, and their representation graphs are the extended (affine) Dynkin diagrams.

Why ADE keeps appearing. The ADE diagrams are precisely the connected graphs whose largest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix is less than 2. This spectral condition — a purely combinatorial fact about graphs — is the common thread linking Lie algebras, singularity theory, quiver representations, and conformal field theory. Whenever a classification problem reduces to finding graphs with bounded spectral radius, the same nine shapes emerge.

This is not a metaphor. The proofs in each domain independently arrive at the same eigenvalue bound, and the list of graphs satisfying it is finite: the An chains, the Dn forks, and the three exceptional E types. The ubiquity of ADE is a theorem, not a coincidence.

The McKay correspondence

John McKay observed in 1980 that the finite subgroups of — the cyclic groups, binary dihedral groups, and the binary tetrahedral, octahedral, and icosahedral groups — are in bijection with the ADE Dynkin diagrams. Specifically, take a finite subgroup , decompose its two-dimensional representation into irreducibles, and build a graph whose nodes are the irreducible representations and whose edges record how tensoring with the fundamental representation decomposes. The resulting graph is an extended (affine) Dynkin diagram, and removing the extending node gives the ordinary Dynkin diagram.

The correspondence goes deeper than a mere list. The number of irreducible representations of the finite subgroup equals the number of nodes in the affine Dynkin diagram, and the dimensions of these representations are the coefficients of the highest root when expanded in simple roots — a numerical coincidence that McKay's construction explains geometrically.

McKay's miracle. The McKay correspondence connects group theory (finite subgroups of SU(2)), geometry (du Val singularities of surfaces), algebra (quiver representations), and Lie theory (Dynkin diagrams) through a single combinatorial object. It extends further: the resolution of a du Val singularity produces a configuration of rational curves whose intersection matrix is the negative of the Cartan matrix of the corresponding ADE type.

The correspondence has been extended to higher dimensions by Bridgeland, King, and Reid (the derived McKay correspondence), connecting it to derived categories of coherent sheaves. What began as a curious observation about SU(2) subgroups has become a central organizing principle in modern algebraic geometry.

Reading the diagram

Every structural fact about a simple Lie algebra can be extracted from its Dynkin diagram:

  • Rank = number of nodes = dimension of the Cartan subalgebra.
  • Dimension of the Lie algebra = rank + 2 × (number of positive roots). For example, has rank 3 and 6 positive roots, so .
  • Weyl group is generated by reflections, one per node; the bond type determines the order of the product of two reflections (3 for a single bond, 4 for double, 6 for triple).
  • Representations are indexed by dominant weights, one fundamental weight per node. Marking a single node selects a fundamental representation.
  • Subalgebras correspond to sub-diagrams: deleting a node gives a semisimple subalgebra. For instance, removing the branch node from reveals an sub-diagram.

The diagram also determines the Dynkin index of embeddings between algebras, the branching rules for how representations decompose under restriction to subalgebras, and the structure of the nilpotent orbits of the algebra. What appears to be a simple graph carries an extraordinary amount of algebraic information.

A particularly elegant application: the highest root of the algebra can be read from the diagram as the unique root whose coefficients in the simple-root basis are maximal. Extending the diagram by adding a node for the negative of the highest root produces the affine Dynkin diagram, which classifies affine Lie algebras, infinite-dimensional algebras central to conformal field theory and string theory.

Historical note

Killing's original 1888–1890 papers contained the essential classification but with several gaps and errors — he initially believed there were more exceptional types. Cartan corrected and completed the classification in his 1894 thesis, establishing the definitive list. The diagrammatic notation we use today was introduced by Dynkin in 1947, replacing Cartan's more cumbersome matrix-based descriptions.

The shift from matrix to picture was not merely cosmetic: the diagram made visible the combinatorial constraints that force the classification, and it became the standard language for discussing root systems, Weyl groups, and representation theory. Today, Dynkin diagrams appear on the first page of any treatment of semisimple Lie algebras — a testament to how thoroughly the visual language has displaced the matrix formalism it replaced.

What the diagram sees

  • Roots — each node is a simple root in the root system.
  • Angles — bonds encode the angle between adjacent roots: no bond (90°), single (120°), double (135°), triple (150°).
  • Root lengths — in non-simply-laced types, the double or triple bond carries an implicit arrow pointing toward the shorter root.
  • The Cartan matrix — recoverable directly from the diagram's adjacency and bond multiplicities.
  • The Weyl group — one generator per node, relations encoded by the edges.
  • Classification — the finiteness theorem: only A, B, C, D, E, F, G shapes are possible.
  • ADE universality — the simply-laced types reappear in singularity theory, quiver representations, and conformal field theory.
  • McKay correspondence — finite subgroups of SU(2) map bijectively to ADE types via representation graphs.
  • Branching rules — sub-diagrams encode how representations decompose under restriction to subalgebras.
  • Affine extension — adding one node for the negative highest root produces the affine Dynkin diagram, governing infinite-dimensional algebras and conformal field theory.
