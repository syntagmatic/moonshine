Nomograms: From a Straightedge to the Smith Chart

Part 11 of 11. Lay a ruler across three graduated scales and, wherever three marks line up, an equation holds. Carried into the complex plane, the same idea gives the Smith chart.

Part 10 used the shape of a diagram to price a computation. A nomogram does the computation itself: graduated scales drawn so that any straight line across them picks out three values satisfying one fixed equation. Lay a ruler on two known values and read the third. The page follows the idea from real scales to projective changes of the chart, and ends with the Smith chart, where the equation is complex and the ruler becomes a pivoted arm.

From crossing curves to straight lines

A chart for can be drawn as hyperbolas on ordinary axes, one per value of . After France's railway law of 1842 made quick earthwork estimates a necessity, the engineer Léon Lalanne found in 1843 that such curves could be made straight by regraduating the axes. He called this anamorphosis and set it out in the Annales des Ponts et Chaussées in 1846. With logarithmic axes, becomes , a family of parallel lines.

Maurice d'Ocagne reached the alignment chart in 1884 by applying a duality to Lalanne's charts. A point-line duality trades points for lines and keeps incidence, so three lines through one point become three points on one line. Each family of straight lines becomes a scale of points, and finding where three lines meet becomes laying a ruler through three marks. The same year Junius Massau generalized anamorphosis to charts built from arbitrary families of straight lines. In 1891 d'Ocagne gathered these methods in a pamphlet and coined the name nomographie for them; his Traité de nomographie followed in 1899.

Figure 2. Anamorphosis, then duality. Logarithmic axes straighten the hyperbolas . The second panel is the dual of the straightened chart, turned 45 degrees with each line sent to the point : the three lines through P become three marks on one ruler. Drag P or either ruler end.

Duality is not the only way to read a sum off three scales. Lallemand's hexagonal charts (1886) put three scales of the same modulus on axes at 120 degrees and read them with a transparent sheet whose three index lines are the diagonals of a regular hexagon. The scales run parallel to the sides of an equilateral triangle, the frame of the ternary diagram in Part 3.

Moving the chart without breaking it

A projective transformation, the map a camera applies when it photographs a flat sheet at an angle, sends straight lines to straight lines. Applied to a nomogram it gives another nomogram for the same equation. In the determinant picture it multiplies the matrix by an invertible 3×3 matrix and rescales rows, which multiplies the determinant by a nonzero number, so zero stays zero. D'Ocagne calls it the transformation homographique, qui conserve l'alignement des points.

The Smith chart

Projective maps have a complex cousin. A Möbius transformation is the projective map of the complex line, and it sends circles and lines to circles and lines. Engineers call it a bilinear transformation, though it has nothing to do with the bilinear interpolation above, which breaks alignments.

Phillip Smith, of the Radio Development Department at Bell Telephone Laboratories, published this chart as a “Transmission Line Calculator” in Electronics in January 1939. In the ordinary resistance-reactance plane he found that the impedances along a line with a fixed standing-wave ratio lie on circles, and so do the points at a fixed distance from the impedance maximum. A change of coordinates made the first family concentric circles and the second straight lines through the centre, Lalanne's move again, and a pivoted arm with a slide stood in for the circles. Mizuhashi Tosaku had published an equivalent chart in Japan in December 1937.

Figure 8. A line is a rotation. Scrub the length: the point turns clockwise, toward the generator, by on its circle of constant , a full turn per half wavelength, and that circle meets the real axis at the SWR. The stub button steps through a single-stub match.

Smith's arm was graduated in , the ratio of minimum to maximum impedance along the line. That is the reciprocal of the standing-wave ratio, so the SWR in Figure 8 is the reciprocal of what his slide would read.
