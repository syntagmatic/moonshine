Nomograms: Calculating with a Straightedge

Part 11 of 11. Three graduated scales and a ruler. Whenever three marks line up, an equation holds, so the chart does the arithmetic.

Part 10 used the shape of a diagram to price a computation. A nomogram is a diagram that does the computation itself. It is a sheet of graduated scales drawn so that any straight line crossing them picks out three values that satisfy one fixed equation. Lay a ruler on two known values and read the third where the ruler crosses the remaining scale.

From crossing curves to straight lines

Graphical tables are older than the ruler. A chart for can be drawn as hyperbolas on ordinary axes, one per value of : find on one axis and on the other, and the curve through their crossing names the product. France's railway law of 1842 created a flood of earthwork estimates, and in 1843 the engineer Léon Lalanne noticed that such curves could be made straight by regraduating the axes. He called this anamorphosis and set it out in the Annales des Ponts et Chaussées in 1846. With logarithmic axes, becomes , a family of parallel lines.

Maurice d'Ocagne reached the alignment chart in 1884 by applying a duality to Lalanne's charts. A point-line duality trades points for lines and keeps incidence, so three lines through one point become three points on one line. Each family of straight lines becomes a scale of points, and finding where three lines meet becomes laying a ruler through three marks. The same year Junius Massau generalized anamorphosis to charts built from arbitrary families of straight lines. In 1891 d'Ocagne gathered these methods in a pamphlet and coined the name nomographie for them; his Traité de nomographie followed in 1899.

Duality is not the only way to read a sum off three scales. Lallemand's hexagonal charts (1886) put three scales of the same modulus on axes at 120 degrees and read them with a transparent sheet whose three index lines are the diagonals of a regular hexagon. The scales run parallel to the sides of an equilateral triangle, the frame of the ternary diagram in Part 3.

Moving the chart without breaking it

A projective transformation, the map a camera applies when it photographs a flat sheet at an angle, sends straight lines to straight lines. Applied to a nomogram it gives another nomogram for the same equation. In the determinant picture it multiplies the matrix by an invertible 3×3 matrix and rescales rows, which multiplies the determinant by a nonzero number, so zero stays zero. D'Ocagne's Traité uses this, the transformation homographique, qui conserve l'alignement des points, to pass from one chart to all the charts equivalent to it.

In medicine, David Grimes dated the heyday of nomograms to roughly 1925 to 1975. Writing in 2008, he found the word spreading again in medical journals and argued that personal computing had removed the need for them, so authors should publish software instead.
