Young Diagrams and Tableaux

The Shape of Partitions — Part 2 of 7

How many ways can you write 5 as a sum of positive integers? Five equals 5, or 4+1, or 3+2, or 3+1+1, or 2+2+1, or 2+1+1+1, or 1+1+1+1+1. Seven ways. These are the partitions of 5, and each one has a shape.

A Young diagram draws a partition as left-justified rows of boxes: the partition (4,2,1) of 7 becomes a staircase of four boxes, then two, then one. This simple picture is one of the most productive objects in mathematics. It encodes the representation theory of the symmetric group, the structure of polynomial rings, and the combinatorics of permutations — all in a grid of boxes.

Partitions and Young Diagrams

A partition of n is a sequence of positive integers that sum to n. The sequence is weakly decreasing: each row is no wider than the one above. The Young diagram is the picture.

Conjugate Partitions

Every partition has a conjugate, obtained by reflecting the Young diagram across its main diagonal — rows become columns and columns become rows. The partition (4,2,1) has conjugate (3,2,1,1): three rows of width 3, 2, 1, 1.

Self-conjugate partitions are their own transpose. The partition (3,2,1) is self-conjugate: its diagram is symmetric about the diagonal. Self-conjugate partitions of n are in bijection with partitions of n into distinct odd parts.

The conjugate mirrors live to the right of the editor above. As you edit the partition, the conjugate updates instantly. Try the self-conjugate preset to see a symmetric shape.

Standard Young Tableaux

A standard Young tableau fills the boxes of a Young diagram with the numbers 1 through n, each used exactly once, so that entries increase along every row (left to right) and down every column (top to bottom).

Switch to Fill mode above and click cells in order. The editor enforces the standard tableau constraint: you can only place the next number in a cell whose left neighbour and upper neighbour (if they exist) are already filled. A valid standard Young tableau has every row and column increasing.

Why do tableaux matter? Standard Young tableaux index a basis for the irreducible representations of the symmetric group. The partition shape determines which representation; the tableau determines which basis vector. Two central results connect them: the RSK correspondence bijects permutations with pairs of tableaux, and the number of standard tableaux of a given shape equals the dimension of the corresponding representation.

The Hook-Length Formula

How many standard Young tableaux fit a given shape? The answer comes from a remarkable formula discovered by Frame, Robinson, and Thrall in 1954.

The hook at cell (i, j) consists of the cell itself, everything to its right in the same row (the arm), and everything below it in the same column (the leg). The hook length is arm + leg + 1.

The hook-length formula is exact. It does not approximate or bound — it counts precisely. For the staircase partition (4,3,2,1) of 10, the formula gives 768 standard tableaux: that is, 768 distinct ways to fill a 4+3+2+1 shape with 1 through 10 such that every row and column increases. Each of those 768 tableaux corresponds to a basis vector in the irreducible representation of S10 indexed by this shape.

Partitions and the Symmetric Group

The deepest reason Young diagrams matter is the representation theory of the symmetric group Sn. Every irreducible representation of Sn over the complex numbers corresponds to a partition of n. The dimension of that representation equals the number of standard Young tableaux of that shape.

For S5, the seven partitions of 5 give seven irreducible representations. The trivial representation corresponds to (5) — a single row — with one standard tableau. The sign representation corresponds to (1,1,1,1,1) — a single column — also with one tableau. The standard representation corresponds to (4,1) with four tableaux, so it is four-dimensional.

What the diagram sees

  • Shape — The partition itself: how a number decomposes into summands.
  • Symmetry — The conjugate partition, visible as the transpose of the diagram.
  • Combinatorial structure — Standard tableaux count the ways to assign a linear order compatible with the shape.
  • Hook lengths — A local invariant at each cell that feeds a global counting formula.
  • Representation theory — Each shape names an irreducible representation of Sn; the number of tableaux gives its dimension.
