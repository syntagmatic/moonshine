Ternary Diagrams: Three Numbers on a Triangle

Part 3 of 10. Three proportions that sum to one fit on a triangle, where one point shows all three at once. The same picture serves soil science, chemistry, genetics and probability.

Part 2 read knot invariants off the crossings of a drawing. A ternary diagram is a coordinate system: each point of a triangle stands for three proportions that sum to one. This part covers how to read one, and why data with that constraint needs its own geometry.

Figure 1. One point, three coordinates. Drag the point, or focus it and use the arrow keys. In the first view the dashed perpendiculars are the three coordinates, and the readout checks that they add up to the altitude. Each preset relabels the corners and computes its own structure: the USDA texture class by point-in-polygon, the heterozygote count against 2pq, the colour of the mix, the entropy.

Why a triangle

The constraint leaves two degrees of freedom, so the data lives on a flat slice of : the triangle with the pure compositions at its corners. Drawing it equilateral turns the coordinates into distances. By Viviani's theorem the three perpendiculars from any interior point add up to the altitude, so with altitude 1 they are the proportions. Each side is where the opposite component is zero, the centre is , and no component gets a preferred axis.

Where the triangle came from

Barycentric coordinates go back to Möbius (1827). Maxwell drew a colour triangle in the 1850s, with three primaries at the corners and their mixtures inside. Gibbs used the triangle for three-component mixtures in On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances (1876–78), and Roozeboom made it the standard canvas for ternary phase diagrams in 1894. Bruno de Finetti brought it to population genetics in 1926, where genotype frequencies trace the Hardy-Weinberg parabola.

The closure problem

A soil sample with 30 g sand, 50 g silt and 20 g clay and another with exactly twice each are different vectors in , but the same composition . The map from amounts to the triangle is called closure:

Dividing by the total ties the parts together, so proportions can correlate when the amounts behind them do not. Karl Pearson warned about this "spurious correlation" of ratios in 1897.

After closure
Figure 2. Correlation from closure alone. 160 samples with three independent lognormal amounts (seeded; the first two have σ = 0.5). After closure p1 and p2 are negatively correlated, because they split what the third part leaves. Widen the third amount's spread and the sign flips: a large x3 shrinks both at once. Hover a point to find its twin.

John Aitchison worked with log-ratios instead: in his 1982 paper and 1986 book he introduced perturbation (multiply componentwise, then close) and powering (raise to a power, then close). In 2001 Pawlowsky-Glahn and Egozcue, and independently Billheimer, Guttorp and Fagan, showed that these operations make the simplex a Euclidean vector space. In its isometric log-ratio (ilr) coordinates perturbation is translation and powering is scaling, so a ruler laid on the triangle measures something else.

The probability simplex

The same triangle is the space of probability distributions over three outcomes. The corners are the certain outcomes and the centre is the uniform distribution. The Entropy preset in Figure 1 draws level sets of on the flat triangle. Near the centre they are nearly circles, because entropy is quadratic there to leading order. Further out they become rounded triangles pointing at the corners, since entropy falls off faster toward the edges than toward the corners. Every edge midpoint has exactly 1 bit, so below that level each contour breaks into three arcs, one around each corner.

Information geometry gives the simplex the Fisher metric, which is (a constant multiple of) the round metric on the positive octant of the unit sphere: take square roots of the coordinates and the triangle becomes a spherical octant. Amari's α-connections then give a one-parameter family of "straight lines". At a straight line is a mixture, straight in the probabilities. At it is a Fisher geodesic, a great-circle arc on the octant. At it is straight in the log-probabilities, then renormalised, which is exactly a straight line of the Aitchison geometry.

Entropy along each path
Figure 4. Three straight lines between the same two distributions. Mixture (solid, α = −1), Fisher geodesic (dashed, α = 0) and exponential (dotted, α = +1). Drag either endpoint, or focus it and use the arrow keys, and scrub t to move along all three at once. Each path is parametrised in its own flat coordinates, so the paths agree only at t = 0 and t = 1. Here the exponential path swings toward the p2 corner and loses the most entropy. Entropy is concave, so along the mixture line it never dips below the straight chord between its end values.

Tie-lines and the lever rule

In a liquid phase diagram some mixtures do not stay as one liquid. Inside a miscibility gap a mixture separates into two phases whose compositions sit at the ends of the tie-line through it. The overall point divides that tie-line in inverse proportion to the amounts of the two phases, which is the lever rule, and it is nothing more than conservation of mass.

Figure 5. Reading a phase split. The dome and its tie-lines come from a formula shaped like a common kind of three-liquid system, not from measured data. Drag the overall mixture M, or focus it and use the arrow keys, and step through the reading. The bars check the result: the two phases, weighted by their amounts, add back up to M. Where the tie-lines shrink to nothing, at the plait point, the two phases become one.

Reading the split needs nothing but a straightedge: the mixture and its two phases lie on one line, and that collinearity is the mass balance. Nomograms, the subject of Part 11, build whole calculators on the same fact, placing scales so that a ruler laid across them solves an equation.

Two other shapes are common. A right-triangle ternary puts two components on Cartesian axes and reads the third off the hypotenuse, giving up Viviani's symmetry for a grid that overlays ordinary plots. Four components need a tetrahedron, usually drawn as a rotatable wireframe; beyond four, projections and slices take over.
