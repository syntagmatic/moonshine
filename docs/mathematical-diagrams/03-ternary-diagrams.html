Ternary Diagrams: Three Numbers on a Triangle

Why a triangle

The constraint turns three numbers into two degrees of freedom. Whatever and happen to be, is forced. So even though the data looks three-dimensional, it lives on a flat 2D slice of : the triangular face of the simplex with vertices at the three pure compositions .

An equilateral triangle is the natural canvas because of Viviani's theorem: from any interior point, the three perpendicular distances to the sides sum to a constant equal to the triangle's altitude. Set that altitude to 1 and the three perpendicular distances are the three coordinates.

There is no preferred axis. Each vertex is the "pure" point for one component (the other two are zero), each side is the locus where the opposite component is zero, and the centre is the uniform mixture . Symmetry across the three components becomes a symmetry of the picture.

Where the triangle came from

Barycentric coordinates go back to Möbius (1827). Maxwell drew a colour triangle in the 1850s, with three primaries at the corners and additive mixtures inside. Gibbs used the triangle for three-component mixtures in On the Equilibrium of Heterogeneous Substances (1876–78), and Roozeboom made it the standard canvas for ternary phase diagrams in 1894. Bruno de Finetti brought it to population genetics in 1926: genotype frequencies trace a parabola, the Hardy-Weinberg locus, which is one of the presets above.

The closure problem

Compositional data has a property that ordinary multivariate methods get wrong. Consider two soil samples:

  • Sample A: 30 g sand, 50 g silt, 20 g clay (100 g total)
  • Sample B: 60 g sand, 100 g silt, 40 g clay (200 g total)

Closure is also what trips up naive analyses. Increasing one component must decrease the others, so the components cannot move independently, and some correlation between them comes from the constraint alone.

John Aitchison's response, in 1982, was to stop treating compositional data as Euclidean. He analysed compositions through log-ratios and introduced perturbation and powering as the natural operations on the simplex. In 2001 Pawlowsky-Glahn and Egozcue, and independently Billheimer, Guttorp and Fagan, showed that these operations with the log-ratio inner product make the simplex a Euclidean vector space, now called the Aitchison geometry. In that geometry the standard tools, such as means, distances and principal components, work as expected on transformed coordinates. The picture is still the triangle, but its metric differs from the one a ruler measures.

The probability simplex

The same triangle is also the space of probability distributions over three outcomes. A point with and all is a probability distribution over three categories, and its space is the simplex . The vertices are the deterministic distributions, the centre is the uniform distribution, and the sides are distributions that put zero mass on one outcome.

Information geometry studies the simplex as a Riemannian manifold equipped with the Fisher metric, under which two distributions are "close" when they assign similar likelihoods to the same observations. The Fisher metric on turns out to be (a constant multiple of) the round metric on the positive octant of the unit sphere in : square-root the coordinates and the simplex maps to a spherical octant.

The iso-entropy contours in the Entropy preset are level sets of the function , drawn on the flat triangle. Their shape comes from the function alone, whatever metric you later put on the simplex. Near the centre they are close to circles, because entropy is quadratic there to leading order. Further out they turn into rounded triangles, since entropy falls off fastest toward the corners.

Amari's α-connections give a one-parameter family of "straight lines" on the simplex. At a straight line is a mixture of two distributions, straight in the probabilities themselves. At it is a geodesic of the Fisher metric, a great-circle arc on the spherical octant. At it is straight in the log-probabilities, then renormalised. Join the same two distributions all three ways and you get three different curves that share only their endpoints.

Variants

Three departures from the equilateral triangle are common enough to recognise. A fourth, contours of a function drawn over the triangle, is what the Entropy preset in the first figure shows.

Right-triangle ternary
Drop the equilateral constraint. Two coordinates become Cartesian and the third is read off the hypotenuse. It loses Viviani's symmetry and gains rectangular grids that overlay easily on other Cartesian plots.
Quaternary tetrahedron
Four components on the 3-simplex, a solid usually drawn as a rotatable wireframe. Beyond four components, projections or slices replace the simplex.
Tie-lines and gaps
In phase diagrams, a miscibility gap is a region the system does not occupy as a single phase. Tie-lines connect the two coexisting phases on its boundary, so the gap shows which mixtures separate.
