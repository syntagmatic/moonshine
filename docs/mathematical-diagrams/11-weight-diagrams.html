Weight Diagrams: Representations Made Visible

Part 11 of 12. A representation of a Lie algebra is an abstract action on a vector space. A weight diagram turns it into a picture on a lattice — dots and multiplicities that you can see, count, and compare.

Every finite-dimensional representation of a semisimple Lie algebra decomposes into simultaneous eigenspaces for the Cartan subalgebra, its maximal commuting part. The eigenvalues are weights, and they live on a lattice in Euclidean space of dimension equal to the rank of the algebra. A weight diagram plots every weight as a dot, sized by its multiplicity (the dimension of the eigenspace).

The symmetry group of the root system acts on the diagram by reflections, the highest weight sits at an extremal vertex, and multiplicities grow toward the interior. Dominant weights

A weight is dominant if all its Dynkin labels are non-negative. The dominant weights are exactly those that appear as highest weights of irreducible representations. In the weight diagram of a given representation, the dominant weights are the ones in the "fundamental Weyl chamber" — the cone defined by the positive simple roots. Every other weight in the diagram is related to a dominant weight by the action of the Weyl group (the reflection symmetry group of the root system), which for is the symmetric group acting by permuting the three coordinates.

The hexagon is the adjoint. The adjoint representation of sl₃ has Dynkin labels (1, 1) and dimension 8. Its weight diagram is a regular hexagon with six outer weights of multiplicity 1 and one central weight of multiplicity 2 — because the Cartan subalgebra is two-dimensional. The six outer weights are the roots; the two interior directions are the Cartan elements. This hexagon is the prototype for all sl₃ weight diagrams.

Multiplicities and Kostka numbers. For sl₃, weights deep in the diagram have higher multiplicity than those at the boundary. These interior multiplicities are Kostka numbers from combinatorics: the number of semistandard Young tableaux of a given shape and content. The bridge between weight diagrams and Young diagrams (see Part 2) is a central connection in representation theory.

Dynkin diagrams determine weight lattices. The Dynkin diagram of the algebra (see Simple Roots and Dynkin Diagrams) encodes the angles between simple roots, which in turn fix the geometry of the weight lattice. Every weight diagram lives on this lattice. The classification of all possible Dynkin diagrams — A, B, C, D, E, F, G — therefore classifies all possible shapes of weight diagrams.

What the diagram sees

  • Dimension — the total number of dots (counting multiplicity) equals the dimension of the representation.
  • Symmetry — the Weyl group acts by reflections on the weight diagram, so every diagram has the symmetry of the root system.
  • Highest weight — the unique extremal dot in the dominant chamber determines the entire representation.
  • Multiplicity — dot sizes reveal the combinatorial depth of the representation, growing toward the interior.
  • Root action — simple roots connect adjacent weights, so the diagram encodes how raising and lowering operators move between eigenspaces.
  • Tensor products — to decompose a tensor product, shift one diagram by each weight of the other and collect the resulting multiplicities.
