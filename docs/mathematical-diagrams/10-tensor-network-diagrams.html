Tensor Network Diagrams: Indices, Contractions, and Cost

Part 10 of 10. Tensor network diagrams draw tensors as nodes and indices as wires. The diagram fixes what a calculation produces, and the order of contraction sets its cost.

Part 9's diagrams were graphs of states joined by moves. A tensor network diagram is itself a formula: each node is a tensor, each wire is an index, and wires joined between two nodes are summed. The order of the sums never changes the answer, but it changes the work, and this part asks by how much.

Nodes and Wires

A node is a tensor and each wire on it is one of its indices. A wire between two nodes is summed, and a wire left open survives into the output, so a diagram with no open wires is a scalar. The matrix product is two nodes joined by one wire. Roger Penrose published the notation in 1971, in a paper on "negative dimensional tensors", after using it privately for years. The winner depends on the numbers on the wires, not only on the picture. Drag b below 20 and contracting B with D first, then absorbing C and A, overtakes the columns; at 15 and below the chain that starts with C and D wins. A planner has to be told the dimensions.

For general networks the cost of the best order is tied to graph structure: Markov and Shi related it to treewidth in their study of quantum-circuit simulation. Software searches exhaustively only for small networks, and uses branch-and-bound, dynamic programming on restricted cases or random-greedy heuristics for larger ones.

Network Families

A matrix product state, or MPS, is a chain: each site tensor has one physical index and shares bond indices with its neighbours. With open ends,

It is the ansatz behind the density-matrix renormalization group in one dimension. A projected entangled pair state (PEPS) puts the same kind of site tensor on a two-dimensional grid. A tree tensor network has no loops, and MERA stacks layers of disentanglers and isometries so that a local observable only sees a narrow causal cone. Figure 3 contracts three of these shapes one tensor at a time: the intermediate's open legs are the bonds crossing the frontier, so with bond dimension χ it has χ to that many entries.

A chain never holds more than one open bond, a tree swept depth first stays near its depth, and the grid swept row by row reaches L + 1.

No order does much better on the grid. At some step the absorbed set holds about half the tensors, and any half of an L × L grid has at least L bonds leaving it, so exact contraction of a two-dimensional PEPS costs time exponential in its width. Practical PEPS calculations use approximate boundary methods and controlled truncation instead.

What a Bond Can Carry

Cut an MPS between sites k and k + 1 and a single bond crosses. Write the state as a matrix with the left sites' values as rows and the right sites' as columns: it factors through that bond, so its rank is at most χ. The matrix's singular values, the Schmidt coefficients, say how much a smaller bond would lose.

2
Figure 4. Eight sites with d = 2; each panel is one cut, marked on the small chain under it. Bars are the Schmidt coefficients (singular values) on a scale from 0 to 1, and grey slots show the most the cut allows, 2min(k, 8−k). Bars right of the dashed line are what a bond of size χ throws away, and ε is the sum of their squares. The two random states are seeded simulated examples. The default MPS has at most three coefficients at any cut, so χ = 2 drops the third at cuts 2 to 6.

A state needs a large bond only where its spectrum is broad. GHZ has two equal coefficients at every cut, however long the chain. The rainbow state, which pairs site j with site 9 − j in a singlet, fills every slot, and so does a random vector: its middle bond would need 2N/2, which is why a random state has no efficient MPS. Keeping the χ largest coefficients at every cut gives an MPS within squared distance 2(ε1 + … + εN−1) of the state (Verstraete and Cirac, 2006). In a PEPS a cut around a region crosses one bond per unit of its boundary, which is the area law that ground states of gapped local Hamiltonians are believed to obey, and that Hastings proved in one dimension.
