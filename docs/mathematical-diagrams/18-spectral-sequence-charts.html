The Core Concept

A spectral sequence is a sequence of bigraded objects , each equipped with a differential . The next page is the homology of the current page:

The picture is useful because every group or module is placed at a lattice point. In a first-quadrant chart, the horizontal coordinate is often a filtration degree , the vertical coordinate is a complementary degree , and the diagonal records total degree. A dot at usually means "there is a nonzero class in ."

This article uses the homological convention common in introductory Serre spectral sequence charts: . With that convention, moves one step left, moves two steps left and one step up, and moves three steps left and two steps up. Cohomological spectral sequences often reverse this direction, and Adams charts commonly use axes labelled by filtration and stem, so the same algebra can look rotated or sheared.

The chart is not the computation by itself. It records where groups live, where differentials are allowed to go, which classes survive, and what filtration pieces remain. The hard work is usually identifying the nonzero differentials and resolving any extension problems after the page.

This is why spectral sequence charts are drawn page by page. The page is not just the page with fewer arrows. It is the homology of the previous page, with the killed source-target pairs removed and with new differentials now possible because the bidegree has changed.

What survives

In the toy chart above, the horizontal arrow removes two classes before the page. The arrow removes a different pair before the page. The arrow removes one more pair before the limit page shown here. The green classes have not been removed by any displayed differential.

Survival has two sides. A class can die because it is the source of a differential, or because it is the target of a differential. Both disappear when we take homology. A class that reaches is a persistent class of the spectral sequence, but it is best to read it as a graded piece of the final object, not automatically as a direct summand.

There are also "forced zeros" in many charts. In a first-quadrant spectral sequence, arrows eventually point outside the quadrant for a fixed bidegree, so sufficiently high differentials into or out of that spot must vanish. This is one reason bounded first-quadrant examples often stabilize after finitely many pages.

Over a field this often behaves like a direct sum of vector spaces. Over abelian groups, the quotients may not determine the whole group uniquely.

Try the extension problem: Keep the same two graded pieces on one total-degree diagonal, then change only how the filtration is glued back together.

Extension data. The two visible quotients are unchanged in the first two modes. What changes is the middle group whose filtration has those quotients.

This is why the last page of a chart is sometimes a beginning rather than the end. It tells you which graded pieces remain and where they sit along each total-degree diagonal. The final answer may still require deciding how those pieces fit together inside the filtered object.
