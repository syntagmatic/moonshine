Thickening a graph

A ribbon graph, or fatgraph, is a finite graph with a cyclic order on the half-edges at each vertex. To thicken it, make each vertex a small oriented disk and each edge a band, glued to the disks in the prescribed order. The result is an oriented surface with boundary that deformation retracts onto the graph. The graph alone does not fix it: the theta graph above becomes a pair of pants or a once-punctured torus depending only on the orders.

Label the half-edges, or darts. Pairing the two darts of each edge is a fixed-point-free involution , and the cyclic orders, read counterclockwise, form a second permutation . A graph already drawn on an oriented surface gets from the orientation. A ribbon graph runs the other way and builds the surface from . A flipped vertex shows up in the plane as two bands that cross; in the surface they simply pass each other.

Maps, dessins and metrics

A ribbon graph is the same data as a map: a graph embedded in an oriented surface with disk faces, with as its rotation system. Dessins d'enfants are bipartite maps, studied together with a Belyi map. Putting a new vertex in the middle of every edge turns any ribbon graph into a clean dessin, which is why the two are usually studied side by side.

Metric ribbon graphs add a positive length to each edge. Strebel's theorem on quadratic differentials turns them into a model of moduli space: given a Riemann surface of genus g with n ≥ 1 punctures and a positive number for each puncture, there is exactly one metric ribbon graph on it, every vertex of valence at least three, whose boundary cycles surround the punctures with those perimeters. A sphere with three punctures has no moduli at all, so the three perimeters alone must decide the graph. Figure 3 solves for it. The triangle is the ternary diagram of Part 3, plotting the perimeters as shares of their total.
