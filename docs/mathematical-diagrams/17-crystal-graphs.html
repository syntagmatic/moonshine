Core Concept

A crystal graph is a diagram attached to a crystal basis. In Kashiwara's construction, one starts with a representation of a quantum group . The parameter is not simply substituted by zero in the algebra. Instead, a suitable lattice and basis have a well-behaved limit as . The remaining data is discrete enough to draw.

The vertices are basis elements in that limit. Each vertex has a weight. For every simple root index , there are partial maps , usually called Kashiwara operators. When , the graph has a directed edge of color from to . The operator goes back along the same edge.

So the diagram is not just a weight diagram with extra arrows. A weight diagram records where weight spaces sit. A crystal graph tries to remember how the simple-root operators move individual basis elements between those weights. In representations with multiplicities, this distinction matters: several vertices can have the same weight but different incoming and outgoing arrows.

A useful mental model. A crystal graph is a deterministic colored graph. For a fixed color , each vertex has at most one outgoing edge and at most one incoming edge. The representation has become a collection of operator strings that still remember weights.

In higher rank, every simple root gets its own color. A vertex may have a red arrow and a green arrow, for example. The local patterns among those colors encode the Cartan matrix, so the crystal is constrained by the same root-system geometry that appeared in Dynkin and weight diagrams.

Highest and lowest weights

A highest-weight crystal has a distinguished vertex killed by all raising operators:

From that vertex, the rest of the connected crystal is generated by lowering operators. For an integrable highest-weight module, this graph is finite in finite type and has a lowest-weight vertex when the representation is finite-dimensional. The lowest vertex is killed by all lowering operators. In the string, highest and lowest are the two ends of the line.

The phrase "highest" depends on a choice of positive roots. Once that choice is fixed, the highest weight determines the irreducible finite-dimensional representation. The crystal graph is a finer object than the highest weight alone because it gives a combinatorial model for the basis and for tensor-product behavior.

One reason crystals are useful is that tensor products can be handled with explicit rules on vertices. That does not make tensor products trivial, but it moves part of the calculation into graph combinatorics. This was already visible in Kashiwara's early papers: the crystal graph of a tensor product can be described directly from the factors.

Tableau connection

In type A, many highest-weight crystals can be realized by semistandard Young tableaux. This is the bridge to the earlier Young-diagram page. The shape records the highest weight, while the entries record a basis element. The weight of a tableau is its content, meaning how many 1s, 2s, 3s, and so on appear.

The usual type A rule reads a tableau as a word, cancels adjacent pairs according to a signature convention, and then changes the appropriate uncancelled letter. The exact reading order varies by author, so the safest statement is structural: the operators move through semistandard tableaux while preserving the crystal axioms. Kashiwara and Nakashima gave explicit tableau models for the classical types, with type A matching the familiar semistandard tableaux.

This connection explains why crystal graphs show up in combinatorics. They organize tableaux, words, Littlewood-Richardson behavior, and character calculations into the same operator picture used for representations.

Concept Summary

  • Vertices are crystal-basis elements. They are basis elements that survive a controlled limit of a quantum-group representation.
  • Edges are Kashiwara operators. An -colored edge records ; the reverse move is .
  • Weights remain visible. Applying subtracts the simple root ; applying adds it back.
  • Highest-weight crystals start from one source. A highest vertex is killed by all raising operators, and lowering operators generate the connected crystal.
  • Type A crystals have tableau models. Semistandard tableaux give concrete vertices, with the crystal operators implemented by signature rules on entries.
  • The graph is humble but rich. It does not contain every vector-space detail of the representation, but it preserves enough structure to support character, tensor-product, and branching calculations.
