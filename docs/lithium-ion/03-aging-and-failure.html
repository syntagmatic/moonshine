Aging and Failure: The Gradual Burn and the Sudden Kill

Most cells die slowly, as a passivation layer thickens on the anode like √t. A few die fast, when lithium plates out as metallic dendrites and crosses the separator. The same cell can suffer both.

Averaged over a fleet, a cell's decline looks smooth. Capacity drifts down a few percent per year, the impedance semicircle of Part 4 widens, and charging takes a little longer. That gradual decline comes mostly from the solid-electrolyte interphase thickening, at a rate that follows the Arrhenius law.

A second mode waits outside the smooth plot. Push too much current at too low a temperature, or into a cell whose SEI has stiffened, and the anode surface potential crosses zero versus Li/Li⁺. Lithium stops intercalating and starts plating. The deposits grow unstably, because sharp tips collect more flux than flat regions, and when one tip reaches the separator the cell shorts in milliseconds.

SEI: the slow burn

Below roughly 0.8 V versus Li/Li⁺ (most of a charged cell's operating window), the carbonate electrolyte (EC, DMC, DEC) is not thermodynamically stable against graphite. Its solvents reduce on the carbon surface and precipitate with Li⁺ as an insoluble crust: the solid-electrolyte interphase. In a mature cell it is a few to a few tens of nanometres thick and chemically layered, inorganic near the electrode (LiF from LiPF₆ reduction, Li₂CO₃, Li₂O), organic near the electrolyte (lithium alkyl carbonates, ROCO₂Li, polymers). The cell needs it, since without it the electrolyte would keep reducing and the cell would die in hours. The layer conducts Li⁺ but blocks the electrons that would drive further reduction, and every commercial cell grows it deliberately during formation. It is also the main vehicle of calendar aging, the capacity and resistance drift that happens whether or not you use the cell.

The growth follows √t because a reactant, an electron leaking out of the graphite or a solvent molecule diffusing in, must cross the existing layer of thickness δ to reach the reaction front. Its flux scales as 1/δ, so the layer grows at that rate:

This is Wagner's parabolic law, the same rule that governs high-temperature oxidation of metals and the thick-oxide limit of silicon oxidation in the Deal–Grove model. Any passivation layer that grows by diffusion through itself obeys δ ∝ √t. The prefactor k(T) is a diffusion coefficient scaled by reactant concentration, sharply Arrhenius in temperature (Ea for Li transport through SEI is reported in the 40–60 kJ/mol range) and sensitive to state of charge (higher SOC, lower anode potential, more driving force for reduction).

Calendar versus cycle

Real aging mixes calendar and cycle fade. Each day the cell sits, SEI grows as √t. Each cycle cracks graphite particles and exposes fresh surface, and a new patch of SEI grows to cover it. Cycling also adds mechanical damage such as binder fatigue and particles that lose electrical contact. Figure 2 lumps all of this into an N0.7 power law beside the √t calendar term. Cycle fade is steeply nonlinear in depth of discharge: a cell that survives ~500 full-depth cycles typically tolerates several thousand at 30% DOD.

Which channel dominates depends on use. A phone at body temperature near full charge is calendar-dominated: it sits hot at 100% SOC, accruing SEI around the clock. A grid battery doing hundreds of full-depth cycles at room temperature is cycle-dominated. A daily-driven EV is a mix.

Temperature matters a great deal. At Ea ≈ 50 kJ/mol, going from 25 to 35 °C roughly doubles k, and going to 45 °C multiplies it by about 4. Thickness goes as √k, so fade at any given time is only about 2× the 25 °C figure, but since the time to reach a given thickness scales as 1/k, the cell reaches any fixed damage threshold about four times sooner. A phone sits near full charge at 30 to 35 °C in a pocket, and an EV in a Phoenix garage sits at 40 to 45 °C all summer. By this arithmetic, most of their damage happens while they sit.

Dendrites: the fast kill

SEI growth gives months of warning in capacity and impedance data. Dendrites give none. They nucleate when the cell is pushed too hard at the wrong moment, and once one bridges the separator the cell is gone.

Charging pushes Li⁺ through the electrolyte, across the SEI, and into graphite galleries. The voltage cost is the charge-transfer overpotential ηct. When η grows large enough that the anode surface potential crosses zero versus Li/Li⁺, a shortcut opens: reduce Li⁺ to metallic lithium on the outside of the particle. Cold makes this worse in two ways. The exchange current density i0 shrinks, which raises η, and the solid-state diffusivity Ds shrinks too, so the surface concentration saturates faster and pulls Ugraphite down to meet it. Fast charging a cold cell is the classic way to plate lithium.

Once a microscopic protrusion of metallic Li forms, geometry takes over. Li⁺ concentration obeys Laplace's equation ∇²c = 0 at steady state, and gradients concentrate at sharp curvature. A bump collects more flux than a flat region, the flux deposits more Li at the bump, and the bump grows. This is the Mullins–Sekerka instability: a flat interface is unstable to every perturbation longer than a cutoff wavelength, and only surface tension stabilises the shorter ones. The result is a self-similar branching morphology with fractal dimension

for 2-D off-lattice clusters, the Witten–Sander result, consistent with the branched morphology seen in cryo-EM of plated lithium. Diffusion-limited aggregation is a cartoon of Mullins–Sekerka. Random walkers are released one at a time from a distant source, and each one sticks when it lands next to the cluster. The walkers stand in for the diffusion field, since their statistics solve Laplace's equation. The cluster absorbs flux fastest at its tips, so bumps grow into arms and the arms sprout bumps of their own. A downward bias, standing in for the applied field, gives straight, forest-like dendrites, and without bias they look mossy. Both morphologies appear in real cells.

At a 60 px gap it happens quickly.

The two failures are coupled. A thicker SEI and higher interface impedance (Part 4) push ηct up, higher η drives the anode surface potential down, and a lower surface potential brings on plating sooner. So the gradual failure often sets up the sudden one: an aged cell has a plating threshold that has moved closer. A charge protocol that stays clear of plating on a new cell can cross the threshold on the same cell years later, on a cold morning, with no change in the charger.

The full decay picture

Together, the two channels give a typical cell hundreds of cycles of gradual fade, with a slope set by T, SOC and use pattern. Some cells hit a "capacity knee", where fade accelerates because mechanical cracking exposes fresh graphite that grows its own SEI. And a cold fast charge can cause a short that the capacity curve gave no warning of. When a shorted cell vents into its neighbours, the problem becomes the pack's (Part 5).

Mitigation follows mechanism. Calendar fade is slowed by storing cells cooler and at lower SOC, which is why EVs are often set to stop at 70% and phones pause at 80% on long plug-ins. Cycle fade is slowed by narrower DOD windows and moderate C-rates. Plating is prevented by the BMS, which measures current and temperature, estimates η and holds the charge current below the plating limit. That is why a phone that charges in thirty minutes at room temperature can take two hours in a cold car, and why chargers derate sharply below 0 °C.
