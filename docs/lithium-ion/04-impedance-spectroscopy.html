Impedance Spectroscopy: The Cell in the Frequency Domain

Inject a small sinusoidal current at angular frequency ω, read back the voltage, and compute Z(ω) = V(ω)/I(ω) over eight decades. The losses and aging from the earlier parts each leave a mark on the resulting curve.

One measurement, every timescale

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) sorts a cell's processes by timescale. Ohmic conduction responds at the highest frequencies, interfacial kinetics around milliseconds, and mass transport over seconds and longer. All of it is read through the two terminals of a sealed cell.

The Randles circuit, read left to right

The standard lumped model of one interface is Randles: a series Rs (electrolyte and contact ohmic losses, the IR term of Part 2), then two paths in parallel. One is the double-layer capacitance Cdl, which charges without any lithium crossing the interface. The other is the faradaic path, where lithium does cross: charge-transfer resistance Rct (Butler-Volmer, Part 2) in series with a Warburg element W for semi-infinite diffusion. The Warburg element belongs in that branch because diffusion limits only the current that reacts.

A Nyquist plot puts Z'(ω) on the horizontal axis and −Z''(ω) on the vertical, so capacitive impedances sit above the line. Swept from high to low frequency, the Randles circuit traces three features:

  • The high-frequency intercept is Rs. Every capacitor has become a short, and only the series ohmic path remains.
  • A semicircle of diameter Rct, with its apex at ωpeak = 1/(Rct·Cdl). The RC time constant measures the Butler-Volmer kinetics of Part 2.
  • A 45° tail at low frequency from the Warburg element. ZW = σW/√ω · (1 − j), so Re = −Im and the slope is exactly one. That follows from Fick's law in a half-space, with no fit parameter. Extrapolated back, the tail hits the real axis at Rs + Rct − 2σW²Cdl.

Real cells depart from this in two main ways. A depressed arc, flattened with its centre below the real axis, marks a heterogeneous interface: Cdl is replaced by a constant-phase element with ZCPE = 1/(Q(jω)φ), where φCPE = 1 is ideal and φCPE ≈ 0.8 is typical. A turn-up at very low f, where the 45° line bends toward vertical, is finite-length diffusion: the Li+ gradient has reached the particle centre at ω ≈ D/a² (a = particle radius), and W behaves like a capacitor rather than a half-space.

A Randles fit to a two-terminal cell also lumps anode and cathode together. Real lithium-ion spectra often show two arcs, a small one at high frequency from the graphite SEI and a larger one at mid frequency from charge transfer at the cathode, which this model merges into one. Separating them needs a reference electrode.

Aging as a deforming spectrum

Each Randles element drifts as a cell ages:

  • Rs rises as SEI formation consumes electrolyte and thermal cycling degrades contacts (Part 3). The high-f intercept slides right.
  • Rct rises because Li+ must cross a thickening SEI before the charge-transfer step, and active surface area drops as particles passivate (Part 3). The semicircle swells.
  • σW rises as particle cracking lengthens diffusion paths and lost lithium inventory steepens the concentration gradient (Part 2, Part 3). The low-f tail stands taller.

A periodic impedance sweep therefore works as an aging log, read through the terminals without opening the cell.

Full spectra come from a potentiostat on a bench: in the lab, in end-of-line testing, or when a used pack is graded for second life. A vehicle BMS rarely runs a sweep. It watches the voltage step when current changes, which gives Rs plus part of the arc, and tracks that number over months. Onboard impedance measurement is an active research area, and the spectrum above is what it is trying to recover.
