Aging and Failure: The Gradual Burn and the Sudden Kill

Two decay curves, two personalities. Most cells die the slow way, a passivation layer creeping along the anode as √t. A few die the fast way, when lithium plates out as metallic dendrites and crosses the separator in milliseconds. Why the same cell can suffer both.

A cell's end-of-life looks smooth in a fleet-averaged plot: capacity drifts down a few percent per year, the Nyquist semicircle widens, the charger takes a little longer. That is the gradual channel, the solid-electrolyte interphase thickening under its own thermodynamics. Inevitable, predictable, Arrhenius.

A second mode waits outside the smooth plot. Push too much current at too low a temperature, or into a cell whose SEI has already stiffened, and the anode surface potential crosses zero versus Li/Li⁺. Lithium stops intercalating and starts plating. Deposits grow as Laplacian instabilities, sharp tips concentrate flux, sharp gets sharper, and when one tip reaches the separator the cell shorts in milliseconds. The same physics that sculpts frost on a window ends the cell's life.

SEI: the slow burn

Below roughly 0.8 V versus Li/Li⁺ (most of a charged cell's operating window), the carbonate electrolyte (EC, DMC, DEC) is not thermodynamically stable against graphite. Its solvents reduce on the carbon surface and precipitate with Li⁺ as an insoluble crust: the solid-electrolyte interphase. In a mature cell it is tens to a few hundred nanometres thick and chemically layered, inorganic near the electrode (LiF from LiPF₆ reduction, Li₂CO₃, Li₂O), organic near the electrolyte (lithium alkyl carbonates, ROCO₂Li, polymers). It is a feature, not a bug: without it the electrolyte would keep reducing and the cell would die in hours. The layer passivates, ionically conductive enough to shuttle Li⁺, electronically insulating enough to block the electrons that would drive further reduction. Every commercial cell grows it deliberately during formation. It is also the main vehicle of calendar aging, the capacity and resistance drift that happens whether or not you use the cell.

Why √t? A reactant, an electron leaking out of the graphite, or a solvent molecule diffusing in, must cross an existing layer of thickness δ to reach the reaction front. Its flux scales as 1/δ, so the layer grows at that rate:

This is Wagner parabolic oxidation, the same rule that governs iron rusting and silicon oxidation in the Deal–Grove thin-oxide limit. Any passivation layer that grows by diffusion through itself obeys δ ∝ √t. The prefactor k(T) is a diffusion coefficient scaled by reactant concentration, sharply Arrhenius in temperature (Ea for Li transport through SEI is reported in the 40–60 kJ/mol range) and sensitive to state of charge (higher SOC, lower anode potential, more driving force for reduction).

Calendar versus cycle

Real aging is never pure calendar or pure cycle. Each day the cell sits, SEI grows as √t. Each cycle cracks graphite particles, exposes fresh basal plane, and a new patch of SEI grows to cover it, cycling accelerates the same chemistry running in the background, and adds genuinely mechanical terms (binder fatigue, particle isolation) on top. We collapse the cycle bundle into an N0.7 power law and pit it against the √t calendar term. Cycle fade is steeply nonlinear in depth of discharge: a cell that survives ~500 full-depth cycles typically tolerates several thousand at 30% DOD.

Which channel dominates depends on use. A phone at body temperature near full charge is calendar-dominated, it sits, hot, at 100% SOC, accruing SEI around the clock. A grid battery doing hundreds of full-depth cycles at room temperature is cycle-dominated. A daily-driven EV at moderate SOC is a mix. Arrhenius has teeth. At Ea ≈ 50 kJ/mol, 25 → 35 °C roughly doubles k; 25 → 45 °C is about 4×. Thickness goes as √k, so fade at 45 °C is only ~2× the 25 °C number, but it arrives at the damage threshold twice as fast. A phone sits near full charge at 30–35 °C in a pocket; a Phoenix-garage EV sees 40–45 °C baseline all summer. Both are calendar-fade factories by this math alone, the dominant damage is done while they sit.

Dendrites: the fast kill

SEI growth is gradual, capacity fade is a smooth curve, the impedance spectrum evolves cycle by cycle, fleet telemetry has months to notice. Dendrites are the opposite. They nucleate when you push too hard at the wrong moment, and once one bridges the separator the cell is gone in milliseconds. The difference is kind, not degree.

Charging pushes Li⁺ through the electrolyte, across the SEI, and into graphite galleries. That path has a voltage cost, the charge-transfer overpotential ηct. When η grows large enough that the anode surface potential versus Li/Li⁺ crosses zero, a shortcut opens: reduce Li⁺ directly to metallic lithium on the outside of the particle, bypassing the lattice. Cold compounds the problem twice, exchange current density i0 shrinks (Arrhenius), which raises η; solid-state diffusivity Ds also shrinks, so surface concentration saturates faster and pulls Ugraphite down to meet it. Fast charge + cold cell is the classic plating cocktail.

Once a microscopic protrusion of metallic Li forms on the surface, geometry takes over. Li⁺ concentration in the electrolyte obeys Laplace's equation ∇²c = 0 at steady state, and Laplace solutions have no interior maxima, gradients concentrate at sharp curvature. A bump collects more flux than a flat region; that flux deposits more Li at the bump; the bump grows. This is the Mullins–Sekerka instability: every wavelength of a flat interface is unstable, short wavelengths limited only by surface tension. The result is a self-similar branching morphology, fractal in the statistical sense, with dimension

for 2-D off-lattice clusters, the Witten–Sander result, consistent with the branched morphology seen in cryo-EM of plated lithium. Diffusion-limited aggregation is Mullins–Sekerka as a cartoon: release random walkers one at a time from a distant source; when one lands next to the cluster, it sticks. The walkers are the diffusion field, their trajectories solve Laplace statistically. The cluster absorbs flux fastest at its tips, so bumps become arms, arms sprout bumps, bumps sprout sub-arms. The cartoon even picks up the external field: a downward bias yields straight, forest-like dendrites; without bias they look mossy and isotropic. Both morphologies appear in real cells.

The two failures couple. SEI quality and interface impedance (Part 7) both push ηct up. Higher η drives the anode surface potential down. Lower surface potential hastens plating. Catastrophic failure is often gated by the gradual one: an aged cell is not a fresh cell with less charge, it is a cell whose plating threshold has moved closer. A new pack tolerates 3C charging at 0 °C; after two years of calendar fade the same protocol plates on the first cold morning.

The full decay picture

Put the two channels together and a typical cell's life looks like this. Hundreds of cycles of gradual fade, slope set by T, SOC, and use pattern. Occasionally a "capacity knee," a sudden inflection where fade accelerates because mechanical cracking opens fresh graphite that grows its own SEI at the t = 0 part of the √t curve. Sometimes, if the knee is followed by a cold fast-charge or the cell is already near its plating envelope, a sudden short, no warning in the capacity curve, milliseconds from intact to dead. Pack-level consequences (Part 2's thermal runaway) follow when one shorted cell vents into its neighbours.

Mitigation follows mechanism. Calendar fade is attacked by storing cooler and at lower SOC, why EVs park at 70% and phones throttle past 80% on long plug-ins. Cycle fade is attacked by narrower DOD windows and moderate C-rates. Plating is attacked by the BMS: measure current and temperature, estimate η, refuse to push past the budget. That is why your phone charges in thirty minutes at room temperature but two hours in a cold car, and why every modern BMS derates aggressively below 0 °C.

Takeaways

  • Gradual: SEI grows by diffusion through itself, δ(t) = √(k(T) · t). Arrhenius in T, sensitive to SOC.
  • Calendar vs cycle: both are real; the mix is set by use. Phones die calendar, grid batteries die cycle, EVs are in between.
  • Catastrophic: plating onsets when graphite surface potential crosses 0 V vs Li/Li⁺. Dendrites grow as Laplacian instabilities, Df ≈ 1.71 in 2D.
  • Coupled: aged SEI and elevated Rct raise η, lowering the plating threshold. Gradual failure gates catastrophic failure.
