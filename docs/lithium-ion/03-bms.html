Battery Management Systems (BMS)

A small computer keeps the pack alive: it estimates state of charge the chemistry won't reveal, equalizes drifting cells, and trips protection circuits before things catch fire.

A pack is only as good as its worst cell, every 18650 leaves the line slightly different, and left alone those differences compound. The battery management system, or BMS, is what keeps them from destroying the pack. It is the least glamorous component of most battery products, and so the first thing a budget manufacturer cuts. Customers don't notice a BMS until it fails.

A BMS has three jobs. Estimate: SOC, SOH, resistance, temperature. Maintain: balance drifted cells, request heating or cooling, gate charge and discharge. Protect: cut off before any cell crosses an absolute limit. A phone BMS is firmware on a power-management IC. An EV BMS is a product of its own: ISO-26262 certification, redundant voltage measurement, tens of thousands of lines of code.

Estimating where you are

No sensor reads state of charge directly; the BMS infers it. Two easy strategies exist, both broken. Voltage lookup: measure terminal voltage, consult a stored V(x) curve, read off SOC. This works for NMC and alkaline. It fails catastrophically on LFP, whose open-circuit voltage is nearly flat from 20% to 90%: no slope to invert, so a millivolt of noise becomes tens of percent of SOC error. LFP piles on 10–30 mV of OCV hysteresis, the same 3.3 V means one SOC after a charge, another after a discharge.

Coulomb counting (integrate current from a known starting point) is exact if your current sensor is exact and you never lose power. Neither holds. A 0.5% sensor bias drifts into several percent of SOC error over a day, and every reboot wipes the integrator. Real BMSes blend: trust voltage where its slope is steep, coulomb counting where it isn't, reconcile at known endpoints, a full charge pegs the integrator to 100%. The automotive standard is an extended Kalman filter (EKF), which formalizes the blend as an optimal recursive estimator weighted by each signal's noise; unscented (UKF) and H-infinity variants buy accuracy at higher compute.

SOH (state of health). Alongside SOC, the BMS tracks how much capacity the cell has lost relative to new. SOH updates on the timescale of weeks, not seconds, by comparing expected and measured voltage trajectories across known endpoints. A cell at 80% SOH delivers 80% of its original capacity.

Keeping cells in step

Once cells drift, something has to pull them back to a common SOC. Series cells share the same current, so all gain or lose the same coulombs per second; but capacities differ by a few percent, so SOCs diverge cycle by cycle. The highest cell hits Vmax first on charge, forcing the pack to stop; the lowest hits Vmin first on discharge. An unbalanced pack loses capacity on both ends. Thermal gradients amplify the drift: cells in the middle of a pack run hotter than edges, and cells near the coolant inlet cooler than those near the outlet; neighbors age unevenly just from where they sit.

Passive balancing is universal and crude. Each cell gets a switched parallel resistor; near top-of-charge, the BMS bleeds excess off the highest cells as heat. Typical currents are 50–200 mA, so rebalancing a drifted pack takes many hours and the energy is wasted. One MOSFET plus one resistor per cell. Consumer gear gets passive. Active balancing transfers charge from high to low instead of burning it: switched-capacitor, inductor, or flyback-transformer networks move energy sideways at 80–95% round-trip efficiency, an order of magnitude faster (minutes, not hours), significantly more complex. EVs and grid installations use it because at those energies every percent matters and pack life extends enough to pay for the hardware.

The protection boundary

Every cell has a safe operating envelope in (V, I, T). Inside, it charges and discharges normally; outside, damage begins: lithium plates the anode instead of intercalating, electrolyte decomposes on the cathode, the cell self-heats, and past ~150 °C runaway begins. The envelope is nested: green safe, amber warn (BMS derates), red fault (BMS cuts off), deep-red hard fault (main contactor opens). Concrete per-cell limits: NMC 2.7–4.2 V (survives 2.5–4.25 V, damaging), LFP 2.5–3.65 V. Temperature: charge above 0 °C (below, lithium plates); work under ~45 °C to slow aging and well under 60 °C to stay off the runaway on-ramp.

Envelope width depends on chemistry and pack design. A liquid-cooled EV holds the pack near 25 °C under a 300 kW fast-charge and gets a wide envelope. A flashlight has no cooling but gets away with a narrow one because its currents are low. A grid battery sits between; massive thermal mass buys response time, and cell count guarantees a few always drift.

The envelope is a projection. Modern EV BMSes monitor dozens of signals beyond (V, I, T): per-cell voltage, per-module temperature, isolation resistance to chassis, current-sensor plausibility, contactor welded-shut state, disagreement between redundant voltage-measurement ICs. The three-dimensional zone here is a readable projection of a higher-dimensional safe set. ISO-26262 analysis asks, of every single-point failure in that space, whether the BMS can still refuse to hurt anyone.
