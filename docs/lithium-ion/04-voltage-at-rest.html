The Voltage at Rest

The voltage you read at rest, what sets it, what shapes it takes, and why some shapes are easier to read back than others.

Start at the simplest measurement: the voltage on a multimeter when nothing is connected and everything has stopped moving. The open-circuit voltage, OCV, is a thermodynamic statement the cell makes about itself, the baseline from which loss after loss will be subtracted once current flows.

OCV is the difference in lithium's chemical potential between the two electrodes, divided by the Faraday constant; its value is set by chemistry; its shape versus state-of-charge decides whether a BMS can recover charge state from a voltmeter. Below we watch a layered host fill one gallery at a time, write V(x) as the derivative of a free energy, and land on dV/dx, the quantity that actually matters to the software.

A layered host

Graphite is a stack of sp² carbon honeycombs, covalently bonded in-plane and held to their neighbours above and below only by van der Waals attraction between π-clouds. The few-ångström gap between adjacent sheets is a gallery. On charge, Li⁺ squeezes from the electrolyte into those galleries; the carbon sheets themselves are off-limits. Fully lithiated graphite is LiC₆, one lithium per six carbons, all parked in galleries.

Filling does not proceed uniformly. At low lithium content, Li occupies every n-th gallery and fills it completely, leaving the rest empty. The state with every n-th gallery full is stage n; a charging electrode walks empty (dilute) → stage 4 → stage 3 → stage 2 → stage 1 (LiC₆). Opening a gallery costs a fixed elastic energy (the two bounding sheets must push apart by about 10%, straining their van der Waals bonds) and that cost scales with the number of opened galleries, not how full they are. Once open, each additional Li is nearly free. The system therefore minimises opened galleries; entropy pushes the other way, but elasticity wins until the current stage saturates.

First-order, not continuous. Because opening costs a fixed energy and filling an open gallery is cheap, every gallery is either fully open or fully closed, with no intermediate. Stage-to-stage transitions are first-order phase transitions, and that discreteness is what makes voltage plateaus possible.

From lattice to voltage

OCV is a derivative of the host's Gibbs free energy. The chemical potential of lithium in the host is μ(x) = ∂G/∂x. Moving a mole of Li from a reference electrode (at μ_ref) into the host at composition x performs reversible work (μ − μ_ref) per mole; for a single-electron transfer the cell does −FV of electrical work per mole. Setting the two equal:

For a half-cell against lithium metal, μ_ref = μ_Li°, and V(x) encodes the shape of G(x). A convex G with a smooth minimum gives a smoothly sloping V. But when G(x) has two competing wells (say stage 2 and stage 1) the equilibrium free energy between them is not the curve itself but the common tangent joining the wells. Between the tangent points the system is a mechanical mixture: adding lithium converts one phase to the other while μ, the slope of G, stays fixed. Constant slope of G means constant V. Every plateau in a half-cell curve is a phase-coexistence region, and the plateau voltage is the tangent's slope.

This is the Gibbs common-tangent construction, the thermodynamic machinery linking Figure 1's staging lattice to the flat stretches of a slow charge curve.

The cathode menu

Graphite is the standard anode. The cathode, where nearly every commercial chemistry decision is made, can go either way, phase-separating like graphite or forming a continuous solid solution. Two chemistries bracket the range: LFP (lithium iron phosphate, LiFePO₄) phase-separates into one long flat plateau near 3.42 V spanning almost its entire capacity; NMC (nickel-manganese-cobalt oxide) is a solid solution whose voltage slopes smoothly from ~4.2 V down to ~3.4 V on discharge. Why the shape matters

At rest the BMS sees one observable, a voltmeter reading, and wants the hidden state x. Given a known V(x), it computes x = V⁻¹(V), an inverse that is numerically well-behaved only where the forward map is strictly monotonic with non-vanishing slope. SOC sensitivity to a millivolt of voltage is the reciprocal of that slope:

Flat plateau, near-zero slope, near-infinite reciprocal. A plateau is an informational blackout on the voltage channel: a millivolt of noise projects to tens of percent of SOC. This is not a BMS bug; it is a thermodynamic property of the chemistry, fixed by the common-tangent construction above.

Figure 3 plots dV/dx for all three materials in mV per %SOC. The shaded band is |dV/dx| < 5 mV/%, the threshold below which a realistic ±5 mV sensor noise balloons to ≥1 % SOC uncertainty per sample. LFP sits inside the band across almost its entire useful SOC range; NMC stays safely outside; graphite's steep contribution dominates the full-cell sensitivity away from its plateaus.

A voltage-based SOC estimator computes V⁻¹ of the green curve; on the plateau that inverse does not exist in any numerically useful sense, and no filter tuning recovers information the thermodynamics does not encode. This is why NMC packs track truth cleanly from voltage alone, while LFP packs must lean entirely on coulomb counting.

The LFP tradeoff, named. The same flatness that pays for LFP's industry-leading safety and cycle life (olivine is thermally stable and its lattice barely deforms across charge and discharge) destroys voltage-based SOC observability. LFP BMS teams earn their paychecks on coulomb counting, on dV/dt during relaxation, on temperature compensation, and on scheduled full discharges to re-anchor the integrator. NMC packs get SOC almost for free from voltage; LFP packs pay for it in software.
