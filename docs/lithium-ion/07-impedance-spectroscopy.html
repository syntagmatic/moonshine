Impedance Spectroscopy: The Cell in the Frequency Domain

Inject a small sinusoidal current at angular frequency ω, read back the voltage, compute Z(ω) = V(ω)/I(ω), sweep eight decades. Every feature of the resulting Nyquist curve is an echo of a chapter: Rs from pack wiring, a semicircle from Butler-Volmer, a 45° tail from solid-state diffusion, drift over life from SEI growth. One measurement, through a sealed case, that re-reads the whole series.

One measurement, every timescale

EIS puts the whole cell onto one plot. The frequency sweep separates physics by timescale: microseconds for ohmic conduction, milliseconds for interfacial kinetics, seconds for mass transport. The terminals carry the whole story without optical access or a sampling port.

The Randles circuit, read left to right

The standard lumped model of one interface is Randles: a series Rs (electrolyte and contact ohmic losses, Part 2), a parallel block of charge-transfer Rct (Butler-Volmer, Part 5) and double-layer Cdl, and a Warburg element W for semi-infinite diffusion (Part 5):

A Nyquist plot puts Z'(ω) on the horizontal axis and −Z''(ω) on the vertical; the negated imaginary axis is historical but convenient, since capacitive impedances then sit above the line. Swept high-f to low-f, Randles traces exactly three features, each with a chapter:

  • High-f intercept = Rs. Every capacitor has collapsed to a short; only the series ohmic path remains (Part 2).
  • Semicircle, diameter Rct, apex at ωpeak = 1/(Rct·Cdl). The RC time constant is the kinetic fingerprint of Butler-Volmer (Part 5).
  • 45° tail at low f = Warburg. ZW = σW/√ω · (1 − j), so Re = −Im and the slope is exactly one, not a fit parameter, just Fick's law in a half-space (Part 5). Extrapolated back, the tail hits the real axis at Rs + Rct − 2σW²Cdl.

Two departures from this idealisation dominate real cells. A depressed arc (flattened, its centre below the real axis) marks a heterogeneous interface; Cdl is replaced by a constant-phase element with ZCPE = 1/(Q(jω)φ), where φCPE = 1 is ideal and φCPE ≈ 0.8 is typical. A turn-up at very low f, where the 45° line bends toward vertical, is finite-length diffusion: the Li+ gradient has reached the particle centre at ω ≈ D/a² (a = particle radius), and W behaves like a capacitor rather than a half-space.

One interface, two electrodes. A Randles fit to a two-terminal cell lumps anode and cathode together. Real Li-ion EIS often shows two arcs, a small one at high f from the graphite SEI, a larger one at mid f from the cathode charge transfer, that this simplified Randles compresses into one. Separating them cleanly requires a reference electrode (three-electrode EIS).

Aging as a deforming spectrum

Every Randles element drifts as a cell ages, and every drift has a chapter responsible:

  • Rs rises, electrolyte consumed by SEI formation, interconnects loosened by thermal cycling (Part 2, Part 6). The high-f intercept slides right.
  • Rct rises, Li+ must tunnel through a thickening SEI before the charge-transfer step, and active surface area drops as particles passivate (Part 6). The semicircle swells.
  • σW rises, particle cracking lengthens diffusion paths and lost lithium inventory steepens the concentration gradient (Part 5, Part 6). The low-f tail stands taller.

Intercept slides right, semicircle swells, tail stands taller. Run a periodic impedance sweep and that triplet is the cell's aging log, extracted through the terminals without cracking the can.

The cell, in one curve

The high-f intercept says how the wiring is holding up. The semicircle says how alive the interface is. The 45° tail says how much lithium is still free to move. Their drift over months and years is the cell's diary.
