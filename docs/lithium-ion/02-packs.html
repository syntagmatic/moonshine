Packs: Many Cells, One Pain Point

Three questions dominate pack design: how series × parallel arithmetic works, what happens when identical cells drift apart, and what stops one cell's failure from spreading.

One 18650 cell holds about 10 Wh, enough for a keyboard light. Useful packs wire cells in series for voltage (flashlight 6 V, e-bike 48 V, EV 350–400 V) and in parallel for capacity (EV packs hold 50–100 kWh). The same physics binds a 4-cell flashlight and a 4,000-cell EV; only the arithmetic changes.

Moving from one cell to many introduces a problem: cells are not identical, and the weakest cell wins. One weak cell in a series string throttles the whole string. One hot cell in a parallel group ages faster than its neighbours. Six canonical scales, from a 4-cell flashlight to a 50,000-cell grid container, face the same three questions.

The arithmetic

Two rules. In a series string the voltages add; capacity stays the cell's. In a parallel group the capacities add; voltage stays the cell's. An nSpP pack at Vc, Qc therefore delivers nVc at pQc. A 13S5P e-bike at 3.7 V × 2.9 Ah is 48 V × 14.5 Ah ≈ 700 Wh. A Tesla Model 3 Long Range pack (96S46P of 21700 NCA cells, ≈4.8 Ah each) comes out around 355 V × 220 Ah ≈ 78 kWh.

Failure breaks the arithmetic asymmetrically. A cell that dies in a parallel group just trims that group's capacity; current still flows through the remaining p − 1 cells. A cell that dies open in a series string breaks the chain: on a cheap solder-tabbed drill pack with no parallel redundancy, the pack voltage goes to zero. EV packs spend real money on laser-welded busbars and deliberate parallel redundancy so one bad cell is survivable.

Cells are not identical

Three sources of mismatch. Factory binning: even a graded lot carries ≈2–5% capacity spread and similar resistance spread. In-service aging: the cell near the pack's hot spot, or the one that runs closer to the top of the SOC window, ages faster because SEI growth is Arrhenius in T and exponential in SOC. Historical abuse: one dropped pack, one hard fast-charge in the cold, one microdefect, and a single cell has drifted 10% while its neighbours have moved 2%.

Parallel groups self-balance: cells on a shared busbar must sit at the same voltage, so a weaker cell draws less current and recovers. A series string has no such luxury. Every cell sees the same current, and the first cell to hit its lower cut-off forces the BMS to open the string even if the other n − 1 cells have charge left. The weakest series cell sets pack usable capacity, and the gap only widens with age.

Why “bad batteries” die fast. A four-year-old laptop pack dies because one of its six cells has drifted 15% low. Charging terminates when that cell hits 4.20 V, leaving the other five near 4.12, a little missed energy. Discharging terminates when it hits 3.0 V, leaving the rest near 3.2, a lot of missed energy. The pack behaves as if it were made entirely of that weakest cell. Balancing buys the deficit back, either by bleeding the stronger cells (passive) or shuttling charge between them (active).

When one cell goes

The worst pack failure is thermal runaway. When a cell shorts internally (dendrite, contamination, mechanical crush) it dumps stored energy as heat in seconds. SEI breakdown begins near 90 °C, the separator melts around 130–160 °C, and above 200–230 °C the NMC cathode decomposes and releases oxygen that ignites the electrolyte. A single cell can peak at 600–800 °C. The pack-level question is whether that heat crosses into its neighbours before cooling pulls it away.

Propagation is a race. Conduction: the hot cell heats the can of its neighbour, dragging it toward its own trigger temperature. Cooling: air, cold plates, liquid coolant, or full dielectric immersion removes heat before the neighbour ignites. The heat-transfer coefficient climbs roughly two orders of magnitude across this ladder, from ≈10 W/m²K for still air to several thousand for immersion, and pack-level investment follows. Consumer tools have air gaps; EV packs add liquid cold-plates, aerogel firewalls, and upward vent paths; grid containers run dielectric immersion with explicit module isolation.

The Samsung Galaxy Note 7 recall in 2016 stemmed from separator defects that shorted a handful of cells per million. The Boeing 787's 2013 grounding traced back to thermal runaway in its LCO batteries. Tesla pack fires have started with a single cell punctured by road debris, with the chain stopped (or not) by liquid-cooled module walls. The goal of pack-level thermal engineering is to break the chain.

Real packs are not flat grids. Production EV packs add structural firewalls between modules, per-cell pressure-relief vents, and coolant loops routed so a burning module doesn't heat its neighbours. Grid installations add meters of air between racks and explicit rupture zones. The goal is not to make runaway impossible (a bad enough defect always finds a way) but to keep it isolated: one cell, at worst one module, not the whole pack.

Takeaways

  • Series adds voltage; parallel adds capacity. nSpP × (Vc, Qc) gives pack voltage, Ah, and Wh.
  • Failure modes differ: a failed cell in a parallel group trims capacity; a failed cell in a series string can break the pack voltage chain.
  • Identical-at-birth cells fan out over hundreds of cycles. The weakest series cell sets pack usable capacity.
  • Thermal runaway is a race between inter-cell heat conduction and cooling topology. Pack-scale investment in cooling is investment in chain-breaking.
