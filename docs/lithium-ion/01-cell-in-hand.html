A Cell in Your Hand

A small metal cylinder you can hold. The sizes that show up in practice, what the voltage means, how charging works, and how a flashlight runs on one.

Lithium-ion cells are the quiet infrastructure of modern life. Vapes, flashlights, phones, laptops, drills, and increasingly cars run on minor variations of the same object: a small, heavy cylinder (or flat silver pouch) that holds energy that would have astonished an engineer in 1990.

Common cells

A handful of form factors cover most of what you will ever hold. They are standardized, so an "18650" from one manufacturer fits in the same holder as one from another. The name is literal: the first two digits give the diameter in millimetres, the next two the height, and the trailing zero marks it as cylindrical: so 18 mm × 65 mm. A few notes on this lineup:

  • The AA is not lithium-ion. It is an alkaline primary cell, 1.5 V, non-rechargeable in its common form. It is here for scale.
  • The 18650 is the workhorse of the laptop era and the original Tesla Model S/X pack (Panasonic's NCA-chemistry variant). If a "lithium battery" in a flashlight or vape looks like a fat AA, it is almost certainly an 18650.
  • The 21700 (Tesla calls it "2170") is the Model 3 / Model Y cell. Twenty percent more diameter, eight percent more height: about fifty percent more energy per cell. Bigger is cheaper per Wh, up to a thermal limit. Tesla's newer 4680 (46 × 80 mm, Cybertruck and some Texas-built Model Ys) pushes the same logic further.
  • The 26650 is a flashlight and high-drain favorite. Most sold for flashlights are LFP chemistry, which has less energy per gram than NMC but is safer, more tolerant of abuse, and longer-lived.
  • The prismatic LFP pouch is the home-battery and grid-storage cell. Stackable, lower energy density than a cylinder, but cheap and very cycle-stable. Your Powerwall is full of these.

What the voltage means

Put a multimeter across a cell sitting on a table with nothing connected. You read a voltage, say 3.72 V on a half-charged 18650. That's the open-circuit voltage (OCV), set by the chemistry and by how much lithium is currently in the graphite electrode.

OCV tracks state of charge (SOC), but not linearly, and differently for each chemistry. An NMC cell runs from near 4.2 V full down to about 3.0 V empty, smooth slope between. An LFP cell runs from about 3.4 V rested-full to 2.5 V at cutoff, but most of that range hides on a flat plateau near 3.3 V. You can read an NMC cell's charge from voltage alone; an LFP cell, not really.

Nominal vs. actual. Datasheets quote a "nominal voltage," commonly 3.6–3.7 V for NMC/NCA and 3.2 V for LFP. That is the average across a full discharge. It is useful for rating a pack ("this is a 48 V pack") but the real voltage at any moment depends on charge, current, temperature, and age.

Charging: CCCV

You cannot connect a cell to 4.2 V and walk away. When the cell is low, that pushes enormous current, enough to heat it, damage electrodes, and start a fire. Pushing a fixed current forever is also impossible: past the chemistry's ceiling, lithium stops intercalating cleanly into graphite and starts plating as metal. That is how thermal runaways begin.

The standard protocol is two-phase. First, constant current (CC): push a steady current, sized to the cell's capacity: a "1 C" rate fills an empty cell in roughly an hour. Voltage rises with SOC. When it hits the chemistry's ceiling (4.2 V for NMC, 3.65 V for LFP), the charger switches to constant voltage (CV): pin the voltage at the ceiling, let the cell take whatever current it wants. As the cell fills, it accepts less. When current tapers to roughly 3–5% of the CC rate, the cell is called full and the charger disconnects. Watch it unfold.

Figure 2. A CCCV charge traced live. Voltage (left axis, solid) climbs during the constant-current phase (orange band); on hitting Vmax the charger pins the voltage and lets current (right axis, dashed) taper through the constant-voltage phase (green band). The readout calls the moment the phases switch. Raise the C-rate to finish faster; switch chemistry to move the ceiling.

Why not charge faster? Higher C-rates shorten the CC phase but lengthen the CV tail proportionally; most of the wall-clock benefit is gone past 1 C. And above 1 C the cell warms noticeably, which accelerates aging. "Fast-charging" an EV is a careful dance around these limits.

Running it: voltage under load

Connect a load (a flashlight LED) and the cell delivers current. Voltage while running reads less than OCV. A fresh 18650 has an internal resistance of roughly 25–50 mΩ, so pulling half an amp drops the terminals 15–25 mV below rest; three amps drops them 75–150 mV. This voltage sag under load is the most consequential behaviour of a real cell.

The flashlight case is clean. Two driver topologies sit between cell and LED. A constant-current driver is a small switching regulator that holds LED current steady regardless of cell voltage, down to a dropout threshold, giving flat brightness for most of the runtime, then a cliff. A linear driver (or direct drive) is barely a driver at all: LED current, and so brightness, tracks the cell voltage, gradually dimming over the whole runtime. Neither is better; they trade flat output for efficiency and simplicity. Compare them below.

Figure 3. Top: two simplified flashlight drivers. Bottom: a runtime comparison. Cell voltage (left axis) falls as the cell discharges; the linear driver's output (right axis) tracks it down, while the constant-current driver holds flat brightness until the terminal voltage drops below its dropout threshold — then the cliff. Dashed verticals mark the moment each driver's output effectively ends; the area under each lumen curve is total light delivered.

Running voltage sits below OCV by an amount that scales with current; the gap comes from internal resistance and charge-transfer kinetics. And as cells age, that resistance creeps up, so sag grows at the same current. An aged cell has nearly its original OCV on the shelf, but under load it droops further and hits the cutoff earlier. The flashlight dims; the phone shuts off "at 20%"; the drill bogs down.

Takeaways

  • A handful of standardized cylinders (18650, 21700, 26650, 4680) and one flat pouch cover most of the lithium-ion world you actually touch.
  • Charging is constant-current to the voltage ceiling, then constant-voltage until the current tapers to a few percent of where it started. Overcharging past the ceiling is where fires live.
  • Running voltage is always less than open-circuit voltage. The sag scales with current and grows as the cell ages.
