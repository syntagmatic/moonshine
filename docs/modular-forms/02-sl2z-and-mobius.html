SL₂(ℤ) and the Möbius Action

Part 2 of 20. The modular group SL₂(ℤ) acts on ℍ by γ·τ = (aτ+b)/(cτ+d). Generated by S: τ ↦ −1/τ and T: τ ↦ τ+1 with relation (ST)³ = −I. The action is faithful and isometric.

The previous explainer identified the orientation-preserving isometries of ℍ as PSL₂(ℝ). Now we restrict to the discrete subgroup SL₂(ℤ), the modular group, the symmetry group of modular forms. It is generated by two elements: an inversion S and a translation T.

The modular group

The special linear group SL₂(ℤ) consists of all 2×2 matrices with integer entries and determinant 1:

This is an infinite, discrete subgroup of SL₂(ℝ). Its elements act on the upper half-plane ℍ by the same Möbius transformation that defines isometries of the Poincaré metric:

We need to check that this action actually maps ℍ to itself. The key computation is the imaginary part of the image:

ad − bc = 1 and Im τ > 0 make the numerator positive; |cτ + d|² is positive and nonzero, so Im(γ · τ) > 0 and γ preserves ℍ. Large c pushes points toward the real axis.

The matrices γ and −γ define the same Möbius transformation (the signs cancel in the numerator and denominator). The quotient PSL₂(ℤ) = SL₂(ℤ)/{±I} is therefore the group that acts faithfully on ℍ. We will mostly work with SL₂(ℤ) and keep this sign ambiguity in mind.

The generators S and T

Two elements of SL₂(ℤ) are fundamental:

Their geometric actions are transparent. T is a unit translation: it shifts every point one unit to the right. S is an inversion followed by reflection: it maps τ to −1/τ, which in polar form inverts the modulus and reflects across the imaginary axis. The point τ = i is fixed by S (since −1/i = i), and S swaps the regions |τ| < 1 and |τ| > 1.

These two matrices satisfy the relations:

S² = −I (acting trivially on ℍ); (ST)³ = −I as well. These are defining: SL₂(ℤ) = ⟨S, T | S² = −I, (ST)³ = −I⟩, and PSL₂(ℤ) = ℤ/2 * ℤ/3.

Why two generators suffice. The Euclidean algorithm: for γ = [[a,b],[c,d]] with c ≠ 0, reduce |c| by applying T or S. The process terminates at a matrix with c = 0, which is ±T^n. Reading in reverse gives a word in S and T.

Orbits under repeated application

The orbit of τ under S, T reveals the group's structure: T translates horizontally, S inverts through the unit circle, and alternating sequences scatter τ across ℍ.

SL₂(ℤ) is discrete, so every orbit is discrete: no two orbit points can be arbitrarily close. This is what makes the fundamental domain in the next explainer a region with positive area.

Figure 2. Orbit tracker. Drag the blue starting point, then apply S or T repeatedly. Each step adds a segment and a new point (numbered). The orbit scatters across ℍ but remains discrete. Try alternating S and T to see how quickly the orbit spreads.

Orbits and equivalence. Two points τ₁, τ₂ ∈ ℍ are SL₂(ℤ)-equivalent if some γ ∈ SL₂(ℤ) maps one to the other. The orbit of τ is its full equivalence class. In Part 3 we will carve out a fundamental domain: a region in ℍ that contains exactly one representative from each orbit. The generators S and T are precisely the identifications that glue the domain's boundary edges together.

Takeaways

  • SL₂(ℤ) is the group of 2×2 integer matrices with determinant 1, acting on ℍ by τ ↦ (aτ+b)/(cτ+d).
  • The action preserves ℍ because Im(γτ) = Im τ / |cτ+d|² > 0.
  • S: τ ↦ −1/τ (inversion) and T: τ ↦ τ+1 (translation) generate the full group.
  • The relations S² = −I and (ST)³ = −I are the defining relations of SL₂(ℤ).
  • Orbits under SL₂(ℤ) are discrete, setting the stage for the fundamental domain construction.
