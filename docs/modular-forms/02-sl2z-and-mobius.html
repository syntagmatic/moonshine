SL₂(ℤ), Generators and Relations

Part 2 of 14. A modular form is defined by how it behaves under the modular group SL₂(ℤ), the integer matrices acting on the half-plane. This part shows that the whole group is built from two moves, a shift T and an inversion S, and that the two relations between them account for the only two points, up to symmetry, that a nontrivial element holds fixed.

Part 1 ended with SL₂(ℝ) acting on ℍ by isometries. The matrices with integer entries form the modular group SL₂(ℤ), a discrete group of symmetries. Every modular form in this series is defined by how it behaves under this group.

The modular group

The special linear group SL₂(ℤ) consists of all 2×2 matrices with integer entries and determinant 1:

This is an infinite, discrete subgroup of SL₂(ℝ). Its elements act on the upper half-plane ℍ by the same Möbius transformation that defines isometries of the Poincaré metric:

The numerator is positive because ad − bc = 1 and Im τ > 0, and the denominator |cτ + d|² is positive, so Im(γ · τ) > 0. Large c pushes points toward the real axis.

The matrices γ and −γ give the same Möbius transformation, since the signs cancel. The group that acts faithfully on ℍ is therefore the quotient PSL₂(ℤ) = SL₂(ℤ)/{±I}.

The generators S and T

SL₂(ℤ) is generated by two elements:

T shifts every point one unit to the right. S sends τ to −1/τ, which inverts the modulus and reflects across the imaginary axis. It swaps the inside of the unit circle with the outside and fixes i, since −1/i = i.

Every γ in SL₂(ℤ) is a product of S, T and T⁻¹, up to sign. The proof is the Euclidean algorithm run on the first column (a, c). If c ≠ 0, pick the integer n nearest to a/c. Multiplying on the left by T⁻ⁿ replaces a with a − nc, which is smaller than |c| in absolute value. Multiplying on the left by S⁻¹ then swaps the column into (c, −(a − nc)), so the lower-left entry has strictly shrunk. Repeat until c = 0. Relations

The generators are not independent. Squaring S gives −I, and so does cubing ST:

Since −I acts trivially on ℍ, in PSL₂(ℤ) the element S has order 2 and ST has order 3. Every other identity between words in S and T follows from these two:

The symbol ∗ is the free product. An element of ℤ/2 ∗ ℤ/3 is an alternating word in a letter of order 2 and a letter of order 3, and two such words are equal only if they are identical. It has trivial centre and contains free subgroups of every finite rank.

Because SL₂(ℤ) is discrete, the images of τ never pile up at a point of ℍ. They crowd toward the real axis in the Euclidean picture, but in the hyperbolic metric any two distinct images are at least as far apart as τ is from its nearest image. Two points in the same orbit are called SL₂(ℤ)-equivalent, and Part 3 chooses one representative from every orbit.

Elliptic points

For most τ the only element of PSL₂(ℤ) that fixes τ is the identity. There are two exceptions, and they come from the relations.

The first is i. S · i = −1/i = i, so the stabiliser of i in PSL₂(ℤ) is {I, S} ≅ ℤ/2. Near i, S acts as a half-turn: it rotates the plane by π about i.

The second is ρ = e2πi/3 = (−1 + i√3)/2, a primitive cube root of unity. Since ST · τ = −1/(τ + 1) and ρ + 1 = eπi/3, we get ST · ρ = −e−πi/3 = ρ. The stabiliser of ρ is {I, ST, (ST)²} ≅ ℤ/3, and ST rotates the plane about ρ by 2π/3.

Drag the probe point in Figure 3. Its images under the stabiliser stay on one hyperbolic circle around the fixed point, and the geodesic rays to them meet at equal angles, 180° at i and 120° at ρ.

There are no other elliptic points up to equivalence. A matrix γ ∈ SL₂(ℝ), γ ≠ ±I, fixes a point of ℍ only when |tr γ| < 2. For an integer matrix that leaves tr γ ∈ {−1, 0, 1}. Trace 0 means γ² = −I, and every such γ is conjugate in SL₂(ℤ) to ±S, so it fixes a point in the orbit of i. Trace ±1 means γ has order 3 in PSL₂(ℤ) and is conjugate there to (ST)±1, so it fixes a point in the orbit of ρ. Every elliptic point of SL₂(ℤ) is therefore equivalent to i or to ρ. The corner ρ + 1 = −ρ̄ of the fundamental domain is T · ρ, so it lies in the orbit of ρ.

In the quotient SL₂(ℤ)\ℍ these two orbits become cone points. A neighbourhood of i folds in half, and a neighbourhood of ρ folds into thirds, giving cone angles π and 2π/3. The fundamental domain of Part 3 shows this folding, and the cone points reappear as the fractional correction terms when Part 5 counts modular forms.
