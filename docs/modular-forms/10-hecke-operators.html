Hecke Operators and the Hecke Algebra

Part 10 of 20. Tn is a linear operator on Mk. On q-series: Tp acts by am ↦ apm + pk−1 am/p. Hecke eigenforms have multiplicative coefficients and Euler-product L-functions.

We have built up the spaces Mk and Sk, found their dimensions, and written down explicit bases of Eisenstein series and cusp forms. Now we introduce the most powerful piece of machinery in the classical theory: the Hecke operators Tn. These are linear operators on the space of modular forms, and they commute with one another. Simultaneous eigenvectors for all Tn -- the Hecke eigenforms -- have Fourier coefficients that are multiplicative, and their L-functions admit Euler products. This is the bridge between modular forms and arithmetic.

What Hecke operators do

The formal definition of Tn involves double cosets and sums over lattice sublattices. We will not need that definition here. What matters for computation is the action on q-expansions, and that is entirely concrete.

The convention is that a(m/p) = 0 whenever p does not divide m. So for each coefficient b(m), we look at two places in the original q-expansion: the coefficient a(pm) one step "further out," and the coefficient a(m/p) one step "closer in," weighted by pk−1. This recipe is linear in f and preserves both Mk and Sk: if f is a cusp form, so is Tp(f).

For composite n, the Hecke operators satisfy the recursion Tmn = Tm Tn when gcd(m, n) = 1, and Tpr = Tp Tpr−1 − pk−1 Tpr−2 for prime powers. This means the full collection {Tn} is determined by the prime operators {Tp}.

Commutativity and simultaneous diagonalisation

The most remarkable property of the Hecke operators is that they commute:

This holds for all m, n -- even when they share common factors. The Hecke operators therefore generate a commutative algebra of linear operators on Mk (and on Sk). By the spectral theorem for commuting operators on finite-dimensional vector spaces, we can simultaneously diagonalise all Tn. The simultaneous eigenvectors are the Hecke eigenforms.

When does this happen automatically? Whenever dim Sk = 1, there is only one cusp form (up to scaling), and it must be an eigenform for every Tn. The first case is S12, which is one-dimensional and spanned by Δ. So Δ is a Hecke eigenform for free -- no diagonalisation required. The same holds for S16, S18, S20, and S22, each of which is one-dimensional.

The first weight where dim Sk > 1 is k = 24, where dim S24 = 2. Here the space is spanned by Δ · E4³ and Δ · E6² (or any two independent weight-24 cusp forms), and the Hecke operators must be diagonalised to find the two eigenforms. Those eigenforms are no longer recognisable as simple products of Eisenstein series and Δ.

This is a massive compression: an infinite sequence of Fourier coefficients is encoded by its values at primes alone.

Why multiplicativity matters. A generic modular form has coefficients that satisfy no obvious arithmetic relations. Hecke eigenforms are special: their coefficients obey the same multiplicativity that characterises Dirichlet series with Euler products. This is not a coincidence -- it is the mechanism by which modular forms connect to number theory. Every time we prove a modular form is a Hecke eigenform, we unlock multiplicativity for free.

The existence of an Euler product is the hallmark of an "arithmetically significant" L-function. The Riemann zeta function ζ(s) = ∏p (1 − p−s)−1 is the simplest example (weight k = 1 with trivial coefficients). The L-function of Δ is a weight-12 analogue, and its analytic properties (functional equation, analytic continuation, location of zeros) are far richer.

From algebra to analysis. The Hecke algebra is pure linear algebra: commuting operators on a finite-dimensional vector space. The Euler product is pure analysis: an infinite product encoding prime-by-prime factorisation. The bridge between them is the eigenform condition Tp f = a(p) f. This single equation -- an algebraic eigenvector condition -- produces the Euler product, which is analytic gold. It is the mechanism by which the finite-dimensional structure of Mk reaches into the infinite world of primes.

Takeaways

  • Tp on q-series: b(m) = a(pm) + pk−1 a(m/p). The operator is linear, preserves Mk and Sk.
  • Commutativity: Tm Tn = Tn Tm. We can simultaneously diagonalise all Hecke operators.
  • Eigenforms: for normalised eigenforms, λp = a(p), and the coefficients are multiplicative.
  • Δ is an eigenform: Tp(Δ) = τ(p) Δ. The Ramanujan tau function gives the eigenvalues.
  • Euler products: L(s, f) = ∏p (1 − a(p) p−s + pk−1−2s)−1 for normalised Hecke eigenforms.
