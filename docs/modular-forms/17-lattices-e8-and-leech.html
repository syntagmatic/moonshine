E₈, the Leech Lattice, and the Coxeter Projection

Part 17 of 20. Two exceptional lattices underpin the moonshine story: E₈ in 8 dimensions, with 240 roots and optimal packing; and the Leech lattice in 24 dimensions, with no roots, kissing number 196560, and an automorphism group that spontaneously generates sporadic simple groups.

Act I introduced two protagonists and the coincidence between them. Act II builds the structural bridge that will eventually let us prove the coincidence is not a coincidence. That bridge rests on lattice geometry, specifically on two exceptional lattices (E8 in 8 dimensions and the Leech lattice in 24 dimensions), and on the algebraic structures they support.

What E₈ is

A lattice in ℝn is a discrete additive subgroup isomorphic to ℤn: the set of all integer linear combinations of n linearly independent basis vectors. Every lattice tiles ℝn by translating a fundamental cell, and the volume of that cell (the absolute value of the determinant of the basis matrix) is an invariant called the covolume.

The E8 lattice Λ8 is distinguished by three properties:

  1. Even: every vector v ∈ Λ8 has even squared length, v · v ∈ 2ℤ. This means the minimum non-zero squared length is 2, and vectors of squared length 2 are called roots.
  2. Unimodular: the covolume is 1. Equivalently, the lattice equals its own dual: Λ8* = Λ8. This self-duality is an extremely restrictive condition; the only even unimodular lattices in dimensions ≤ 8 are ℤ (in dimension 0, trivially) and E8 itself.
  3. Positive-definite: the inner product is the standard Euclidean one on ℝ8.

In fact, E8 is the unique even, unimodular, positive-definite lattice in 8 dimensions. (Existence and uniqueness go back to Mordell and Witt; the modern proof uses the mass formula for lattices.)

The 240 roots

The root system of E8 is the set of all 240 vectors of squared length 2 in Λ8. In the standard coordinate system on ℝ8, these 240 vectors split into two orbits:

  • 112 vectors of the form ±ei ± ej for 1 ≤ i < j ≤ 8, where ek is the kth standard basis vector. These are the roots of the D8 sublattice.
  • 128 vectors of the form ½(±1, ±1, ±1, ±1, ±1, ±1, ±1, ±1) with an even number of minus signs. These are the half-integer "spinor" vectors that extend D8 to E8.

The Dynkin diagram of E8 encodes the angles between eight simple roots (a minimal generating set for the root system). It has the distinctive shape of a chain of seven nodes with a single branch at the third node from one end, giving a T-shape that Part 5 of the Exceptional Atlas analyses in detail.

Figure 1. The E₈ Dynkin diagram: eight nodes, seven edges, one branch. Each node is a simple root; an edge means the two simple roots meet at 120°; no edge means 90°. The branching at node 3 is the structural feature that makes E₈ exceptional: it is the longest branch-at-three diagram that satisfies the positive-definiteness constraint (1/p + 1/q + 1/r > 1). Adding one more node would violate the constraint, so E₈ is the end of the E-series.

The E8 lattice packing achieves the optimal kissing number (240) and sphere packing density in ℝ8, proven by Maryna Viazovska in 2016. Parts 13–15 of the Exceptional Atlas cover the full story, from the 240 roots through the Gosset polytope and the Coxeter-plane projection to Viazovska's proof and the Leech lattice.

The Leech lattice

In 1967 John Leech published a lattice in ℝ24 that he had constructed using the binary Golay code. In 1968 John Conway, upon learning of the lattice, spent a famous twelve-hour session computing its automorphism group and discovered three new sporadic simple groups in the process. The lattice is now called Λ24 or the Leech lattice, and it is the geometric hub of the moonshine story.

What makes the Leech lattice special

Even unimodular positive-definite lattices exist only in dimensions divisible by 8. In dimension 24, the classification (completed by Niemeier) lists exactly 24 such lattices, distinguished by their root systems. Of those 24, exactly one has an empty root system: no vectors of squared length 2 at all. That is the Leech lattice.

The absence of roots is the key property. It means:

  • The minimum squared length of a non-zero vector is 4, not 2. Every non-zero vector is at least as long as √4 = 2.
  • The lattice has no Lie-theoretic structure in the sense of a root system. It cannot be built as a root lattice of a Lie algebra. This makes its automorphism group fundamentally different from the Weyl groups that govern E8.
  • When we build the lattice vertex operator algebra VΛ₂₄ in Act III, the absence of roots will mean the weight-1 space of VΛ₂₄ is zero, a property that the moonshine module V♮ inherits as dim V0♮ = 0.

The binary Golay code

The Leech lattice is assembled from a specific finite object: the extended binary Golay code [24, 12, 8]. It is a collection of 4,096 twenty-four-bit strings (out of all 224 = 16,777,216 possible strings) with the property that any two distinct codewords differ in at least 8 positions. That minimum distance of 8 is what makes the code useful: a string with up to 3 bit-flips away from a codeword has that codeword as its unique nearest neighbour, so the code corrects any 3-bit error. Its weight distribution is {0: 1, 8: 759, 12: 2576, 16: 759, 24: 1}; the 759 weight-8 codewords are called octads and they carry the combinatorial soul of the code.

Leech's construction (1967) takes these 4,096 codewords and lifts each to a family of 24-dimensional integer vectors: given a codeword c ∈ {0, 1}24, collect all integer vectors v ∈ ℤ24 whose coordinates satisfy vi ≡ ci (mod 2) plus a correction for the global parity; properly scaled and sublattice-corrected, the union is the Leech lattice. The minimum distance 8 of the code becomes the minimum squared norm 4 of the lattice, and the rootless property (no shell at squared norm 2) corresponds to the code having no weight-4 codewords.

The Conway groups

The automorphism group of the Leech lattice, the group of all distance-preserving linear maps ℝ24 → ℝ24 that send Λ24 to itself, is called Co0. It has order

Co0 contains {±I} as its centre; the quotient Co0/{±I} is the Conway group Co1, a sporadic simple group of order ~4.16 × 1018. All three Conway groups are sporadic simple groups sitting inside the happy family of the Monster:

  • Co1 = Co0/{±I}: simple, order 4157776806543360000. Acts faithfully on the minimum-shell vectors.
  • Co2: the stabiliser in Co0 of a type-2 vector (a vector of squared norm 4). Simple, order 42305421312000.
  • Co3: the stabiliser of a type-3 vector (squared norm 6). Simple, order 495766656000.

Deep holes

A deep hole of a lattice packing is a point maximally far from every lattice point: the centre of the largest empty sphere. For the Leech lattice, Conway and Sloane showed that the deep holes are classified by the 23 other Niemeier lattices. The most important for the moonshine story is the E₈³ deep hole, where the local structure decomposes into three orthogonal copies of E8.

Visualising the Leech lattice

A lattice in ℝ24 is invisible in the same way a symphony is inaudible when printed on paper: it has more dimensions than our output device can handle. Mathematicians work around this by projecting: choosing a 2-dimensional subspace and orthogonally projecting the lattice vectors onto it. Most projections produce a featureless blob. A few special ones, chosen to align with the lattice's symmetries, produce highly structured mandalas of concentric rings.

The Coxeter plane

The most informative 2D projection is onto the Coxeter plane, defined as the eigenplane of the Coxeter element (the product of all simple reflections in a fixed order) for the eigenvalue e2πi/h, where h is the Coxeter number. The Leech lattice has no Weyl group (it has no roots), but its automorphism group Co0 contains elements whose eigenvalue structure picks out a preferred plane with high rotational symmetry. The projection of the 196560 minimum-shell vectors onto this plane reveals concentric rings of points, each ring at a different radius, with the number of points per ring determined by the orbital structure of Co0.

The E₈³ deep hole

The most important structural feature of the Leech lattice for the moonshine story is the E₈³ deep hole: a point in ℝ24 that is maximally far from every lattice point, whose local neighbourhood decomposes as three orthogonal copies of the E8 lattice.

Concretely, there exist three mutually orthogonal 8-dimensional subspaces W1, W2, W3 ⊂ ℝ24 with W1 ⊕ W2 ⊕ W3 = ℝ24, such that when you restrict the Leech lattice to the neighbourhood of the deep hole, the nearby lattice vectors decompose as elements of E8 ⊕ E8 ⊕ E8. This is the E₈³ deep hole, and it provides the explicit geometric link from E8 to Λ24.

Figure 5. Schematic of the E₈³ deep hole. The 24-dimensional space decomposes as three orthogonal 8-dimensional blocks. In each block, the lattice vectors near the deep hole form an E₈ root system. The deep hole itself sits at the centre of the Voronoi cell, equidistant from all nearby lattice points. This decomposition is the key geometric ingredient for the E₈ → Leech → Monster bridge.

From lattice to algebra. The E₈³ deep hole is not just a geometric curiosity; it is the doorway from lattice geometry into algebra. Each of the three E₈ copies produces a "conformal vector of central charge ½" in the vertex operator algebra on the Leech lattice, and those conformal vectors relate to the 2A involutions of the Monster. The geometry of the deep hole is the geometry of the Monster's internal structure.

Takeaways

  • E₈ is the unique even unimodular lattice in 8D. 240 roots, optimal packing (Viazovska 2016).
  • The Leech lattice Λ₂₄ is the unique even unimodular lattice in 24D with no roots. Kissing number 196560 (optimal), Aut = Co₀.
  • Co₁, Co₂, Co₃ are sporadic simple groups discovered by Conway in the automorphism group of Λ₂₄.
  • The E₈³ deep hole decomposes ℝ²⁴ into three orthogonal E₈ copies, the geometric bridge to the Monster.
  • No roots → no weight-1 space → no Lie symmetry → Aut(V♮) is purely finite (the Monster).
