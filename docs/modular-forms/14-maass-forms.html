Maass Forms and Non-Holomorphic Generalisations

Part 14 of 20. Real-analytic eigenfunctions of the hyperbolic Laplacian Δf = λf on ℍ/SL₂(ℤ). Fourier expansions involve Bessel functions. The Selberg trace formula relates eigenvalues to closed geodesics.

Beyond holomorphicity

For thirteen explainers we have studied holomorphic modular forms: functions on the upper half-plane that are complex-differentiable, transform with the weight-k automorphy factor, and grow moderately at the cusp. Holomorphicity is a severe constraint. It forces Fourier expansions to involve only non-negative powers of , it makes the space of weight-k forms finite-dimensional, and it ties coefficients to arithmetic quantities like divisor sums and point counts on elliptic curves. But holomorphic forms are not the only natural functions on the quotient surface SL₂(ℤ)\ℍ.

There is another class, discovered by Hans Maass in 1949, which relaxes holomorphicity but imposes a different rigidity condition. A Maass form is a real-analytic function u: ℍ → ℝ (or ℂ) that is invariant under SL₂(ℤ), is an eigenfunction of the hyperbolic Laplacian, and decays in the cusp. These three conditions together are enough to produce a rich spectral theory, but the flavour is quite different from the holomorphic world. Where holomorphic forms live in algebra and number theory, Maass forms live at the intersection of spectral geometry, analytic number theory, and quantum chaos.

The operator Δ is the Laplace–Beltrami operator of the Poincaré metric. It is invariant under every isometry of ℍ, and in particular under the action of SL₂(ℤ). This means that if u is an eigenfunction of Δ and also SL₂(ℤ)-invariant, then both properties are mutually compatible: the group symmetry does not interfere with the spectral condition. Holomorphic modular forms of weight 0 would be constant (by Liouville's theorem on the compact quotient). Maass forms fill the gap: they are the non-trivial weight-0 automorphic eigenfunctions.

The Laplacian and its spectrum

On a compact Riemannian manifold, the Laplacian has a discrete spectrum of eigenvalues accumulating at infinity. The surface SL₂(ℤ)\ℍ is not compact — it has a cusp stretching to y = ∞ — but it has finite hyperbolic area (equal to π/3), and the Laplacian still has a discrete part to its spectrum, supplemented by a continuous part coming from Eisenstein series. We focus on the discrete spectrum: the Maass cusp forms.

The eigenvalues are not known in closed form. Unlike the holomorphic case, where dimensions and Fourier coefficients are given by exact formulas involving divisor sums and Bernoulli numbers, the Maass spectrum is transcendental and can only be approximated numerically. This makes Maass forms simultaneously harder and more mysterious.

Weyl's law gives the count. The number of Maass eigenvalues with r ≤ R grows as R²/12 for large R. This follows from the general Weyl asymptotic for the Laplacian on a surface of area π/3. The distribution of the gaps between consecutive eigenvalues is predicted by random matrix theory (the GOE ensemble), a phenomenon sometimes called "quantum chaos on the modular surface."

Fourier expansions with Bessel functions

A holomorphic modular form has a Fourier expansion in powers of : the familiar q-expansion. A Maass form, being real-analytic rather than holomorphic, has a different kind of Fourier expansion. The periodicity u(x + 1, y) = u(x, y) still forces a Fourier series in x, but the y-dependence is governed by a second-order ODE — the modified Bessel equation — rather than by the simple exponential e−2πny of the holomorphic case.

The coefficients a(n) are the arithmetic content of the Maass form. They satisfy Hecke multiplicativity relations (just as holomorphic Hecke eigenforms do), and they are expected to satisfy a Ramanujan-type bound |a(n)| ≤ C · nε for every ε > 0. This is still unproved in general — one of the outstanding conjectures in analytic number theory.

Bessel vs. exponential. In the holomorphic expansion f(τ) = Σ a(n) qn, the y-dependence is e−2πny — a simple exponential decay. In the Maass expansion, Kir(2πny) also decays exponentially for large y, but it oscillates as a function of r (the spectral parameter). This oscillation is what gives the eigenfunction its wave-like nodal pattern on the fundamental domain.

The Selberg trace formula

The deepest structural result about Maass forms is the Selberg trace formula, proved by Atle Selberg in 1956. It is an exact identity that relates two completely different ways of describing the quotient surface SL₂(ℤ)\ℍ:

  • Spectral side: a sum over the Laplacian eigenvalues λj = 1/4 + rj².
  • Geometric side: a sum over conjugacy classes of hyperbolic elements in SL₂(ℤ), which correspond to closed geodesics on the surface.

The left side is purely analytic: it asks about the eigenvalues of a differential operator. The right side is purely geometric: the lengths of closed geodesics are determined by the traces of hyperbolic matrices in SL₂(ℤ). The trace formula says these two descriptions encode exactly the same information.

This duality is a non-abelian generalisation of the Poisson summation formula (which relates a sum over integers to a sum over dual integers). It has had enormous consequences:

  • Weyl's law: the eigenvalue counting function N(R) ~ R²/12 follows from the identity term on the geometric side.
  • Prime geodesic theorem: closed geodesics of length ≤ L grow as eL/L, analogous to the prime number theorem.
  • Selberg zeta function: an Euler product over closed geodesics whose zeros encode the Maass eigenvalues, paralleling the Riemann zeta function.

The Selberg trace formula is the prototype for the Arthur–Selberg trace formula on higher-rank groups, which is the engine behind the Langlands programme. Takeaways

  • Maass forms are real-analytic, SL₂(ℤ)-invariant eigenfunctions of the hyperbolic Laplacian Δ = −y²(∂²/∂x² + ∂²/∂y²).
  • The eigenvalue is λ = 1/4 + r² with r ≥ 0 real. The first eigenvalue has r₁ ≈ 9.534.
  • Fourier expansions replace the exponential q-series with Bessel-function expansions: u(x, y) = √y Σ a(n) Kir(2π|n|y) e2πinx.
  • The Selberg trace formula equates a spectral sum (over eigenvalues) with a geometric sum (over closed geodesics), a deep duality between analysis and geometry.
  • Maass forms complete the spectral decomposition of L²(SL₂(ℤ)\ℍ) alongside holomorphic forms and Eisenstein series.
