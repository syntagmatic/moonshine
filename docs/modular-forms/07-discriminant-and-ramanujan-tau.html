The Discriminant, Ramanujan's τ, and Cusp Forms

Part 7 of 14. The discriminant Δ is the first cusp form. Its coefficients, Ramanujan's τ(n), follow patterns Ramanujan conjectured in 1916, the last proved in 1974.

The Eisenstein series tend to 1 at the cusp. A cusp form is a modular form that vanishes there instead, and the first one is the modular discriminant Δ(τ), at weight 12. Its coefficients τ(n) are Ramanujan's tau function. Ramanujan conjectured that τ is multiplicative and bounded in size, and found that it agrees with a divisor sum mod 691. Multiplicativity is explained by the Hecke operators of Part 8, and the bound by Deligne's 1974 proof of the Weil conjectures. This part computes Δ and checks the patterns numerically.

In the heatmap, |Δ| on the fundamental domain is largest at its bottom corners ρ and ρ + 1 and falls to zero toward the cusp. Below the domain it grows again, as the factor (cτ + d)12 takes over, except near the rational points on the real axis, which are cusps too.

The product formula shows Δ vanishes to order 1 at the cusp: as Im τ → ∞, q → 0 and each (1 − qn) → 1, so Δ ~ q → 0.

The name comes from elliptic curves. A lattice Λ = ℤτ + ℤ defines an elliptic curve y² = 4x³ − g₂x − g₃, and g₂³ − 27g₃², which is the cubic's discriminant up to a factor of 16, equals (2π)¹² Δ(τ). Δ ≠ 0 iff the cubic has distinct roots and the curve is non-singular. Since Δ vanishes only at the cusp, every lattice in ℍ gives a non-singular curve.

Ramanujan's τ function

The q-expansion of Δ defines an arithmetic function:

n ↦ τ(n) is Ramanujan's tau function. Values: τ(1) = 1, τ(2) = −24, τ(3) = 252, τ(4) = −1472, τ(5) = 4830. Signs oscillate irregularly, and the sizes sit on the scale of n11/2: for n ≤ 30 the ratio |τ(n)|/n11/2 stays between 0.2 and 1.2.

In 1916 Ramanujan computed the first 30 values and conjectured three properties:

  1. Multiplicativity: τ(mn) = τ(m)τ(n) whenever gcd(m, n) = 1.
  2. Prime-power recursion: τ(pr+1) = τ(p)τ(pr) − p11τ(pr−1) for each prime p.
  3. The Ramanujan conjecture: |τ(p)| ≤ 2p11/2 for each prime p.

Mordell proved the first two properties in 1917. His argument used operators on weight-12 forms that Hecke later generalised in 1937 into the Hecke operators of Part 8. In that language Δ is a Hecke eigenform, and eigenforms have multiplicative coefficients. The third was not proved until Deligne's 1974 proof of the Weil conjectures.

The congruence mod 691

Ramanujan also found a congruence:

691 is the numerator of B12/12, where B12 = −691/2730. The normalisation of E12 divides by B12, so E12 = 1 + (65520/691) Σ σ11(n) qn. Since dim M12 = 2, E12 is a combination of E43 and Δ. Matching the constant term and the q-coefficient (720 for E43) gives E12 = E43 − (432000/691)Δ. Clear the denominator and compare coefficients of qn:

The E43 term has integer coefficients multiplied by 691, so it vanishes mod 691. Both 65520 and −432000 are ≡ 566 mod 691, and 566 is invertible mod the prime 691, so τ(n) ≡ σ11(n). It is the first case of a general pattern, in which primes dividing Bernoulli numerators control congruences between modular forms.

691 is an irregular prime: it divides the numerator of a Bernoulli number Bk with k ≤ p − 3 (here B12), which by Kummer's criterion is the same as p dividing the class number of ℚ(ζp). Ribet (1976) used a congruence of this kind, between an Eisenstein series and a cusp form, to prove the converse of Herbrand's theorem: p dividing Bk forces a specific piece of that class group to be nontrivial.

Lehmer's conjecture and open questions

Lehmer (1947) asked whether τ(n) = 0 for any n ≥ 1. τ(n) ≠ 0 has been verified for all n below about 8.16 × 1023 (Derickx, van Hoeij and Zeng, 2013), but the question is still open.

Deligne's bound |τ(p)| ≤ 2p11/2 implies |τ(n)| ≤ d(n) · n11/2 where d(n) is the divisor count. So τ(n) grows roughly as n11/2 = n(k−1)/2.

The Sato–Tate conjecture (theorem, Barnet-Lamb–Geraghty–Harris–Taylor 2011) says the normalised values τ(p)/(2p11/2) are equidistributed on [−1, 1] with respect to (2/π)√(1 − t²) dt, the distribution of half the trace of a random SU(2) matrix.

Part 8 develops the Hecke operators behind Mordell's proof. Δ is an eigenform of every one of them, and the multiplicativity of τ follows from that.
