Eisenstein Series and q-Expansions

Part 7 of 15. Ek(τ) = ½ Σ 1/(mτ+n)k. Converges for k ≥ 4. The q-expansion has integer coefficients involving divisor sums σk−1(n).

We have the stage (the upper half-plane), the symmetry group (SL2(ℤ)), and the definition of a modular form: a holomorphic function on ℍ that transforms with weight k under the modular group and is bounded at the cusp. Now we need examples. The Eisenstein series are the simplest non-trivial modular forms we can write down, and they arise from the most direct idea imaginable: sum a weight-k expression over the entire lattice.

The lattice-sum definition

Fix an even integer k ≥ 4. The Eisenstein series of weight k is defined by summing over all nonzero points of the lattice ℤ + τℤ:

Here the sum runs over all pairs (m, n) ∈ ℤ² with (m, n) ≠ (0, 0). Each term contributes 1/(mτ + n)k, a complex number whose magnitude is 1/|mτ + n|k. The factor ½ accounts for the pairing (m, n) ↔ (−m, −n), which produces the same value when k is even.

The lattice perspective makes the definition feel natural. Think of the lattice Λτ = ℤ + τℤ as an infinite grid of points in the complex plane. Ek(τ) is a kind of "potential" that measures how the lattice responds to a weight-k probe placed at the origin: each lattice point contributes inversely as the k-th power of its distance from zero. Points close to the origin dominate the sum; distant points contribute negligibly.

Absolute convergence

The sum converges absolutely for k ≥ 4 but not for k = 2. The reason is geometric. In a lattice, the number of points at distance roughly R from the origin grows like R (there are about 2πR/area points in an annulus of width 1 at radius R). Each contributes roughly 1/Rk to the sum, so the total contribution from the annulus is about R · R−k = R1−k. This is summable over R = 1, 2, 3, ... precisely when 1 − k < −1, i.e. k > 2. For k = 2 the sum diverges (conditionally convergent, but not absolutely), and the theory of weight-2 Eisenstein series requires more care.

The comparison with the Riemann zeta function is instructive. Restricting to the "real axis" of the lattice (m = 0, n ≠ 0) gives terms 1/nk, whose sum is 2ζ(k). This converges for k ≥ 2, but the full two-dimensional sum has more terms to control. The two-dimensional version needs k ≥ 4 because the lattice is two-dimensional: roughly, each "ring" at distance R contributes O(R) points, each of size O(R−k), so the convergence condition is ∑ R1−k < ∞, which holds iff k > 2. The borderline case k = 2 requires Eisenstein summation (summing in a specific order), and the resulting E2 is only a "quasi-modular" form — it picks up an extra correction term under modular transformations.

Since Ek is built by summing over all (m, n), and an SL2(ℤ) transformation merely permutes the lattice points, the sum is automatically invariant under the modular group. That is the beauty of the lattice-sum construction: modularity is built in from the start. One checks that Ek is holomorphic on ℍ and bounded at the cusp, so it is a modular form of weight k.

For odd k, the Eisenstein series vanishes identically. Each term (mτ + n)−k pairs with (−mτ − n)−k = −(mτ + n)−k when k is odd, so the two cancel. This is consistent with the general theory: there are no modular forms of odd weight for the full modular group SL2(ℤ).

E4: the first Eisenstein series

E4 is the modular form of lowest weight (for the full modular group, the space M2 is trivially zero). Since dim M4 = 1, every weight-4 modular form is a scalar multiple of E4. It is, in a precise sense, the simplest modular form that exists.

E6: weight-6 Eisenstein series

The space M6 is also one-dimensional, spanned by E6. Together, E4 and E6 generate the entire graded ring of modular forms: every modular form of every weight is a polynomial in E4 and E6. This remarkable fact means that Eisenstein series are not merely examples — they are the atoms from which all modular forms are built.

Now the coefficient of qn is −504 σ5(n). These grow quickly: σ5(2) = 1 + 32 = 33, so the second coefficient is −504 · 33 = −16 632.

Notice that E4 has all positive coefficients, while E6 alternates. Both have integer coefficients, a fact that is not at all obvious from the lattice-sum definition and which has deep number-theoretic significance.

Higher-weight Eisenstein series are not independent. In fact, E8 = E42: the weight-8 Eisenstein series equals the square of the weight-4 series, coefficient by coefficient. This is because the space M8 of weight-8 modular forms is one-dimensional, so E8 must be proportional to the only available monomial E42, and matching constant terms forces equality. This purely arithmetic identity, which would be extremely difficult to prove by elementary means, falls out immediately from the theory of modular forms. We simply observe that two modular forms of the same weight with the same leading coefficient must be identical, and read off the coefficient identity. This is one of the first payoffs of the theory: structural insights into number theory for free.

Divisor sums

The divisor sum function σk(n) is the sum of the k-th powers of all positive divisors of n:

For instance, the divisors of 12 are {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12}, so σ3(12) = 1 + 8 + 27 + 64 + 216 + 1728 = 2044. When k = 0, σ0(n) simply counts the number of divisors. When k = 1, σ1(n) is the sum of divisors. For the Eisenstein series Ek, the relevant divisor sum is σk−1(n).

A crucial property: σk is multiplicative. If gcd(m, n) = 1, then σk(mn) = σk(m) · σk(n). This follows from the bijection between divisors of mn and pairs (d1, d2) where d1 | m and d2 | n. Multiplicativity means that to compute σk(n) for any n, it suffices to know it at prime powers and then multiply. For prime powers, the formula simplifies to a geometric sum:

When n = p is prime, the only divisors are 1 and p, so σk(p) = 1 + pk. This means the coefficient of qp in Ek is ck(1 + pk−1), which grows like pk−1. Why integer coefficients? The q-expansion coefficients of Ek are integers because ck = −2k/Bk is always an integer (a fact about Bernoulli numbers tied to the von Staudt–Clausen theorem), and σk−1(n) is an integer by definition. This integrality is a prerequisite for the whole arithmetic theory of modular forms: the ring of modular forms over ℤ is generated by E4 and E6.

Experiment with the figure. When τ is near the real axis (Im τ small), the lattice is squeezed vertically and many points cluster near the origin, making the sum large. When τ = i, the lattice is square and the sum is smallest by symmetry. For large Im τ, the lattice stretches vertically, the nearest lattice points are the integers n ≠ 0 on the real axis, and Ek(τ) approaches its constant term 1 (all qn terms are exponentially suppressed because |q| = e−2π Im τ is small).

The status bar compares two values: the partial lattice sum (computed by direct summation over lattice points up to the chosen range) and the q-expansion value (computed from the Fourier series). As you increase the range, the lattice sum converges toward the q-expansion value. The q-expansion typically converges faster because each qn term already bundles infinitely many lattice contributions together.

Try setting τ = ρ = e2πi/3 (roughly −0.5 + 0.87i). The lattice Λρ is the hexagonal lattice, maximally symmetric. At this point E6(ρ) = 0 — the hexagonal lattice is a zero of the weight-6 Eisenstein series, a fact we will exploit when building the discriminant function in the next explainer.

Convergence speed and weight. Higher weight means faster convergence. At weight 4, distant lattice points still contribute noticeably; at weight 10, only the nearest neighbours matter. This is visible in the figure: increase k and watch the colour concentrate near the origin. Mathematically, the tail of the sum is O(R1−k), so each additional unit of weight buys another power of decay.

From lattice sum to q-expansion: two views of the same function

The lattice-sum definition and the q-expansion are two different representations of the same function. The lattice sum makes modularity obvious (SL2(ℤ) just permutes the lattice) but hides the arithmetic. The q-expansion makes the arithmetic explicit (divisor sums, integrality, multiplicativity) but disguises the modularity. Moving between these two views is a recurring theme in the theory of modular forms, and the Lipschitz summation formula is the bridge. In later explainers we will see the same duality for the discriminant, the j-function, and L-functions: each has a "geometric" definition and an "arithmetic" expansion, and the deepest results come from playing the two off against each other.

What we take with us

  • Ek(τ) is defined as a sum over the lattice ℤ + τℤ, converging absolutely for k ≥ 4.
  • The q-expansion is Ek = 1 + ck ∑ σk−1(n) qn, with c4 = 240, c6 = −504.
  • Divisor sums σk(n) = ∑d|n dk are the building blocks of Eisenstein coefficients.
  • All coefficients are integers. The ring of modular forms is generated by E4 and E6 over ℤ.
  • E8 = E42 because M8 is one-dimensional, giving non-trivial identities among divisor sums.
  • In the next explainer, we use E4 and E6 to build the discriminant Δ = (E43 − E62)/1728, the first cusp form, whose coefficients are the Ramanujan τ-function.
