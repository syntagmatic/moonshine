The Modularity Theorem and Elliptic Curves

Part 12 of 20. Every elliptic curve E/ℚ with conductor N arises from a weight-2 newform f ∈ S₂(Γ₀(N)): L(s,E) = L(s,f). Wiles' proof (1995) implies Fermat's Last Theorem.

This is where everything converges. The Modularity Theorem reveals that two completely different areas of math—the geometric world of modular forms and the algebraic world of elliptic curves—are actually identical.

Every elliptic curve generates a sequence of numbers (called Frobenius traces) by counting points on the curve for different prime numbers. The Modularity Theorem states that this sequence of numbers perfectly matches the coefficients of a modular form. A deeply complex algebraic equation is entirely controlled by a geometric fundamental domain.

The modularity theorem

Every elliptic curve over ℚ has an L-function that is already a modular L-function:

Modularity theorem. For every elliptic curve E/ℚ of conductor N, there exists a weight-2 newform f ∈ S₂(Γ₀(N)) such that ap(f) = ap(E) for all primes p of good reduction. Equivalently, L(s, E) = L(s, f).

The conductor N measures E's bad behaviour at each prime. Good primes contribute nothing; bad primes contribute pk. N is the same level appearing in Γ₀(N) of the associated modular form.

History: Taniyama (1955) suggested the link. Shimura (1964) refined it for CM curves. Weil (1967) added the functional-equation condition. Then:

  • 1995: Andrew Wiles, with a key contribution from Richard Taylor, proved modularity for all semistable elliptic curves over ℚ.
  • 2001: Breuil, Conrad, Diamond, and Taylor (BCDT) extended the proof to all elliptic curves over ℚ.

"ap(f) = ap(E) for all good p" says cusp form Fourier coefficients are point counts. Complex analysis on ℍ and arithmetic geometry over finite fields are the same.

Consequence: Fermat's Last Theorem

The most famous consequence is Fermat's Last Theorem: no positive integer solutions to an + bn = cn for n ≥ 3. The logical chain:

Suppose ap + bp = cp for prime p ≥ 5. Frey (1984) observed that the Frey curve

has discriminant involving (abc)2p and conductor the radical of abc. Frey and Serre argued this curve cannot be modular.

Ribet (1990) proved Serre's conjecture: if the Frey curve were modular of level N, level-lowering would reduce to N = 2. But S₂(Γ₀(2)) = 0 (X₀(2) has genus 0), so the Frey curve cannot be modular.

Wiles: every semistable elliptic curve over ℚ is modular, and the Frey curve is semistable. Contradiction. Chain: Fermat solution → Frey curve → Wiles modularity → Ribet level-lowering → S₂(Γ₀(2)) = 0 → contradiction.

The zero-dimensional argument. Fermat's Last Theorem ultimately rests on dim S₂(Γ₀(2)) = 0 — a 358-year-old problem reduced to a zero-dimensional vector space.

Takeaways

  • The Modularity Theorem: Every elliptic curve perfectly matches a weight-2 modular form. The sequence of numbers generated by the curve (its point counts) are exactly the Fourier coefficients of the form.
  • Two Worlds Combine: This theorem bridges algebraic geometry (counting solutions to equations) with complex analysis (the symmetric geometry of the upper half-plane).
  • Fermat's Last Theorem: Because all elliptic curves must be modular, and a hypothetical solution to Fermat's equation would create an elliptic curve that *cannot* be modular, Fermat's Last Theorem is proven true.
