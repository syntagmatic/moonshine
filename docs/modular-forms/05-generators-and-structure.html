Generators, Relations, and the Modular Group's Structure

Part 5 of 20. PSL₂(ℤ) = ⟨S, T | S² = (ST)³ = 1⟩. Elliptic points i (order 2) and ρ = e2πi/3 (order 3) are orbifold singularities. The finite area of the fundamental domain explains why Mk is finite-dimensional.

Every element of PSL₂(ℤ) is a product of two generators S and T subject to two relations. This algebraic structure marks two elliptic points in F where the group action has non-trivial stabilisers. The combination of these stabilisers and F's finite area forces Mk to be finite-dimensional.

Elliptic points

Most points τ ∈ F have a trivial stabiliser: the only element of PSL₂(ℤ) fixing τ is the identity. But two special points break this rule.

The point i. Apply S to τ = i: we get S · i = −1/i = i. So S fixes i, and the stabiliser of i in PSL₂(ℤ) is {I, S} ≅ ℤ/2. The point i sits on the unit-circle boundary arc of F, exactly where the S-wall of the fundamental domain passes through.

The point ρ. Let ρ = e2πi/3 = (−1 + i√3)/2, the primitive cube root of unity in ℤ. Then ST · ρ = ρ, and (ST)² · ρ = ρ as well. The stabiliser is {I, ST, (ST)²} ≅ ℤ/3. The point ρ is the upper-left vertex of F, where the left edge meets the circular arc.

i and ρ are the only points in F with non-trivial stabilisers. In the quotient orbifold PSL₂(ℤ)\ℍ they become cone points with opening angles π and 2π/3, appearing as correction terms in the dimension formula.

Figure 2. Elliptic point visualiser. The fundamental domain F is shown with the two elliptic points i and ρ marked. Toggle between them to see the stabiliser action animated: S maps i to itself (a 180° rotation that looks trivial on ℍ), while ST cycles ρ through a 3-fold orbit that returns to ρ after three applications.

Why only two. γ ∈ PSL₂(ℤ) fixes a point in ℍ only if |tr γ| < 2 (elliptic). For integer matrices with determinant 1, |tr γ| ∈ {0, 1}: trace 0 gives S-conjugates (order 2, fixing i-conjugates); trace 1 gives ST-conjugates (order 3, fixing ρ-conjugates).

The word-length metric on PSL₂(ℤ) is a combinatorial shadow of hyperbolic geometry. Short words act near F; long words send points far. The Cayley graph is a trivalent-bivalent tree (the group is a free product), embedded in the hyperbolic plane.

Finite area forces finite-dimensionality. ℍ/PSL₂(ℤ) has area π/3, so its compactification is a compact Riemann surface. On compact surfaces, sections of any line bundle form a finite-dimensional space (Riemann–Roch). The weight-k law defines a line bundle whose global sections are Mk.

Takeaways

  • Generators: PSL₂(ℤ) = ⟨S, T | S² = (ST)³ = 1⟩ ≅ ℤ/2 * ℤ/3. Every element is a word in S and T.
  • Elliptic points: i (stabiliser ≅ ℤ/2, from S) and ρ = e2πi/3 (stabiliser ≅ ℤ/3, from ST) are the only orbifold singularities.
  • Finite area: area(F) = π/3. This forces Mk to be finite-dimensional via Riemann–Roch on the compactified quotient.
  • Dimension formula: dim Mk = ⌊k/12⌋ + corrections from the elliptic points and cusp.
