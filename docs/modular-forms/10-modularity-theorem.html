The Modularity Theorem and Elliptic Curves

Part 10 of 14. An elliptic curve over ℚ can be studied one prime at a time by counting its points mod p. The modularity theorem says those counts are encoded in the Fourier coefficients of a weight-2 cusp form whose level is the curve's conductor. Wiles' 1995 proof of the semistable case, together with Ribet's theorem, proves Fermat's Last Theorem.

Point counting ties an elliptic curve to a sequence of numbers: for each prime p, count the solutions mod p and record how far the count is from p + 1. These are the Frobenius traces ap. The modularity theorem says that for every elliptic curve over ℚ, this sequence is the list of Fourier coefficients of a weight-2 cusp form of the kind built in the previous part.

The modularity theorem

Modularity theorem. For every elliptic curve E/ℚ of conductor N, there exists a weight-2 newform f ∈ S₂(Γ₀(N)) such that ap(f) = ap(E) for all primes p of good reduction. Equivalently, L(s, E) = L(s, f).

The conductor N measures how badly E behaves at each prime. Good primes contribute nothing. A bad prime contributes p when the reduction is multiplicative (a node) and p2 when it is additive (a cusp), except that the exponent can reach 5 at p = 3 and 8 at p = 2. It is the same N as the level of Γ₀(N) for the associated form.

Taniyama raised the question in a problem list at the 1955 Tokyo–Nikko symposium, in a form not quite correct as stated. Shimura put it in its modern shape for curves over ℚ in conversations with Serre and Weil in the early 1960s. Weil's 1967 paper proved a converse theorem and made the level of the form equal to the conductor of the curve, which is why all three names are attached to the conjecture. Some cases were already theorems: Shimura showed in 1971 that curves over ℚ with complex multiplication are modular, building on Deuring's description of their L-functions as Hecke L-functions. Then:

  • 1995: Andrew Wiles, with a key contribution from Richard Taylor, proved modularity for all semistable elliptic curves over ℚ.
  • 2001: Breuil, Conrad, Diamond, and Taylor (BCDT) extended the proof to all elliptic curves over ℚ.

The equation ap(f) = ap(E) says that Fourier coefficients of a cusp form, which come from complex analysis on ℍ, are point counts over finite fields.

Fermat's Last Theorem

The most famous consequence is Fermat's Last Theorem: an + bn = cn has no solutions in positive integers for n ≥ 3.

Every n ≥ 3 is a multiple of 4 or of an odd prime, so it is enough to rule out n = 4 and odd primes. Fermat's own infinite-descent argument settles n = 4. Euler published a proof for n = 3 in 1770; one step had a gap, repaired from Euler's other work, and independent proofs followed from Kausler (1802) and Legendre (1823). That leaves primes p ≥ 5.

Suppose ap + bp = cp with a, b, c coprime and p ≥ 5 prime. Reorder and change signs so that b is even and a ≡ −1 (mod 4). Frey, in an Oberwolfach lecture published in 1986, observed that the Frey curve

is semistable, with minimal discriminant (abc)2p/28 and conductor N = rad(abc), the product of the primes dividing abc. Frey suggested this curve could not be modular, and Serre (1987) formulated the conjectures that would prove it.

Ribet proved the level-lowering case Serre needed, known as the epsilon conjecture, in 1986 (published in Inventiones in 1990): if the Frey curve were modular of level N = rad(abc), its mod-p representation would also come from a newform of level 2. Every odd prime of N drops out because p divides the exponent of that prime in the discriminant; 2 stays because its exponent, 2p·v2(abc) − 8, is not a multiple of p. (Serre's full modularity conjecture for mod-p representations is a separate and much stronger statement, proved by Khare and Wintenberger in 2009.) But S₂(Γ₀(2)) = 0, because X₀(2) has genus 0, so the Frey curve cannot be modular.

Wiles proved that every semistable elliptic curve over ℚ is modular, and the Frey curve is semistable. So a Fermat solution would give a modular Frey curve. Ribet's theorem then says its mod-p representation comes from a weight-2 newform of level 2, and there is none, since S₂(Γ₀(2)) = 0.
