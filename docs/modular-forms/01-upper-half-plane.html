The Upper Half-Plane and Hyperbolic Geometry

Part 1 of 20. The natural home of modular forms: the upper half of the complex plane, equipped with a metric that makes it a model of the hyperbolic plane. Everything in this series lives here.

Modular forms are functions on a specific space: the upper half-plane ℍ = {τ ∈ ℂ : Im τ > 0}. This is the open upper half of the complex plane, the set of all complex numbers with strictly positive imaginary part. It is an open, connected, simply connected region in ℂ, and as a smooth manifold it is diffeomorphic to ℝ². None of that is interesting yet. What makes ℍ remarkable is the metric it carries.

The Poincaré metric on ℍ turns it into a model of the hyperbolic plane, a complete Riemannian surface with constant Gaussian curvature −1. In this metric, distances grow as you approach the real axis (the "boundary at infinity"), geodesics are semicircles orthogonal to the real axis or vertical lines, and the group of orientation-preserving isometries is PSL₂(ℝ), the group of fractional linear transformations with real coefficients. This geometry is not merely background decoration: the transformation law of modular forms is written in terms of these isometries, and the finiteness of the fundamental domain's hyperbolic area is what forces the space of weight-k forms to be finite-dimensional.

The Poincaré metric

At each point τ ∈ ℍ, the Poincaré metric tensor is

This says: the infinitesimal distance between τ and τ + dτ is |dτ|/Im τ. Near the real axis, where Im τ is small, the metric blows up, distances become enormous, and a small Euclidean step becomes a large hyperbolic step. High in the half-plane, where Im τ is large, the metric shrinks, and Euclidean distances approximate hyperbolic ones. The boundary Im τ = 0 is infinitely far away in the Poincaré metric, it is not part of ℍ but an ideal boundary at infinity.

Geodesics

A geodesic in ℍ is a curve of locally shortest length. The geodesics of the Poincaré metric are exactly two kinds of curves:

  1. Vertical lines {Re τ = c} for any constant c ∈ ℝ.
  2. Semicircles with centre on the real axis and orthogonal to it.

No other curves are geodesics. In particular, horizontal lines (which are geodesics in Euclidean geometry) are not geodesics in ℍ. The shortest path from τ₁ to τ₂ in the Poincaré metric bends upward, away from the real axis, because the metric is cheaper (distances are smaller) at greater heights.

Why "upward" is cheaper. The metric ds = |dτ|/Im τ divides by height. Travelling at height y = 2 costs half as much per unit Euclidean distance as travelling at height y = 1. So the geodesic detours upward to accumulate less total cost, even though the Euclidean path length is longer. This is the defining feature of hyperbolic geometry: shortest paths curve away from the boundary.

Isometries: PSL₂(ℝ)

An isometry of (ℍ, ds²) is a diffeomorphism that preserves the metric. The orientation-preserving isometries of ℍ form the group PSL₂(ℝ) = SL₂(ℝ)/{±I}, acting by Möbius transformations:

This map sends ℍ to ℍ (the imaginary part of γ · τ is Im τ / |cτ + d|², which is positive), and it preserves the Poincaré metric. PSL₂(ℝ) is the full group of orientation-preserving isometries, just as the Euclidean group of translations and rotations is the isometry group of the flat plane. The crucial difference is that PSL₂(ℝ) is 3-dimensional (matching the 3 real parameters in a 2×2 matrix of determinant 1, modulo sign), while the Euclidean isometry group is also 3-dimensional (2 translations, 1 rotation). In the next explainer we will restrict from PSL₂(ℝ) to the discrete subgroup PSL₂(ℤ), and that restriction is where number theory enters the picture.

The Poincaré disk model

There is a second standard model of the hyperbolic plane: the Poincaré disk 𝔻 = {w ∈ ℂ : |w| < 1}, with metric ds² = 4|dw|²/(1 − |w|²)². The two models are related by the Cayley transform

which is itself a Möbius transformation. Under this map, the real axis of ℍ goes to the unit circle ∂𝔻, the point i ∈ ℍ goes to 0 ∈ 𝔻, and geodesics in ℍ (vertical lines and semicircles) map to geodesics in 𝔻 (diameters and circular arcs orthogonal to ∂𝔻). The two models carry the same geometry; the disk model is sometimes more convenient for visualisation because the boundary is compact.

Why this geometry matters for modular forms

The Poincaré metric on ℍ matters for modular forms because the transformation law f(γτ) = (cτ+d)k f(τ) is written in terms of the same group PSL₂(ℝ) (or its discrete subgroup PSL₂(ℤ)) that acts as isometries. The weight factor (cτ+d)k is the automorphy factor, a cocycle that couples the value of f at one point to its value at any equivalent point under the group action. The entire classical theory of modular forms is an interplay between the complex analysis of functions on ℍ and the Riemannian geometry of the Poincaré metric. The next four explainers build up the group-theoretic and geometric machinery; beginning in explainer 06, we put functions on this stage and ask what the transformation law forces them to be.

Takeaways

  • ℍ = {τ ∈ ℂ : Im τ > 0} is the upper half-plane, equipped with the Poincaré metric ds² = |dτ|²/(Im τ)².
  • The metric has constant curvature −1. Distances blow up near the real axis and shrink at height.
  • Geodesics are vertical lines and semicircles orthogonal to ℝ.
  • PSL₂(ℝ) is the full orientation-preserving isometry group, acting by τ ↦ (aτ+b)/(cτ+d).
  • The Poincaré disk is an equivalent model; the Cayley transform w = (τ−i)/(τ+i) maps between the two.
