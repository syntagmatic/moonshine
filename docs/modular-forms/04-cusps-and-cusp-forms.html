Cusps and the Cusp-Form Condition

Part 4 of 15. Cusps are the rational points ℚ ∪ {∞} on the boundary of ℍ, all equivalent under SL₂(ℤ). A modular form must be holomorphic at cusps; a cusp form must vanish there.

We have built a stage (the upper half-plane ℍ), a group acting on it (SL₂(ℤ)), and a fundamental domain that tiles ℍ under that action. But the fundamental domain is not compact: it has a spike running off to i∞. The boundary of ℍ is the real line, and among all real numbers the rational ones play a special role. These rational boundary points, together with the point at infinity, are the cusps. Understanding their behaviour is essential before we can define modular forms, because the definition demands holomorphicity not just on ℍ itself but also at the cusps.

What is a cusp?

The cusps of SL₂(ℤ) are the points of ℚ ∪ {i∞} on the boundary of ℍ. These are not points in ℍ (the real axis is infinitely far away in the Poincaré metric), but they are limit points of the fundamental domain. The cusp i∞ is the top of the spike: trajectories along the vertical line Re τ = 0 go to i∞ as Im τ → ∞. Every rational number p/q is also a cusp, approached by geodesics descending toward the real axis.

The key fact is that SL₂(ℤ) acts transitively on ℚ ∪ {i∞}: given any p/q ∈ ℚ (in lowest terms), we can find a matrix

with ad − bc = 1 that sends i∞ to p/q, since γ · i∞ = a/c = p/q. Finding such a, b, c, d amounts to the extended Euclidean algorithm. This means that all cusps are equivalent under SL₂(ℤ). For the full modular group, there is exactly one cusp up to equivalence, and we may always take it to be i∞.

One cusp, infinitely many representatives. The rational numbers 0, 1, 1/2, 3/7, −5/3, ... are all distinct points on the real line, but as cusps of SL₂(ℤ) they are all equivalent to i∞. The quotient SL₂(ℤ)\ℍ* (where ℍ* = ℍ ∪ ℚ ∪ {i∞}) is a compact Riemann surface of genus 0 with a single cusp. For congruence subgroups Γ₀(N) the situation changes: not all rationals are equivalent, and the number of inequivalent cusps grows with N.

The q-expansion

The map τ ↦ q = e2πiτ sends the half-plane ℍ to the punctured unit disk {0 < |q| < 1}: if τ = x + iy with y > 0, then |q| = e−2πy < 1, and as Im τ → ∞ we get q → 0. The periodicity f(τ + 1) = f(τ) means f is really a function of q, and we can write

This is the q-expansion (or Fourier expansion at the cusp). It is a Laurent series in q. The behaviour at the cusp i∞ corresponds to the behaviour at q = 0:

  • Holomorphic at the cusp: a(n) = 0 for all n < 0. No pole at q = 0. This is part of the definition of a modular form.
  • Vanishes at the cusp: additionally a(0) = 0. The function goes to zero as τ → i∞. This defines a cusp form.

The distinction matters enormously. Eisenstein series have a(0) ≠ 0 (their constant term is 1 after normalisation). The discriminant Δ(τ) = q − 24q² + 252q³ − ... has a(0) = 0 and is the prototypical cusp form. Cusp forms form a subspace Sk ⊂ Mk, and the quotient Mk/Sk is spanned by Eisenstein series.

Convergence of the q-expansion improves as Im τ grows. Since |q| = e−2π Im τ, large imaginary parts mean |q| is tiny, and even a few terms of the series give an excellent approximation. Near the real axis, |q| approaches 1, and convergence is slow.

Cusp equivalence

We claimed that every rational p/q is equivalent to i∞ under SL₂(ℤ). The figure below demonstrates this concretely: enter two rationals and see the SL₂(ℤ) matrix that maps one to the other, computed via the extended Euclidean algorithm. Since each rational is equivalent to i∞, any two rationals are equivalent to each other by composing the two maps through i∞.

Figure 1. Cusp equivalence under SL₂(ℤ). Two rational cusps on the real axis and the matrix γ that maps one to the other. The arc shows the geodesic connecting them through ℍ. All rational cusps (and i∞) are equivalent: there is one cusp orbit.

The extended Euclidean algorithm is SL₂(ℤ) in disguise. Given p/q in lowest terms, the extended Euclidean algorithm finds integers a, b with ap + bq = 1. Then the matrix [[p, −b],[q, a]] has determinant pa − q(−b) = pa + qb = 1, so it lies in SL₂(ℤ), and it sends i∞ to p/q. The algorithm that every undergraduate meets in a first number-theory course is secretly computing elements of the modular group.

What we take with us

  • Cusps are the rational points ℚ ∪ {i∞} on the boundary of ℍ.
  • Under SL₂(ℤ), all cusps are equivalent. There is one cusp orbit for the full modular group.
  • The stabiliser of i∞ is generated by T: τ ↦ τ + 1, forcing periodicity and a Fourier expansion in q = e2πiτ.
  • A modular form has a(n) = 0 for n < 0 (holomorphic at the cusp). A cusp form additionally has a(0) = 0 (vanishes at the cusp).
  • Convergence of the q-expansion improves exponentially with Im τ: |q| = e−2π Im τ.
