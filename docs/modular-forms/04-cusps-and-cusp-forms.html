Cusps and the Cusp-Form Condition

Part 4 of 20. Cusps are the rational points ℚ ∪ {∞} on the boundary of ℍ, all equivalent under SL₂(ℤ). A modular form must be holomorphic at cusps; a cusp form must vanish there.

The fundamental domain is not a closed, compact shape: it has a spike that shoots infinitely far up. The "points at infinity" where these spikes eventually end up are called cusps. Every modular form must be well-behaved at these infinitely distant points.

What is a cusp?

The cusps of SL₂(ℤ) are the points of ℚ ∪ {i∞} on the boundary of ℍ — limit points, not points in ℍ. i∞ is the top of the spike; every rational p/q is a cusp approached by geodesics descending to the real axis.

One cusp, many representatives. Rationals 0, 1, 1/2, 3/7, −5/3 are all equivalent as cusps. ℍ*/SL₂(ℤ) is a compact Riemann surface of genus 0 with one cusp. Congruence subgroups Γ₀(N) have more inequivalent cusps as N grows.

The q-expansion

τ ↦ q = e2πiτ sends ℍ to the punctured disk {0 < |q| < 1}: |q| = e−2πy, and Im τ → ∞ gives q → 0. Periodicity f(τ + 1) = f(τ) makes f a function of q:

The q-expansion is a Laurent series in q. Behaviour at i∞ is behaviour at q = 0:

  • Holomorphic at the cusp: a(n) = 0 for all n < 0. No pole at q = 0. This is part of the definition of a modular form.
  • Vanishes at the cusp: additionally a(0) = 0. The function goes to zero as τ → i∞. This defines a cusp form.

Eisenstein series have a(0) ≠ 0 (constant term 1 after normalisation); the discriminant Δ(τ) = q − 24q² + 252q³ − ... has a(0) = 0 and is the prototypical cusp form. Sk ⊂ Mk, and Mk/Sk is spanned by Eisenstein series.

Convergence improves with Im τ. Large imaginary parts mean |q| is tiny; near the real axis convergence is slow.

Cusp equivalence

Every rational p/q is equivalent to i∞, so any two rationals are equivalent to each other by composing through i∞. The connecting matrix comes from the extended Euclidean algorithm.

The extended Euclidean algorithm is SL₂(ℤ) in disguise. Given p/q in lowest terms, the algorithm finds a, b with ap + bq = 1. Then [[p, −b],[q, a]] has determinant 1, lies in SL₂(ℤ), and sends i∞ to p/q.

Takeaways

  • Points at infinity: Cusps are the rational points on the real line, plus the point at infinity.
  • It's all one point: The modular group can map any cusp to any other cusp. To the modular group, there is only one unique cusp in the entire space.
  • The q-expansion: Because the fundamental domain repeats horizontally, functions on it are periodic and can be written as Fourier series (q-expansions).
  • The Cusp Form rule: A modular form is just well-behaved (doesn't blow up) at the cusp. A cusp form actually goes completely to zero at the cusp.
