The Fundamental Domain and Its Boundaries

Part 3 of 14. F = {τ ∈ ℍ : |Re τ| ≤ 1/2, |τ| ≥ 1}. Left and right edges identified by T, arc identified by S. SL₂(ℤ)-translates tile ℍ. The quotient ℍ/SL₂(ℤ) is a modular curve of genus 0.

Part 2 left us with the orbits of SL₂(ℤ) acting on ℍ. A fundamental domain is a region that contains one representative of each orbit, apart from some identifications along its edges. Its images under the group tile ℍ, so a function that respects the group is determined by what it does on one tile.

The fundamental domain F

The standard fundamental domain for SL₂(ℤ) is the set

F has three boundary pieces: the vertical edges Re τ = ±1/2 with Im τ ≥ √3/2, and the arc |τ| = 1 from ρ = e2πi/3 to −ρ̄ = 1/2 + (√3/2)i, with its apex at i. T: τ ↦ τ + 1 identifies the two vertical edges, and S: τ ↦ −1/τ identifies the two halves of the arc.

Every τ ∈ ℍ is equivalent to a point of F, and distinct interior points of F are inequivalent. The first half has a constructive proof, the reduction algorithm of Figure 2 below. The hyperbolic area of F is finite:

Finite area is what makes spaces of modular forms finite-dimensional. A modular form is determined by its values on F, and once the cusp is added F becomes compact, which leaves only finitely many degrees of freedom.

The tiling of ℍ

As γ ranges over SL₂(ℤ), the images γ · F cover ℍ with no gaps, and two of them overlap only along boundaries. Each γ is an isometry, so every tile has the same area, angles and shape as F. The tiles look different only in Euclidean coordinates, where those near the real axis shrink without bound.

Boundary identifications and the quotient

Gluing F's identified edges gives the quotient space ℍ/SL₂(ℤ). Gluing the vertical edges with T makes a cylinder, and folding the bottom arc in half with S closes off its bottom. The result is a sphere with one puncture, the cusp at i∞.

Compactifying by adding the cusp gives the modular curve ℍ*/SL₂(ℤ) ≅ ℙ1(ℂ), the Riemann sphere. The j-function of Part 11 realises this isomorphism explicitly.

The quotient is an orbifold rather than a smooth surface because of the two elliptic points from Part 2: i, with stabiliser of order 2 in PSL₂(ℤ), and ρ, with stabiliser of order 3.

The fundamental domain F is a hyperbolic triangle with vertices at ρ, −ρ̄, and i∞, with interior angles π/3, π/3, and 0 (the cusp angle). The angle sum is 2π/3, and the Gauss-Bonnet theorem gives area = π − (π/3 + π/3 + 0) = π/3, confirming the formula above. After boundary identifications, ρ and −ρ̄ merge into a single orbifold point with cone angle 2π/3, and the two sides meeting at i∞ merge into the cusp.

Drag and reduce

Given τ ∈ ℍ, the reduction algorithm alternates T or T⁻¹, which shifts Re τ into [−1/2, 1/2], with S, which pushes |τ| above 1. On |τ| < 1, S increases Im τ, so the process terminates.

Figure 2. Drag τ (blue point) anywhere in ℍ. Orange segments are T-translations and purple segments are S-inversions; together they carry τ into F.

Points high in ℍ need at most one T-shift. Points near the real axis can need many rounds, and the count grows as τ gets closer to the axis.

Domain colouring and the phase portrait

The tessellation shows the group. To see a function that respects it, colour each τ by the value of the j-function of Part 11, the Hauptmodul for ℍ/SL₂(ℤ). Its graph would live in ℝ4, so we use domain colouring. Hue gives the argument of j(τ), once around the colour wheel from 0 to 2π. Brightness gives log |j(τ)|, from black near the zero at ρ to white near the pole at the cusp.

For each pixel we reduce τ to F and sum the first ten terms of j(τ) = q−1 + 744 + 196884 q + ⋯. After reduction Im τ ≥ √3/2, so |q| = e−2π Im τ is small and the series converges fast.

Figure 3. Domain colouring of j(τ). The bright band at the top is the pole at the cusp, and the dark spots at tile corners are the zeros, at ρ and its images. The grid toggle overlays the tessellation.

Since j(γτ) = j(τ), every tile in Figure 3 carries the colouring of F, shrunk by a Möbius map, and the tile boundaries of Figure 1 are where the pattern repeats. The zeros at the copies of ρ cluster toward every rational point, and every tile has its own white cusp. Only weight-0 functions like j repeat exactly; a form of weight k picks up the factor (cτ+d)k from tile to tile.
