The Fundamental Domain and Its Boundaries

Part 3 of 20. F = {τ ∈ ℍ : |Re τ| ≤ 1/2, |τ| ≥ 1}. Left and right edges identified by T, arc identified by S. SL₂(ℤ)-translates tile ℍ. The quotient ℍ/SL₂(ℤ) is a modular curve of genus 0.

We have the stage (the upper half-plane ℍ with its Poincaré metric) and the actors (the modular group SL₂(ℤ) acting by Möbius transformations). Now we need a fundamental domain: a region in ℍ that contains exactly one representative from each orbit of the group action, so that we can study functions on the quotient ℍ/SL₂(ℤ) by studying functions on this single region. The fundamental domain for SL₂(ℤ) is one of the most recognisable shapes in mathematics.

The fundamental domain F

The standard fundamental domain for SL₂(ℤ) is the set

This region has three boundary pieces. The left edge is the vertical segment Re τ = −1/2 for Im τ ≥ √3/2. The right edge is the vertical segment Re τ = 1/2 for Im τ ≥ √3/2. The bottom arc is the circular arc |τ| = 1 connecting the two lower corners. The two corners themselves are the points ρ = e2πi/3 = −1/2 + (√3/2)i on the left and −ρ̄ = 1/2 + (√3/2)i on the right, together with the apex of the arc at i.

The left and right edges are identified by the translation T: τ ↦ τ + 1. A point on the left edge at Re τ = −1/2 maps to the corresponding point on the right edge at Re τ = 1/2 at the same height. The two halves of the bottom arc (left of i and right of i) are identified by the inversion S: τ ↦ −1/τ. A point τ on the arc with Re τ < 0 maps to the point −1/τ on the arc with Re τ > 0.

With these identifications, F is a fundamental domain for SL₂(ℤ): every point τ ∈ ℍ is SL₂(ℤ)-equivalent to at least one point in F, and no two interior points of F are equivalent. On the boundary, the identifications above are the only redundancies. The proof is constructive: given any τ ∈ ℍ, we can reduce it into F by alternately applying T (to shift Re τ into [−1/2, 1/2]) and S (to push |τ| above 1). This process terminates because each application of S increases Im τ when |τ| < 1.

This finiteness is what ultimately forces spaces of modular forms to be finite-dimensional: a holomorphic function satisfying the weight-k transformation law is determined by its behaviour on F, and F is compact enough (in the hyperbolic sense, after adding the cusp) that only finitely many degrees of freedom remain.

The tiling of ℍ

The SL₂(ℤ)-translates of F tile the entire upper half-plane: the images γ · F, as γ ranges over SL₂(ℤ), cover ℍ with no gaps and no overlaps (except on boundaries, which have measure zero).

Near the real axis, tiles crowd together and shrink in Euclidean size but have the same hyperbolic area π/3. Use the buttons to increase the tile count.

The visual effect is striking. Near i∞ (the top of the picture), F is wide and tall. But as we look closer to the real axis, the tiles become Euclidean-small and crowd together in a fractal-like pattern. This is exactly the hyperbolic geometry at work: all tiles have the same hyperbolic area π/3, but the Poincaré metric compresses Euclidean distances near the boundary. The real axis is infinitely far away in the hyperbolic metric, so the tessellation extends downward without bound.

Every tile is isometric to F. Because each γ ∈ SL₂(ℤ) is an isometry of the Poincaré metric, every tile γ · F has the same hyperbolic area, the same internal angles, and the same shape as F. The tiles only look different because we are drawing them in Euclidean coordinates. In the hyperbolic metric, the tessellation is perfectly regular.

Boundary identifications and the quotient

When we glue the identified boundary edges of F together, we obtain the quotient space ℍ/SL₂(ℤ). Topologically, the gluing works as follows. First, identifying the left and right edges (via T) turns F into a cylinder. Then, identifying the two halves of the bottom arc (via S) collapses the bottom of the cylinder to a point. The result is topologically a sphere with one puncture: a genus-0 surface missing a single point. The missing point corresponds to the cusp at i∞.

More precisely, we can compactify the quotient by adding the cusp, obtaining the completed modular curve ℍ*/SL₂(ℤ) ≅ ℙ1(ℂ), the Riemann sphere. The j-function (explored in explainer 13) realises this isomorphism explicitly: j maps the modular curve bijectively onto ℂ ∪ {∞}.

There are two special points in the quotient that are not smooth. The point τ = i is fixed by S (since S(i) = −1/i = i), so it has a stabiliser of order 2 in PSL₂(ℤ). The point τ = ρ = e2πi/3 is fixed by ST (since (ST)(ρ) = ρ), giving it a stabiliser of order 3. These are elliptic points, and they make the quotient an orbifold rather than a manifold. We will explore this further in explainer 05.

From hyperbolic polygon to Riemann sphere. The fundamental domain F is a hyperbolic triangle with vertices at ρ, −ρ̄, and i∞, with interior angles π/3, π/3, and 0 (the cusp angle). The angle sum is 2π/3, and the Gauss-Bonnet theorem gives area = π − (π/3 + π/3 + 0) = π/3, confirming the formula above. After boundary identifications, ρ and −ρ̄ merge into a single orbifold point with cone angle 2π/3, and the two sides meeting at i∞ merge into the cusp.

Drag and reduce

The reduction algorithm is the constructive proof that F is a fundamental domain. Given any τ ∈ ℍ, we alternate two operations: apply T or T⁻¹ to shift Re τ into [−1/2, 1/2], then apply S if |τ| < 1. Each application of S to a point with |τ| < 1 sends it to a point with |τ| > 1 (and in particular with larger imaginary part), so the process terminates in finitely many steps.

Figure 2. Drag τ (blue point) anywhere in ℍ. The reduction algorithm applies S and T steps (shown as coloured path segments) to bring τ into the fundamental domain F. Orange segments are T-translations; purple segments are S-inversions. The number of steps typically grows as τ approaches the real axis.

Notice that points high in the half-plane need at most one T-shift to land in F (they already satisfy |τ| ≥ 1). Points near the real axis, by contrast, may need many steps: the S-inversion launches them high, then they drift back down and may need another round of shifting and inverting. The algorithm is efficient in practice, but points with small imaginary part and large real part (deep inside a narrow tile near the real axis) can require many steps.

Domain colouring and the phase portrait

The tessellation shows the group SL₂(ℤ) at work on ℍ. To see a function at work on the same picture, we need to paint each point τ with a colour that encodes the output of some modular-invariant function. The canonical choice is the j-function (explainer 13), the unique Hauptmodul for ℍ/SL₂(ℤ). It is a complex-valued function of a complex variable, so its graph naturally lives in ℝ4. Since we only have two screen dimensions for the input, we give up on drawing the graph and encode the output as colour instead. This technique is called domain colouring.

The recipe is standard:

  • Hue encodes the argument of j(τ). Red at argument 0, cycling through orange, yellow, green, cyan, blue, magenta, and back to red at 2π.
  • Brightness encodes the modulus. Large |j| goes toward white (the pole at the cusp); small |j| goes toward black (the zero at ρ). A logarithmic scale keeps both visible.

To compute j(τ) at each pixel, we first reduce τ to F (where the q-expansion converges quickly), evaluate the first ten terms of the q-series j(τ) = q−1 + 744 + 196884 q + ⋯, and paint. For τ near the real axis, the reduction step lifts Im τ into F where |q| = e−2π Im τ is small and the series converges in a handful of terms.

Figure 3. Domain colouring of j(τ) on the upper half-plane. Hue = arg j(τ), brightness = log |j(τ)|. The bright band at the top is the pole (j → ∞ at the cusp). The dark spots at the corners of every tile are the zeros (j(ρ) = 0). The pattern is SL₂(ℤ)-periodic: every tile is a colour-identical copy of every other, because j takes the same values on corresponding points. The grid toggle overlays the tessellation for orientation.

What the picture shows

Three features of the domain-coloured image are worth noting explicitly.

Self-similarity. Every tile is a shrunken, Möbius-transformed copy of F, and the colouring within it is an identically transformed copy of the colouring in F. This self-similarity is not approximate: it is exact, forced by the modular invariance j(γτ) = j(τ).

The zeros at ρ. At the two lower corners of F, j = 0. In the domain-coloured picture, zeros show up as dark spots where all hues converge (because the argument of 0 is undefined). Every copy of ρ in the tessellation (and there are infinitely many, clustering toward every rational point on the real axis) produces another dark spot.

The pole at the cusp. At the top of F, j has a simple pole. In the picture, the cusp direction washes out to bright white as |j| → ∞. Every tile has its own cusp direction (a vertex that abuts the real line), and near that vertex the colouring goes white.

One picture, two stories. Figure 1 showed the action of SL₂(ℤ) as a tessellation. Figure 3 shows the invariance as a colouring. The two pictures agree at every point: the group boundaries in Figure 1 are exactly the loci where the colour pattern in Figure 3 repeats. Every modular form of weight 0 (like j) produces such a picture; forms of higher weight produce pictures that transform under a factor (cτ+d)k between tiles rather than being identical. We will return to this in explainers 07, 08, and 13.

Takeaways

  • F = {τ ∈ ℍ : |Re τ| ≤ 1/2, |τ| ≥ 1} is the standard fundamental domain for SL₂(ℤ). Every orbit has a unique representative in F (up to boundary identifications).
  • Boundary identifications: left/right edges by T, arc halves by S. Corners ρ and −ρ̄ merge; i is fixed by S.
  • The SL₂(ℤ)-translates of F tile ℍ with no gaps or overlaps. All tiles have hyperbolic area π/3.
  • The quotient ℍ/SL₂(ℤ) is a genus-0 surface (sphere minus the cusp at i∞). Adding the cusp gives ℙ1(ℂ).
  • The reduction algorithm (alternate T-shifts and S-inversions) constructively maps any τ into F in finitely many steps.
  • Domain colouring paints arg j(τ) as hue and |j(τ)| as brightness. The result is self-similar across tiles, with dark zeros at images of ρ and bright poles at images of the cusp, the invariance j(γτ) = j(τ) made visible.
