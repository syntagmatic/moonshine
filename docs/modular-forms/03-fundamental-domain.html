The Fundamental Domain and Its Boundaries

Part 3 of 20. F = {τ ∈ ℍ : |Re τ| ≤ 1/2, |τ| ≥ 1}. Left and right edges identified by T, arc identified by S. SL₂(ℤ)-translates tile ℍ. The quotient ℍ/SL₂(ℤ) is a modular curve of genus 0.

The hyperbolic plane is filled with an infinite number of identical tiles created by the modular group. A fundamental domain is just one of these tiles—a single region containing exactly one representative from every possible orbit. By studying this single region, the entire space can be understood.

The fundamental domain F

The standard fundamental domain for SL₂(ℤ) is the set

F has three boundary pieces. Left and right edges are vertical segments at Re τ = ±1/2 with Im τ ≥ √3/2. The bottom is the arc |τ| = 1 between the corners ρ = e2πi/3 and −ρ̄ = 1/2 + (√3/2)i, with apex at i.

T: τ ↦ τ + 1 identifies the left and right edges. S: τ ↦ −1/τ identifies the two halves of the bottom arc.

F is a fundamental domain: every τ ∈ ℍ is equivalent to one in F, and distinct interior points are inequivalent. The proof is constructive: alternate T (shifting Re τ into [−1/2, 1/2]) and S (pushing |τ| above 1). The process terminates because S increases Im τ when |τ| < 1.

The tiling of ℍ

The SL₂(ℤ)-translates of F tile the entire upper half-plane: the images γ · F, as γ ranges over SL₂(ℤ), cover ℍ with no gaps and no overlaps (except on boundaries, which have measure zero).

F is wide and tall near i∞; closer to the real axis tiles shrink and crowd. All tiles have hyperbolic area π/3, but the Poincaré metric compresses Euclidean distances near the boundary, so the tessellation extends downward without bound.

Every tile is isometric to F. γ · F has the same hyperbolic area, angles, and shape as F. The tiles only look different in Euclidean coordinates.

Boundary identifications and the quotient

Gluing F's identified edges gives the quotient space ℍ/SL₂(ℤ). Identifying left and right edges via T makes a cylinder; identifying the two halves of the bottom arc via S collapses the cylinder's bottom to a point. The result is a sphere with one puncture, the cusp at i∞.

Compactifying by adding the cusp gives the modular curve ℍ*/SL₂(ℤ) ≅ ℙ1(ℂ), the Riemann sphere. The j-function (explainer 13) realises this isomorphism explicitly.

Two non-smooth points: τ = i is fixed by S (stabiliser order 2 in PSL₂(ℤ)); τ = ρ = e2πi/3 is fixed by ST (stabiliser order 3). These are elliptic points, making the quotient an orbifold.

From hyperbolic polygon to Riemann sphere. The fundamental domain F is a hyperbolic triangle with vertices at ρ, −ρ̄, and i∞, with interior angles π/3, π/3, and 0 (the cusp angle). The angle sum is 2π/3, and the Gauss-Bonnet theorem gives area = π − (π/3 + π/3 + 0) = π/3, confirming the formula above. After boundary identifications, ρ and −ρ̄ merge into a single orbifold point with cone angle 2π/3, and the two sides meeting at i∞ merge into the cusp.

Drag and reduce

The reduction algorithm is the constructive proof. Given τ ∈ ℍ, alternate T (or T⁻¹) to shift Re τ into [−1/2, 1/2] and S to push |τ| above 1. S on |τ| < 1 increases Im τ, so the process terminates.

Play: Drag τ (blue point) anywhere in ℍ. The reduction algorithm applies S and T steps (shown as coloured path segments) to bring τ into the fundamental domain F. Orange segments are T-translations; purple segments are S-inversions. The number of steps typically grows as τ approaches the real axis.

Points high in ℍ need at most one T-shift. Points near the real axis may need many steps: S launches them high, they drift down, and the cycle repeats. Tiles deep near the real axis can require many iterations.

Domain colouring and the phase portrait

The tessellation shows the group. To see a function, paint each τ with a colour encoding a modular-invariant function. The canonical choice is the j-function (explainer 13), the unique Hauptmodul for ℍ/SL₂(ℤ). The graph would live in ℝ4; we encode the output as colour, called domain colouring.

The recipe is standard:

  • Hue encodes the argument of j(τ). Red at argument 0, cycling through orange, yellow, green, cyan, blue, magenta, and back to red at 2π.
  • Brightness encodes the modulus. Large |j| goes toward white (the pole at the cusp); small |j| goes toward black (the zero at ρ). A logarithmic scale keeps both visible.

For each pixel we reduce τ to F, evaluate the first ten terms of j(τ) = q−1 + 744 + 196884 q + ⋯, and paint. For τ near the real axis, reduction lifts Im τ into F where |q| = e−2π Im τ is small and convergence is fast.

Play: Domain colouring of j(τ) on the upper half-plane. Hue = arg j(τ), brightness = log |j(τ)|. The bright band at the top is the pole (j → ∞ at the cusp). The dark spots at the corners of every tile are the zeros (j(ρ) = 0). The pattern is SL₂(ℤ)-periodic: every tile is a colour-identical copy of every other, because j takes the same values on corresponding points. What the picture shows

Three features:

Self-similarity. Every tile is a shrunken Möbius copy of F with identical colouring, by modular invariance j(γτ) = j(τ).

Zeros at ρ. j = 0 at the lower corners of F. Zeros appear as dark spots where hues converge. Every copy of ρ produces another, clustering toward every rational point.

Pole at the cusp. j has a simple pole at the top of F. The cusp direction washes to white as |j| → ∞. Every tile has its own cusp.

Takeaways

  • One tile to rule them all: The fundamental domain (F) is a single, representative region. Every point in the entire upper half-plane can be mapped into this exact region using just S and T transformations.
  • Tiling the plane: The modular group copies this single domain to tile the entire upper half-plane with identical copies, leaving no gaps and no overlaps.
  • Rolling it up: Gluing the edges of the fundamental domain together (left to right, and the bottom arc to itself) rolls the domain up into a sphere with a single puncture at infinity.
  • Visualising symmetry: Domain colouring reveals the incredible symmetry of modular forms, repeating the exact same color pattern endlessly inside every single tile.
