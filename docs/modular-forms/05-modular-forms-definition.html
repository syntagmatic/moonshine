Modular Forms and Their Dimensions

Part 5 of 14. With the group, its domain and the cusp in place, this part states the definition of a modular form of weight k. The conditions are strict enough that each weight has only about k/12 independent forms, all polynomials in two Eisenstein series, and that finiteness turns equalities between q-series into identities that can be checked from a few coefficients.

Parts 1 to 4 set up the group SL₂(ℤ), its fundamental domain and the cusp. This part puts them together into the definition of a modular form. A modular form is much more constrained than a general holomorphic function on the upper half-plane. The weight-k factor ties its values together along each SL₂(ℤ)-orbit, and it may not blow up at the cusp. At weight k these conditions leave only about k/12 independent forms, all built from two generators. That finiteness is what makes modular forms useful: when a space has dimension one, any two forms in it are proportional, and comparing their q-expansions gives identities between integer sequences.

The simplest examples are the Eisenstein series Ek, built in Part 6. The heatmap shows log|Ek(τ)| on the fundamental domain, where the dark spots are zeros.

E4 and E6 are algebraically independent, so the monomials with 4a + 6b = k form a basis of Mk. (If a nontrivial relation held, E43/E62 would be a root of a polynomial with constant coefficients, hence constant. It cannot be, because it is 0 at ρ and 1 at i∞.)

Over the integers two generators are not enough. Counting dimensions

The count comes from the valence formula. For a nonzero f in Mk, the orders of vanishing at the cusp, at the two elliptic points i and ρ, and at the other points of the fundamental domain satisfy

The elliptic points count with weight ½ and ⅓ because their stabilisers in PSL₂(ℤ) have order 2 and 3 (Part 3). The left side is a sum of non-negative terms, and for small k there are very few ways to reach k/12:

  • k = 2: 1/6 cannot be written as n + a/2 + b/3 with non-negative integers, so M2 = 0. The q-series E2 = 1 − 24Σσ1(n)qn exists, but it fails the S-law by an extra term proportional to τ.
  • k = 4: 1/3 forces a single simple zero at ρ and nothing else. k = 6: 1/2 forces a single zero at i. That is what the heatmap shows.
  • k = 12: Δ has a simple zero at the cusp, which uses up all of 12/12 = 1. So Δ has no zeros in ℍ.

Computing a basis

A form in Mk is determined by its first dim Mk coefficients. (The difference of two forms that agree that far would vanish at the cusp to order at least dim Mk, and the valence formula does not allow that for a nonzero form.)
