The Ring of Modular Forms and Dimension Formulas

Part 9 of 15. M* = ℂ[E₄, E₆]. Every modular form is a polynomial in E₄ and E₆. dim Mk depends on k via ⌊k/12⌋, and Sk = Δ · Mk−12.

We have now met several modular forms individually: the Eisenstein series E₄ and E₆ in Part 7, and the discriminant Δ in Part 8. But modular forms are not isolated objects. They form a ring: products of modular forms are modular forms, and the weights add under multiplication. In this explainer we step back from individual forms and study the totality of all modular forms for SL₂(ℤ). The result is remarkably clean: the graded ring of modular forms is a polynomial ring in two generators, and we can write down exact dimension formulas for each weight.

This structural result is both beautiful and practical. Beautiful because an infinite-dimensional algebra with deep arithmetic content is generated by just two explicit functions. Practical because it means that if we want to find a modular form of weight k with prescribed properties, we only need to search through polynomials in E₄ and E₆ of the right total weight. The search space is finite-dimensional, and we know its dimension exactly.

The graded ring M*

Let Mk denote the space of modular forms of weight k for SL₂(ℤ). We defined this space in Part 6: it consists of all holomorphic functions f: ℍ → ℂ satisfying the weight-k transformation law f(γτ) = (cτ+d)k f(τ) for all γ ∈ SL₂(ℤ), and holomorphic at the cusp. The graded ring is the direct sum

So fg ∈ Mj+k, and multiplication respects the grading. The constant function 1 lies in M₀, serving as the identity. We already know two explicit generators: E₄ ∈ M₄ and E₆ ∈ M₆. The fundamental structure theorem says these are the only generators we need.

This is the statement that every modular form for SL₂(ℤ) can be written as a polynomial in E₄ and E₆. Since E₄ has weight 4 and E₆ has weight 6, a monomial E₄aE₆b has weight 4a + 6b. For a given weight k, the possible monomials are the non-negative integer solutions to 4a + 6b = k.

Why E₄ and E₆ suffice. The key arithmetic fact is that gcd(4, 6) = 2. Every even integer k ≥ 4 (except k = 2, where dim M₂ = 0) can be written as 4a + 6b with non-negative integers a, b. The proof that M* = ℂ[E₄, E₆] proceeds by induction on the weight: given f ∈ Mk, we subtract a suitable multiple of E₄aE₆b to kill the constant term of the q-expansion, producing a cusp form. If k < 12, the only cusp form is zero and we are done. If k ≥ 12, we divide by Δ to get a form in Mk−12 and apply the inductive hypothesis, using the fact that Δ = (E₄³ − E₆²)/1728 is itself a polynomial in E₄ and E₆.

Dimension formulas

Since Mk is a finite-dimensional vector space, we can ask for its dimension. The answer comes from the Riemann-Roch theorem applied to the compactified modular curve X(1) = ℍ*/SL₂(ℤ), which is a Riemann surface of genus 0 with two orbifold points (of orders 2 and 3) and one cusp. The formula is:

Let us unpack this. For negative or odd k, dim Mk = 0: there are no modular forms of odd weight (because −I ∈ SL₂(ℤ) forces f = (−1)kf), and no nonzero holomorphic forms of negative weight (they would blow up at the cusp). For k = 0 the only modular forms are constants, so dim M₀ = 1. For k = 2, even though the formula might suggest otherwise, the contribution from the orbifold points reduces the dimension to 0. For even k ≥ 4, the dimension grows roughly as k/12, stepping up by 1 each time k increases by 12.

A cusp form is a modular form whose constant term in the q-expansion vanishes. Requiring one coefficient to be zero costs one degree of freedom, so dim Sk = dim Mk − 1 for k ≥ 4 (and dim Sk = 0 for k < 12). The fact that dim S₁₂ = 1 tells us that there is a unique cusp form of weight 12, up to scalar: this is the discriminant Δ that we met in Part 8.

Basis browser

For each weight k, the monomials E₄aE₆b satisfying 4a + 6b = k form a basis of Mk. Let us list a few examples to see the pattern.

At weight 4: the only monomial is E₄1E₆0 = E₄, giving dim M₄ = 1. At weight 6: E₄0E₆1 = E₆, so dim M₆ = 1. At weight 8: E₄2, so dim M₈ = 1. (This means E₈ = E₄², a non-trivial identity between Eisenstein series that we can verify by comparing q-expansions.) At weight 10: E₄E₆, so dim M₁₀ = 1. At weight 12: E₄³ and E₆², giving dim M₁₂ = 2. The cusp form Δ = (E₄³ − E₆²)/1728 lives in the 1-dimensional subspace S₁₂.

The pattern continues: each time we pass a multiple of 12, the dimension increases by 1, with a correction at weights congruent to 2 mod 12. The basis browser in Figure 2 lets us explore this for any weight, computing the actual q-series coefficients of each monomial.

This is the dimension argument in action: when dim Mk = 1, any two normalised forms of weight k are forced to be identical, and comparing their q-coefficients yields number-theoretic identities for free.

The ideal of cusp forms

The cusp forms Sk are the modular forms in Mk whose q-expansion has no constant term: a₀ = 0. They form a subspace of Mk, and in fact the collection S* = ⊕ Sk is an ideal in the ring M*. The structure of this ideal is governed by the discriminant Δ.

This says: every cusp form of weight k is Δ times a modular form of weight k − 12. The map f ↦ Δf sends Mk−12 isomorphically onto Sk. The inverse is f ↦ f/Δ, which is well-defined because Δ never vanishes on ℍ (it vanishes only at the cusp, but f also vanishes there, so the quotient is holomorphic everywhere).

The consequences are immediate. Since dim Sk = dim Mk−12, we get dim Sk = dim Mk − 1 for k ≥ 4. And since Δ = (E₄³ − E₆²)/1728, the ideal S* is the principal ideal generated by Δ inside ℂ[E₄, E₆]. Every cusp form is a polynomial in E₄ and E₆, divisible by the specific polynomial E₄³ − E₆².

For example, S₁₂ = ℂ · Δ is 1-dimensional: the discriminant is the unique normalised weight-12 cusp form. S₁₆ = Δ · M₄ = ℂ · ΔE₄ is also 1-dimensional. The first weight where the cusp-form space becomes 2-dimensional is k = 24, where S₂₄ = Δ · M₁₂ has basis {Δ · E₄³, Δ · E₆²}, or equivalently {Δ², Δ · E₄³} after rearranging. These are the weight-24 forms that vanish at the cusp.

What we take with us

  • M* = ℂ[E₄, E₆]: the graded ring of modular forms for SL₂(ℤ) is a polynomial ring in E₄ (weight 4) and E₆ (weight 6).
  • Dimension formula: dim Mk = ⌊k/12⌋ + 1 for even k ≥ 4 with k not congruent to 2 mod 12; ⌊k/12⌋ if k ≡ 2 mod 12.
  • Cusp forms: Sk = Δ · Mk−12, so dim Sk = dim Mk − 1 for k ≥ 4.
  • Free identities: when dim Mk = 1, normalised forms of weight k are forced to be equal, yielding arithmetic identities like E₈ = E₄².
