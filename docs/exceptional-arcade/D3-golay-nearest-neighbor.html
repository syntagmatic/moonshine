D3 · Golay Nearest Neighbor

Track D, Game 3. The 24-bit analog of D2's Hamming decoder. A corrupted Golay codeword arrives. Pick the unique nearest valid codeword from four candidates before the timer runs out.

Prerequisite. Drills Exceptional Atlas Part 14 Packing, Codes, and the Leech Lattice for both the Construction A intuition and why the 24D Golay code matters. Read before playing if the bit patterns below feel arbitrary.

The extended binary Golay code packs 4,096 codewords into the 16,777,216 binary strings of length 24. Every pair of distinct codewords differs in at least 8 positions, so any received word with three or fewer flipped bits has a single nearest codeword and the decoder can recover it. With four flipped bits the radius-4 spheres of two codewords can overlap and unique decoding fails. This game makes the reader play the role of the decoder: a corrupted word arrives, four valid codewords are offered, and the goal is to spot the one with the smallest Hamming distance to what came in.

The full Golay code's 4,096 candidates are too many to display, so each puzzle pre-selects three decoy codewords drawn from the actual Golay code. The correct candidate really is the unique nearest neighbor among all 4,096 — the decoys are picked to be plausible but slightly farther away.

Why four candidates and not 4,096

The full nearest-neighbor problem in the Golay code asks: given any 24-bit string, find which of the 4,096 codewords is closest. Doing this by inspection means scanning every codeword once — a punishing exercise even with a list in front of you. The classical syndrome-decoder algorithms (Curtis's MOG search, Conway's hexacode decoder) trade exhaustive search for clever lookups in the parity-check structure. Both routes are too long-form for an arcade round.

The mechanic above is a strict subproblem. The four candidates always include the genuine nearest codeword among all 4,096; the three decoys are real Golay codewords drawn at random from a slightly larger Hamming ball around the received word. If the player picks the right one, they have correctly identified the unique nearest neighbor of the full code — the decoy filter just shrinks the visible search space from 4,096 to 4 without changing the answer.

Every Golay codeword sits at distance exactly 0, 8, 12, 16, or 24 from any other Golay codeword. That gap is what makes 1, 2, or 3 bit flips uniquely correctable: even after three errors, the corrupted word is at distance 3 from one codeword and at least distance 5 from every other codeword. The decoder can't be fooled.

At 4 bit flips, the geometry changes. A radius-4 ball around the received word can touch six different Golay codewords at once, so unique decoding fails. The Challenge tier therefore caps at 3 flips and pushes difficulty through the timer rather than the noise level — this keeps the round honest. Decoder design at the radius-4 boundary is exactly the work the Conway hexacode decoder does, and it is the topic of E8 series #13 and #14.

Construction A connection. The Leech lattice is built from the Golay code the way E8 is built from the [8,4,4] Hamming code: start with all integer vectors in that reduce mod 2 to a Golay codeword, scale, and repeat for refinements. Nearest-codeword decoding in the Golay code translates directly into nearest-lattice-point decoding in Leech. Every correct round in this game is a successful Leech-decoder run on a tiny patch of the lattice.
