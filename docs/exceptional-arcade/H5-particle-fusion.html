Particle Fusion

Pick two roots of E8. If their sum is also a root, the two "particles" fuse into a third. If not, they scatter without trace. Explore the 6,720 valid fusion vertices of the E8 root system as a hidden physics reaction table.

Prerequisite. E8 series explainer #03 Meet E8, the 240 Roots. This exhibit takes the "particles on a lattice" metaphor from Garrett Lisi's An Exceptionally Simple Theory of Everything and makes it clickable. You do not need to agree with Lisi's physics to enjoy the combinatorics.

Here is a game that plays itself out in every Lie algebra. Take two roots, α and β. Add them as 8-dimensional vectors: α + β. If the result is also a root of the same system, the pair has "fused" into a third root. If it is not a root, the addition went outside the 240-vector set and the pair is "scattered". This is the root lattice's internal product, and it is entirely visible without any physics at all — you do not need to believe in a force-carrier interpretation to see the combinatorial skeleton.

The skeleton turns out to be rigid. Of the 28,680 unordered pairs of distinct E8 roots, exactly 6,720 fuse and the rest scatter. The 6,720 is the same count as the edge count of the Gosset polytope 4₂₁ — not a coincidence, because the condition "α + β is a root" is equivalent to "α and β have inner product exactly −1", which is the same as "−α and β are joined by an edge of the Gosset polytope". Every fusion in this game is, secretly, a polytope edge going through the origin.

The one trick. Most pairs scatter. For any given first root, only 56 of the other 239 roots will fuse with it — exactly the kissing-number partners of its antipode. The remaining 183 pairs go nowhere. Pick the first root and watch the 56 partners light up as gold halos.

How to read it. 240 dots in the E8 Coxeter-plane projection. Click one to pick the first particle (blue halo); its 56 fusion partners light up with gold halos. Click one of those partners to complete a fusion, or click any other dot to see a scattered pair. A pink beam connects the two sources through the origin to the fusion result; a gray dotted beam marks a scatter. The "Fusions found" counter tracks distinct fusions you have personally triggered.

Walkthrough

1. Pick any root. Click any of the 240 dots. It turns blue. Then 56 of the other 239 dots light up with gold halos — these are the roots that will successfully fuse with your pick. The other 183 dots stay dim: they will scatter. This asymmetric split is the structure constant of E8 made visible.
2. Fuse into a third root. Click one of the gold-haloed dots. The two source dots turn into a vector from the origin, and a third dot lights up on the far side — that's the fusion result. The reaction display at the bottom shows the sum in 8D coordinates and confirms the result is another root of E8, with its index. Your fusions-found counter ticks up by one.
3. Force a scatter. Clear the selection, pick a new first root, then click one of the non-haloed dots (the dim ones). The reaction display turns gray and says "scattered". The sum vector goes to a point outside the 240-vertex set — it is not a root, so no third particle is produced. This is exactly what happens in a particle collision whose total momentum doesn't land on a stable state.
4. Try target-hunt mode. Click the Target hunt button. The exhibit highlights one specific "target" root in gold and challenges you to find any pair whose sum is that target. Click two roots; if they add to the target, you win the round and a new target appears. This is the reverse of the forward fusion question: instead of "pick two, see what you get", it's "pick the goal, find the ingredients".
5. Count toward 6,720. The total fusion count in the system is 6,720. Every time you trigger a new distinct fusion (two roots whose sum is another root), the counter increments. You will almost never reach 6,720 by clicking — that's ten-plus hours of clicking — but the counter is there to remind you that the fusion table is finite. Every particle collision in this toy physics is one of exactly 6,720 entries in a fixed table.

What you just saw

The E8 root system has a built-in notion of "addition that stays inside the system". Not every pair of roots sums to another root — most pairs sum to a vector of a different squared length. But the pairs whose sum does stay in the system form a very particular collection: 6,720 unordered fusing pairs (13,440 if you count the two orderings separately) that together encode the structure constants of the E8 Lie algebra. These are the non-trivial commutators , and in physics they are the vertices at which particles "interact" through a Lie-algebra-valued field theory.

Garrett Lisi's An Exceptionally Simple Theory of Everything (2007) proposed that the Standard Model of particle physics could be embedded inside E8 in exactly this way, with the 240 roots identified with Standard Model particles (quarks, leptons, gauge bosons) plus some predicted new particles. The theory has since been challenged on technical grounds — the full Standard Model probably cannot fit inside E8 as Lisi described it — but the visual metaphor of a root-addition interaction table is independent of whether that particular physics proposal is correct. Any Lie algebra gives rise to this same kind of fusion game; E8 happens to give the richest one, because it has the most roots and therefore the most interaction vertices.

What is beautiful about the exhibit is that you do not need to do any algebra to see the rules. You click two dots; the computer adds two 8-vectors; it looks up whether the result is in the 240-element set. That's it. The fact that the answer is a long-studied Lie algebra structure is incidental to the mechanic. If the arcade were a physics training simulator, this would be the "first day" lesson: addition of roots is the fundamental interaction in Lie-algebra physics, and the list of which pairs can add to a root is the vertex table of a hypothetical particle-physics theory built on E8.
