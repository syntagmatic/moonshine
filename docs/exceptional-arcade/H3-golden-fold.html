Golden Fold

The 240 roots of E8 decompose into two copies of the 4D regular 600-cell, related by a factor of the golden ratio. Click any root to see its partner.

Prerequisite. E8 series explainer #06 The Gosset Polytope 4₂₁. Familiarity with the golden ratio is assumed — nothing more. No prior knowledge of H4 or the 600-cell is required; the exhibit introduces them visually.

Here is a fact that sounds too good to be true: the 240 roots of E8 split cleanly into two groups of 120, each of which is a 4-dimensional regular polytope — specifically, the 600-cell, the 4D analogue of the icosahedron — and the two copies are scaled relative to each other by exactly the golden ratio . Written as an identity of root systems, this is . It is one of the strangest facts in high-dimensional geometry, and it turns out to be visible in the Coxeter-plane picture of E8 if you look at the right thing.

In the Coxeter plane, the 240 roots project onto 8 concentric rings. The 8 rings come in four φ-ratio pairs: for every "inner" radius r there is an "outer" radius equal to rφ, and the 120 roots at inner radii form one copy of the 600-cell while the 120 at outer radii form the other. Better yet, the two copies of the 600-cell occupy literally the same 120 Coxeter-plane positions after you scale the outer copy down by a factor of φ. This exhibit lets you watch that happen.

The one trick. Pull the fold slider to the right. The gold outer 120 collapse inward by a factor of φ and land exactly on top of the blue inner 120. The two sets become pointwise identical — same angles, same distances, same 600-cell. That's the golden fold, demonstrated as a pixel-perfect overlap.

Walkthrough

1. Click an inner blue dot. A pink line appears between it and one of the gold outer dots. The line is radial — selection and partner are on the same ray from the centre. The "Radius ratio" readout shows the quotient of their distances from the centre; it should be almost exactly 1.6180, which is φ to four decimal places.
2. Click a gold outer dot. Same story in reverse. The pairing is fully symmetric — each inner root has exactly one outer partner and vice versa. You can keep clicking around; the "Selected root" and "Its partner" readouts will show the dot indices so you can confirm each click is picking out a different pair.
3. Pull fold to 1. The gold outer 120 contract inward. Watch where they land: they overlap the blue inner 120 pointwise. By fold = 1, the two sets are indistinguishable — same 120 positions, just two colours on top of each other. This overlap is the concrete content of the isomorphism E8 ≅ H4 ⊕ φH4.
4. Isolate one half. Click inner only to hide the outer 120. What's left is the 2D Coxeter-plane projection of a single 600-cell — the 4-dimensional regular polytope with 120 vertices, 720 edges, 1200 triangles, and 600 tetrahedral cells. Click outer only to see the other 600-cell. They're the same shape at different scales.
5. Toggle edges. The lines between dots are the Gosset 4₂₁ edges (the 6,720-edge graph) restricted to whichever half is visible. Fold the outer onto the inner and watch edges meet: the 600-cell's edges inside each half become the Gosset polytope's edges that live inside that half.

What you just saw

The golden fold is not an artefact of a particular projection. The identity E8 = H4 ⊕ φH4 is a statement about the root lattices themselves: every E8 root vector can be written as a linear combination of one H4 root (the 600-cell's root system, at scale 1) and one φ-scaled H4 root, with specific golden-ratio coefficients governing how the two halves fit together. The Coxeter-plane picture is just the place where this fact is easiest to see, because the E8 Coxeter number (30) happens to equal the H4 Coxeter number (30), so projecting E8 and H4 onto their respective Coxeter planes gives the same angular grid — the same 60 angles on the outer circle, spaced so that each of the 4 rings per copy lands on them. That's why the fold works as an overlap rather than as a more complicated map.

H4 is not a crystallographic root system: it has no integer lattice, because the golden ratio is irrational and the 600-cell won't tile 4-space with integer-coordinate vertices. In contrast, E8 is crystallographic — it has the E8 lattice and all the beautiful integer structure that gives us Viazovska's sphere packing proof, the binary Golay code connection, and the lattice's place in the classification of simple Lie algebras. What the golden fold says is that you can build the crystallographic object E8 out of two copies of the non-crystallographic object H4, welded together by the golden ratio. E8 is, in a real sense, "H4 times φ" — and this is why E8 shows up wherever the golden ratio shows up in higher-dimensional geometry (quasicrystals, Penrose tilings, icosahedral projections).

None of this is obvious from the root counts. That 240 = 120 + 120 is an arithmetic fact; that the two halves of 120 are each a 600-cell is a structural fact; and that they are related by φ is a miracle that takes several pages of algebra to prove. The point of this exhibit is to show, without the algebra, that the miracle is real and that you can see it on a flat screen.
