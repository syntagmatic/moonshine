Ascent to E₈

Eight steps from two roots on a line to the 240 roots of E₈. Each step adds one simple root, grows the Dynkin diagram, and pops a new constellation of roots into view — the complete construction of E₈ as a build-up of everything beneath it.

Prerequisite. E8 series explainers #03 Meet E8, the 240 Roots and #04 The Dynkin Diagram. This exhibit is the opening movement of the headline series — watch it first if you want to see where the E8 in the other five pieces comes from.

Simple Lie algebras do not appear fully formed. They are built up, one simple root at a time, from the smallest non-trivial example — , which is just a single segment on a line — to the largest exceptional one — , the subject of the other five headliners in this wing. Between those two extremes lies a chain of finite groups, each one containing its predecessor as a sub-diagram: .

What this exhibit does is show that chain as an animation. Each step adds one simple root to the set of generators, grows the Dynkin diagram by one node and one edge (or more, if the new node causes a branch), and the resulting root system expands: 2 → 6 → 12 → 24 → 40 → 72 → 126 → 240. The expansion is not uniform. The jumps are 4, 6, 12, 16, 32, 54, 114 — each step nearly doubles the previous jump. By the time you reach E₈, you are adding 114 new roots in a single step, the largest jump in the whole sequence.

The one trick. Every root you see in this exhibit is the same point in ℝ⁸ at every step. What changes is which of those points has "joined" the current sub-algebra. The 240 positions are drawn faintly in the background so you can see where the final shape will be, and as you step forward, the sub-algebra's roots light up from that background.

Walkthrough

1. Start at A₁. One node in the Dynkin diagram, two roots on the main stage. This is the entire root system of A₁ = SU(2): a single simple root and its negative, together forming a "line segment" in ℝ⁸ (though you're seeing both points projected into the Coxeter-plane picture of the eventual E₈, so they land where they will end up). Coxeter number h = 2.
2. Click Next to reach A₂. A second Dynkin node appears, connected to the first by a single edge. Four new roots pop into the main stage, for a total of 6. These 6 roots are the vertices of a regular hexagon — A₂'s root system is exactly the hexagonal lattice's minimum vectors — and you can see the 6-fold symmetry forming in the constellation of lit points. Coxeter number h = 3.
3. A₃ and the jump to D₄. A₃ brings a third Dynkin node in a simple chain, for 12 roots total. Now watch the transition from step 3 to step 4: the next simple root does not extend the chain — it attaches off the branch node, creating a Y-shape. That's D₄, the first non-trivial "branched" Lie algebra, with 24 roots. D₄ is famous for having triality: a three-fold symmetry of the three legs of the Y that doesn't exist in any other simple Lie algebra, and a direct reason why 8-dimensional geometry is so special.
4. D₅, and the classical family tops out. D₅ adds a fifth node extending one of the D₄ arms, giving 40 roots and breaking the triality of D₄ into a single distinguished arm. The classical orthogonal Lie algebras D_n can continue indefinitely in this direction — D₆, D₇, D₈, and so on — but adding another node in a different direction unlocks something entirely new.
5. E₆, E₇, E₈. The last three steps extend the long arm of D₅ by one node at a time. Each extension is a different algebra: E₆ (72 roots), E₇ (126 roots), E₈ (240 roots). These are the three exceptional simple Lie algebras — they are not part of any infinite classical family, and the reason they exist at all is delicate. Adding a tenth node to the E₈ chain would give a non-finite Weyl group — an affine algebra, not a simple one — which is why the ascent has to stop at E₈. There is no E₉.

What you just saw

The sequence A₁ → A₂ → A₃ → D₄ → D₅ → E₆ → E₇ → E₈ is the single most beautiful chain in the classification of simple Lie algebras. Every step corresponds to adding one generator to the algebra's Cartan matrix, one node to its Dynkin diagram, and one full dimension to the Cartan subalgebra. The root count at each step is determined entirely by the combinatorics of the diagram: given a Dynkin diagram, the number of roots of its algebra is fixed by a formula you can look up in any table of simple Lie algebras.

But the rank-by-rank ascent is not the only construction of E₈. There are at least four other canonical constructions that an E₈ enthusiast should meet eventually: the D₈ extension (take the root system of D₈ = SO(16) and glue in a half-integer spinor lattice, which is exactly the "integer + half-integer" split you already know from the 240 root coordinates), the H₄ fold (assemble E₈ as two copies of the 600-cell at scales 1 and φ, as shown in H3 Golden Fold), the Construction A build from the Hamming code (as drilled in the D2 and E2 arcade drills), and the Jordan algebra route through the octonions and the exceptional Jordan algebra .

All five constructions give the same E₈ — the same 240 vectors up to isomorphism — but each one highlights a different face of it. The rank-by-rank ascent you just watched highlights E₈'s place at the top of a hierarchy: it is the culmination of a chain of simpler, completely classical algebras, not an object that drops out of the sky. If you see E₈ in physics, quasicrystal geometry, sphere packing, or error-correcting codes, it almost certainly arrived there through one of these constructions. This exhibit shows you the first one, and the other headliners in Track H show you the pay-off for stopping at the top of the ladder.
