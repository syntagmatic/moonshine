Flatland Sections

A 3-dimensional slicing plane sweeps across the Gosset polytope 4₂₁. Watch the cross-section evolve from empty space through a 120-vertex constellation and back out the other side.

Prerequisite. E8 series explainer #06 The Gosset Polytope 4₂₁. This exhibit assumes you already know that 4₂₁ is an 8-dimensional uniform polytope with 240 vertices and 6,720 edges, and that all 240 vertices are unit-length-ish points of ℝ⁸ at a common distance from the origin.

Every 8-dimensional polytope, however rigid and permanent it seems in its native ambient space, has an evolving 3D shadow when you push it through a flat 3-dimensional screen. This is the Flatland metaphor lifted five dimensions up: a Flatlander watching a 3D sphere pass through their plane sees a dot grow into a circle, reach a maximum, and shrink back to a dot. An 8-dimensional polytope sweeping past a 3-dimensional screen is the same experience, one notch more elaborate.

The polytope we are pushing through is 4₂₁, the Gosset polytope whose 240 vertices are exactly the roots of E8. We hold one of its 8 coordinate axes perpendicular to our screen, and we scrub a 3D hyperplane along that axis from the deep negative end to the deep positive end. Every time the hyperplane crosses one of the polytope's 6,720 edges, we get a point of the cross-section. As the slider moves, these intersection points trace out a constellation in 3D — the actual cross-section polytope at that slice level.

The thing to watch for. The slider has five halting points where a whole sheet of vertices of 4₂₁ sits directly on the slicing plane. Between halts, you see only edge crossings — and the constellation is different on either side of each halt.

Walkthrough

1. Start at slice = 0. Load the page. The slicing plane is passing through the origin; the cross-section has its maximum number of intersection points and is the "widest" slice of 4₂₁. Drag the main stage to rotate the view and convince yourself that the pink slicing plane really does go through the centre of the polytope.
2. Scrub to the left edge. Drag the slider to −1.15. The slicing plane is now just past the deepest vertex of the polytope in this direction; nothing crosses it; the cross-section is empty. Slowly drag it back toward zero and watch red intersection points appear at the edge of the polytope, grow into a small constellation, then expand.
3. Snap to the halts. Click Snap to next halt. The slider jumps to one of the five "special" slice levels: −1, −½, 0, +½, +1. At each halt, an entire sheet of 4₂₁ vertices sits exactly on the slicing plane. The number in the "Vertices on slice" readout jumps from zero to some positive value. These halt levels are where the true vertices of 4₂₁ live; in between halts, the red dots are just edge midpoints.
4. Swap the axis. The axis buttons below the slider let you slice along any of the 8 coordinate axes of ℝ⁸ instead of x₁. The polytope itself is completely symmetric — you can choose any axis to scrub along — but you get different halt levels and a different-looking cross-section for each. Axes 1–7 give the same 5 halt levels (−1, −½, 0, +½, +1); the 8th axis (half-integer-only) has a different shape.
5. Sweep automatically. Click Sweep and watch the slider move on its own. The cross-section polytope in the right-hand pane flows from empty, through a series of discrete "stepping stones" at each halt level, back to empty. This continuous evolution is the exact 8-dimensional analogue of watching a 3D sphere pass through Flatland.

What you just saw

The cross-section polytope at a given slice level is a lower-dimensional thing than 4₂₁ itself — by one dimension per axis you slice along. So a generic slice of 4₂₁ (8-dimensional) through a perpendicular 7-plane gives a 7-dimensional polytope; projecting it into your 3D screen is a second step. We combined those two steps: first we took the 7D slice, then we flattened its remaining coordinates onto a projection aligned with E8's Coxeter plane so the cross-section inherits the mandala-like 30-fold rotational symmetry of the Coxeter-plane picture that the E8 series has been showing you all along.

At the five halt levels — integer and half-integer values of the slice coordinate — the cross-section coincides with an actual layer of 4₂₁ vertices, and each layer has interesting sub-structure of its own. The x₁ = ±1 halts each contain 14 vertices: the 14 integer E8 roots whose first coordinate equals ±1 (one ±1 is forced at position 1 and the other ±1 sits at one of the remaining 7 positions, giving 7 × 2 = 14 combinations). The x₁ = 0 halt has 84 vertices, which are exactly the 84 roots of the rank-7 subsystem D₇ — the slice "sees" a D₇ root polytope sitting inside 4₂₁ at that level. And the x₁ = ±½ halts are the half-integer sheets, 64 vertices each, whose union accounts for all 128 half-integer E8 roots.

This is the headliner's whole point. You read in the E8 series that 4₂₁ has 240 vertices and 6,720 edges, and you probably nodded along. But "240 and 6,720" is a pair of counts; it is not a picture. Now you have the picture. The 240 vertices are arranged so that, sliced any way you like, the cross-section's complexity has the shape of a bell curve — empty at the extremes, richest in the middle — and the complexity peaks at the slice levels where actual vertices happen to land. That is the shadow of 4₂₁ that someone in our 3-dimensional world is capable of seeing.
