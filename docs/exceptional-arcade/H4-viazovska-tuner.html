Viazovska's Tuner

A toy model of the 2016 optimal-packing proof. Tune three coefficients of a test function until it is non-positive on every lattice distance and its Fourier transform is non-negative everywhere. Satisfy all constraints and you have a working sketch of the Cohn–Elkies linear programming bound.

Prerequisite. E8 series explainer #12 Viazovska and Optimal Packing. This exhibit assumes you know that in 2016, Maryna Viazovska proved E8 is the densest possible sphere packing in 8 dimensions, using a special "magic function" built from modular forms. What you probably do not know — and what this exhibit is for — is what it feels like to be the person trying to construct such a function.

The Cohn–Elkies linear programming bound (2003) says this: if you can find a well-behaved function on ℝⁿ such that (a) f is non-positive at every non-zero lattice distance of the E8 lattice, and (b) the Fourier transform is non-negative everywhere, and (c) both f(0) and f̂(0) are strictly positive, then the density of any sphere packing in ℝⁿ is bounded above by .

Notice how strange this is. The function f has no direct geometric meaning — it is not the shape of any packing, it is not a distance function, it is not the diameter of anything. It is a purely auxiliary object invented by Cohn and Elkies whose existence implies a bound on packing density. For most dimensions the best known f gives a bound that is loose. For dimensions 1, 2, and 3 the best known f almost matches the actual density but not quite. For dimension 8, Viazovska found an f that matches the E8 density exactly, proving E8 optimal. For dimension 24, Cohn–Kumar–Miller–Radchenko–Viazovska did the same for the Leech lattice the following year.

This exhibit gives you a toy version of the tuning problem. Instead of the real function space (Eisenstein modular forms of weight 12, tensored with certain half-integer-weight modular forms, pulled back through a contour integral...) you have a 3-parameter family: , where a, b, c are the three sliders at your disposal. The Fourier transform of this family happens to have a very simple closed form, so both plots update live as you drag. Your job is to find an (a, b, c) that satisfies all the constraints simultaneously.

The honest disclosure. This is a 1-dimensional toy, not a real 8D proof. The 3 parameters are too few, and the function basis is too narrow, to give a tight E8 bound. But the shape of the problem — "tune coefficients until f is below zero here and f̂ is above zero there" — is exactly what Viazovska's proof does, just with a vastly richer function space. Satisfying this toy is what solving a tiny LP bound feels like.

How to read it. Top plot is f(r), a linear combination of a Gaussian and its first two even-polynomial modifications. Bottom plot is f̂(k), the Fourier transform of that same combination, computed analytically and drawn in real time. The red dots on each plot mark the lattice distances r, k ∈ {√2, √4, √6, √8} — the first four shells of the E8 lattice, which is self-dual so the distance set is the same on both sides. The top plot must be at or below zero on every red dot; the bottom plot must be at or above zero on every red dot and at every point in between.

Walkthrough

1. Start with the defaults. On first load, (a, b, c) = (1, 0, 0). The function f(r) is just a Gaussian, which is positive everywhere, so several red dots on the top plot are above zero. Several constraints in the checklist are already red crosses. The top plot's status tag should be red.
2. Pull b down. Drag the b slider to the left. As b goes negative, the top plot gets a dip that pushes the lattice-point values downward. Keep pulling until the first red dot (at r = √2) just touches zero. Watch the checklist: the first few f-constraints turn green.
3. Check the bottom plot. As you changed b, the bottom plot changed too — because f̂ depends on the same three coefficients. Is it still non-negative everywhere? If not, you've broken an f̂-constraint while fixing an f-constraint. That tug-of-war is the essence of LP tuning: the two sides of the inequality are coupled through the Fourier transform, and moving any slider pushes one side and pulls the other.
4. Try to satisfy everything. Keep tugging on all three sliders until every constraint in the checklist turns green and both plot status tags say OK. This is hard. The 3-parameter family has a feasible region, but it's a narrow sliver, and most slider combinations fall outside it.
5. Click Reveal magic. The sliders jump to the pre-computed configuration (a, b, c) = (1, −½, 0), which satisfies every constraint simultaneously. The top plot now has its maximum right at r = 0, dips through zero exactly at r = √2, and stays negative thereafter. The bottom plot is a positive envelope everywhere. Both status tags light up green, the bound value activates, and you have a toy LP proof of a sphere-packing bound.

What you just saw

Viazovska's 2016 proof works essentially the same way as this toy, except that (i) the function space is much richer (linear combinations of specific modular forms of weight 12, combined through a Laplace transform trick), (ii) the constraints are applied at all lattice distances of E8, not just the first four, and (iii) the "magic" configuration is not reachable by hand — Viazovska's key insight was that the modular form side had just enough freedom to hit every constraint exactly at equality, making the LP bound match the E8 density exactly, and that insight took several hundred pages of number theory to establish. The toy you just played with has 3 sliders; Viazovska's proof has countably infinite parameters and an entire toolkit of modular-form identities to steer them.

The aesthetic lesson here is that the LP bound is non-geometric. Nothing about f looks like a sphere, or a lattice, or a packing. The function lives in the abstract space of well-behaved real-valued functions on ℝⁿ, and the Fourier transform couples the function to itself through integration. The existence of a valid f is an analytic statement, not a geometric one. That's why LP bounds work for problems where direct geometric methods are stuck: they translate a packing question into a functional-analytic question, and sometimes (in dimensions 1, 8, and 24) the translated question happens to be solvable while the original question isn't.

The miracle of Viazovska's proof — the reason it became instantly famous — was not that she used the LP bound. Everyone knew the LP bound. The miracle was that she found a specific f for dimension 8 where everyone else had failed for a decade. The function space she worked in had been known since the 1800s; the connection between modular forms and lattice packings had been suspected since Cohn and Elkies proposed the LP bound in 2003; but nobody had the particular combination of classical number theory, analytic cleverness, and sheer persistence needed to actually write the function down. Viazovska did. One year later she extended the method to dimension 24 and did it again for the Leech lattice. Since 2017 the open dimensions have been every other dimension — 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, ..., where the LP bound is known to not be tight and the optimal packing stays unproven.
