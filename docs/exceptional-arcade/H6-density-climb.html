Density Climb

The sphere-packing density curve from dimension 1 to dimension 24. Two spikes poke up through an otherwise steep decline: E8 at 8 and the Leech lattice at 24. Every other dimension is open.

Prerequisite. E8 series explainers #11 Kissing Number 240, #12 Viazovska and Optimal Packing, and #13 The Leech Lattice. This exhibit assumes you know that sphere packing means "arrange non-overlapping unit balls so as many as possible fit per unit volume", and that the density question is unsolved in almost every dimension.

Here is the intuition most people walk into the sphere-packing question with: higher dimensions should have more room, so packings should get denser with dimension. The intuition is backwards. Density falls as dimension increases, steeply and monotonically in the low dimensions, because there is exponentially more "corner space" in a high-dimensional box and spheres only fill their own volume. By dimension 20, even the best known lattice gets less than 1% of the volume compared to the balls' own volume — it is mostly empty space.

And then, in the middle of this decline, two dimensions sit distinctly above the trend. Dimension 8 and dimension 24. In 8D, the E8 lattice has density π⁴/384 ≈ 0.2537 — still lower than the dimensions 5, 6, 7 record holders in absolute terms, but the decline from 7 to 8 slows sharply and E8 sits well above any smooth extrapolation from the 5–7 curve. From dimension 8 to dimension 9 the curve then drops much more steeply again, which makes the 8D record look, on the graph, like a gentle upward kink — the "E8 spike". Dimension 24 does the same thing more dramatically with the Leech lattice, which has density π¹²/12! ≈ 0.00193 — a tiny absolute number, but enormously bigger than the density of any known lattice in dimensions 20–23. Eight and twenty-four are the only two dimensions where this "above-trend" behaviour is dramatic enough to see with the naked eye, and they are exactly the two dimensions where sphere packing is proven optimal by a modern argument (dimensions 1, 2, and 3 are also proven optimal by older means — trivial, Thue 1890, and Hales 1998 respectively — but those optima sit on the overall decline rather than above it, so they don't register as spikes).

The big question. Why 8 and 24 and nowhere else? This is one of the deepest open questions in lattice theory. The answer, as far as anyone understands, is that these are the two dimensions where certain modular-form identities conspire to make the Cohn–Elkies LP bound tight. But "conspire" is not an explanation; it is a description of the miracle you are about to scrub through.

Walkthrough

1. Click dim 1. The best lattice is ℤ (just the integers) with density exactly 1 — a 1D packing fills the line with touching balls and has no waste. The cubic lattice is the same thing in 1D, so the two curves start at the same point.
2. Click dim 3. The best known 3D lattice is the face-centered-cubic (FCC), and its density is π/√18 ≈ 0.7405. That's what oranges stacked in a crate do. The cubic lattice ℤ³ has density π/6 ≈ 0.524 — substantially worse, because ℤ³ leaves a big central hole in each fundamental cell. This is proven optimal by the Hales proof of Kepler's conjecture, 1998.
3. Click dim 5, 6, 7. The best known lattices in dimensions 5, 6, and 7 are D₅, E₆, E₇. Their densities decline monotonically from about 0.465 to 0.295. Watch the blue curve come down smoothly from 3D toward 7D. Now click dim 8.
4. Click dim 8. E₈ appears here with density π⁴/384 ≈ 0.2537. In absolute terms that is still lower than E₇'s 0.295, so the curve is going down — but by a much smaller amount than the 5→6→7 segment would predict. Linearly extrapolating the 5→7 trend points you toward ≈ 0.23 at dimension 8; E₈ beats that extrapolation by about 9% and sits well above what the surrounding decay implies. Combined with the sharper drop from 8 to 9, the effect on the graph is a visible "kink upward" right at dimension 8 — the E₈ spike.
5. Toggle to log scale, then click dim 24. Switch the y-axis to logarithmic and the overall decay becomes linear-looking (because density falls exponentially with dimension). The 8D and 24D spikes now appear as visible kinks sticking above the log-linear trend — the Leech spike is tiny in linear coordinates but clear on log. The Leech lattice in 24 dimensions has density π¹²/12! ≈ 0.00193, which is the proven optimum for that dimension.

What you just saw

The lattice record-holders in each dimension form a sequence that mostly goes A₁, A₂, A₃, D₄, D₅, E₆, E₇, E₈, then jumps into the "Barnes–Wall" and "laminated lattice" families, and then eventually lands on Λ₂₄ = the Leech lattice, which is the densest known packing in 24D. That particular sequence — cross the A's, then the D's, then the exceptional E's, then the laminated lattices — is what the density curve is graphing. The shape of the curve is the accumulated consequence of every lattice theorist's cleverness from Gauss through Conway and Sloane to Viazovska.

The 8 and 24 spikes stand out because nothing better has ever been found, and more importantly, because the Cohn–Elkies LP bound can be made tight there. In 8D, Viazovska's 2016 proof constructs a specific auxiliary function on ℝ⁸ that satisfies every constraint with equality, which forces the density upper bound to land exactly on the E₈ density and proves optimality. In 24D, the 2017 follow-up (Cohn–Kumar–Miller–Radchenko–Viazovska) does the same for the Leech lattice. In every other dimension above 3, the best known LP bound is strictly larger than the best known packing density, which leaves two possibilities open: (a) better lattices exist and nobody has found them, or (b) the LP bound is not tight in those dimensions. Both possibilities are real and we do not currently know which applies where.

What makes the exhibit worth looking at is the shape of the curve as a whole, not any one data point. You see density falling. You see it fall steeply. You see it fall so steeply that by 24D, even the best-known packing is less than 0.2% of the space — and you realise that the "trivial" idea of increasing density by going to higher dimensions is not just false but backwards. And then, right there in the middle of the decline, you see two modest spikes where the decline suddenly reverses. Those spikes are E₈ and Leech. They are the only two places in all of lattice theory where dimensions 1 through 24 have a clean, provable, exceptionally-symmetric answer to the sphere-packing question. Everywhere else is still up for grabs.
