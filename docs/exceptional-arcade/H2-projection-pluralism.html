Projection Pluralism

Five canonical 2-dimensional shadows of E8, each the Coxeter plane of a different sub-Weyl group, with a slider that smoothly morphs the 240 roots from one picture into the next.

Prerequisite. Exceptional Atlas Part 13: 240 Roots, Gosset, Coxeter. This exhibit assumes you already know that the famous "8 rings with 30-fold symmetry" picture of E8 is one projection among many, and that different projection planes reveal different underlying symmetries.

The 240 roots of E8 live in 8-dimensional Euclidean space. There is no honest picture of them that fits on a flat screen. Every 2-dimensional drawing you've ever seen is a projection — a choice of a 2-plane in ℝ⁸ onto which the 240 points are flattened. And the choice of plane is not trivial: different planes reveal different symmetries of the hidden full object.

This exhibit shows five projections side by side. Each one is the "Coxeter plane" of a different sub-Weyl group of E8 — the 2-plane on which the Coxeter element of that subgroup acts as a rotation by 2π divided by the subgroup's Coxeter number. We start with the full E8 Petrie projection, where the Coxeter number is 30 and the roots organise into 8 concentric rings of 30 dots each. Click any of the other four buttons, and the 240 dots flow — smoothly, not jump-cut — into the projection of a smaller subgroup: E₇ with 18-fold symmetry, E₆ with 12-fold, D₅ with 8-fold, or A₄ with 5-fold.

The one big idea. No projection is "the" picture of E8. They are all 2D shadows of the same 8D object, and the choice of which symmetry to show is a choice about which subgroup to emphasise. The morph between them is not an artefact of rendering — it's the concrete statement that these five pictures come from the same 240 points, just flattened onto five different planes.

How to read it. Each of the 240 dots is one root of E8. Its position is a weighted blend between its coordinates in the "from" projection and its coordinates in the "to" projection, controlled by the morph slider. Slider = 0 shows the from picture exactly; slider = 1 shows the to picture exactly; values in between show the flow. Gosset-polytope edges can be toggled on to see which pairs of roots are adjacent in 4₂₁ — those edges bend and stretch as the projection changes but the combinatorics stay the same.

Walkthrough

1. The default picture. On first load you're looking at the full E8 Coxeter plane projection, also called the Petrie projection. 8 concentric rings of 30 dots each — because the E8 Coxeter number is 30 and the 240 roots fall on 8 distinct projection-radii. This is the picture of E8 on Wikipedia and in the headers of most E8 articles.
2. Click E₇. The 240 dots flow into a new arrangement — 18-fold symmetry now, the signature of the E₇ Coxeter number. The 240 points reorganise onto 8 rings of different populations: some rings with 18 points, others with 54 (three orbits of 18 on the same radius), and a small central ring. The 126 roots of the E₇ sub-algebra and the 114 remaining roots all contribute; the sub-algebra's symmetry is what governs the rotational structure but every point still has to land somewhere. Drag the morph slider back to 0 and forward to 1 to see the same 240 points reorganising between the two projections. Nothing about the roots has changed; you're just looking at them from a different "angle".
3. Step down the chain. Click E₆, then D₅, then A₄. Each step shows a projection onto the Coxeter plane of a smaller subgroup. The symmetry order drops: 30 → 18 → 12 → 8 → 5. By the time you reach A₄ the picture has collapsed into a pentagonal flower; the roots that used to be in the outer E8 rings are now crowded into a smaller region because the A₄ Coxeter plane is less efficient at spreading them out.
4. Toggle the edges off. Uncheck show edges to remove the 6,720 Gosset-polytope edges and leave only the 240 root-dots. In the E₈ Petrie picture, the edges form the "lace" inside each of the 8 rings; in the lower-symmetry projections they get tangled and noisy, which is part of why those projections are less commonly reproduced despite being mathematically exact.
5. Hit Cycle through all. The exhibit will walk itself through all 5 projections, pausing briefly on each and morphing between them. Watch which points stay roughly in place (those whose 8D coordinates happen to project similarly in every plane) and which ones travel the longest distance (those whose projection changes the most). The quieter points are telling you about an eigenvector that's common to all five sub-Coxeter planes.

What you just saw

Each of the five projections is constructed the same way: take a subset of the 8 simple roots of E8, generate the Weyl group of the resulting sub-Dynkin diagram, and find the 2-dimensional real eigenspace of that subgroup's Coxeter element for the eigenvalue , where h is the Coxeter number of the subgroup. That eigenspace is a 2-plane embedded in ℝ⁸, and projecting the 240 roots onto it gives an arrangement where the subgroup's Coxeter element acts as a rotation by 2π/h — hence the h-fold rotational symmetry of each picture.

Of the five, only the full E₈ projection actually rotates all 240 roots into the plane with perfect 30-fold symmetry. The lower subgroups do not contain every E8 root: E₇ misses the 114 roots that "stick out" of the E₇ sub-algebra, and those roots don't land on the regular 18-gon grid — they land wherever the E₇ plane happens to send them. That's why the non-Petrie projections look denser in the middle and less orderly on the edges: you're seeing the structure of the sub-algebra clearly, and the roots outside that sub-algebra superimposed as "noise".

The research report from which this headliner was commissioned calls this principle projection pluralism: no single picture is privileged. The E₈ Petrie projection is most commonly reproduced because it's the prettiest and the most symmetric, but it's one of infinitely many legitimate shadows of the same 8-dimensional object. Choosing a projection is the interactive-media analogue of choosing a camera angle for a sculpture — and this exhibit is the sculpture on a turntable.
