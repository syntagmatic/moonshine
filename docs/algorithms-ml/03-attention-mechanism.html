The Attention Mechanism

How transformers decide which words matter for understanding each other word.

In the sentence "The cat sat on the mat," understanding "sat" requires knowing who sat (the cat) and where (the mat). Some words matter more than others, and which ones matter depends on the word you are trying to understand.

Earlier sequence models read the words in order and compressed the sentence into one fixed-size vector, which lost information in long sentences. Attention lets the model look at every word at once and take a weighted average of them, with more weight on the relevant ones. The interesting part is how the model learns those weights.

From Hard Lookup to Soft Attention

Think about looking up a word in a dictionary. You have a query (the word you want), a set of keys (the dictionary entries), and the values (the definitions). In a hard lookup, your query either matches a key exactly or it does not.

Attention is a soft lookup. The query partially matches many keys at once, each match gets a weight, and the output is a blend of all the values mixed by those weights. The better a key matches the query, the more its value contributes.

Queries, Keys, and Values

Each word enters as a vector, a list of numbers. To compute attention, the model turns each word's vector into three new ones. The query represents what a word is looking for, the key what it offers to be found by, and the value the information it contributes when attended to. Each comes from multiplying the input vector by a learned weight matrix: WQ, WK or WV.

Every word plays all three roles. "Cat" has a query (what is cat looking for?), a key (what makes cat findable?), and a value (what does cat pass along?).

The remaining figures come from a small model that your browser trains when the page loads. It has one attention layer with two heads, and its queries, keys and values have 6 numbers each. Each word's input vector marks which word it is and where it sits. The training sentences come from a toy grammar, like "the old dog slept under a box", with an optional adjective in each half so that no word always sits at the same position. From each word's attention output, a linear readout has to name two other words: the word it attaches to (a determiner or adjective names its noun, the verb its subject, a preposition its noun, and each noun the verb) and the word just before it. Nobody writes down an attention pattern. WQ, WK, WV and the readout start random and are trained by backpropagation for 400 steps.

Scaling and Softmax

The raw attention scores are the dot products of each query with each key, one score per pair of words. Dot products tend to grow with the dimension of the vectors, and large inputs make softmax so peaked that one word gets nearly all the weight, so the scores are divided by the square root of the key dimension, √dk. Softmax then normalizes each row to sum to 1, and those weights mix the value vectors into each word's output.

Figure 3. The first head's attention computation on "The cat sat on the mat", in three stages. Step through with the buttons or by scrolling, and click a row to select a query. The slider replays training: it shows the same computation with the weights saved every 20 steps, from the random start to the end.

Step 1: Raw Scores

Q × KT gives a raw score for every pair of words. Larger numbers mean the query and key point in more similar directions.

Step 2: Scale

Divide by √dk. Without it, large dot products would push softmax toward near-one-hot rows.

Step 3: Softmax

Softmax normalizes each row to sum to 1, turning scores into attention weights. Each row is now a probability distribution over the sentence, and multiplying it by V gives that word's output.

Drag the training slider to the left end. With random WQ and WK, the weights in each row are spread over several words and follow no pattern. As training proceeds, most rows concentrate on one word, and in this head it is usually the word that row has to name: "sat" ends on "cat", "on" and the second "the" end on "mat". Gradient descent pushes the weights this way because the readout can only name "cat" from the output at "sat" if the value vector of "cat" is mixed into it.

Many Heads, Many Relationships

A single head gives each word one set of weights, so it tends to fetch one kind of neighbour at a time. The toy task asks every word two questions, which is why the model has two heads, each with its own WQ, WK and WV. Both heads feed one shared readout, and nothing assigns either head to either question. Full-size transformers have more heads, and their outputs are concatenated and projected back to the model's dimension.

Figure 4. Both heads of the trained model on the same sentence. The text under the grids counts, for each head, how many rows put their largest weight on the attached word and how many on the previous word, and reports the model's accuracy on fresh sentences. Retraining runs the same 400 steps from a different random start.

In the first run, head 1 mostly fetches the attached word and head 2 mostly fetches the previous word. The first "The" is an exception: head 1 looks at the word itself, which is that position's answer to the previous-word question, and head 2 fetches "cat". Retraining shows how much of the split depends on the random start. In some runs the heads swap roles; in others a head spreads its rows across both questions, or looks at words that answer neither, and the readout still recovers both answers from the two heads together.

What the Toy Leaves Out

This model was told what to predict and nothing about where to look. The attention patterns are whatever made the predictions work. Real transformers are trained the same way on a different objective, usually predicting the next word or a masked-out word, with many layers of many heads. When Clark and colleagues (2019) inspected the heads of BERT, they found some that attend to the previous or next token, and some that line up with syntax, such as heads where verbs attend to their direct objects and nouns to their determiners. Many other heads have no such clean reading.
