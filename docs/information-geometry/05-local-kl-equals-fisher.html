Local KL Equals Fisher

Part 5 of 15. The infinitesimal and global pictures are the same picture, up to a Taylor expansion. The asymmetry of KL, the bruises of the triangle inequality, and every other rough edge disappear when you zoom in, leaving behind exactly the Fisher quadratic form.

Parts 2 and 4 gave us two different-looking objects. The Fisher metric is an infinitesimal, symmetric, coordinate-invariant quadratic form on the tangent space of the manifold. KL divergence is a global, asymmetric, non-triangle-inequality-respecting functional on pairs of distributions. They seem to live in different worlds. Yet they are secretly the same object, and the bridge between them is a single Taylor expansion. This is the result that ties the whole of information geometry together, and it is what we are going to prove — slowly, and with a picture to verify it.

So at small distances the two KL directions agree, and the asymmetry you saw in Part 4 is a large-distance phenomenon. Second, the Fisher matrix is uniquely determined by KL through this expansion — if two divergences agree to second order, they share the same Fisher metric. This is a precise version of the intuition that "KL and Fisher measure the same thing at different scales".

The two directions of KL are indistinguishable in the limit of small steps. They differ at third order, and the third-order coefficient is not zero in general — the asymmetry is a real phenomenon at finite distance, as we saw in Part 4. But Information geometry's Riemannian structure cares only about the second-order piece, and at that order KL is perfectly symmetric. This is the formal sense in which "the Fisher metric is the symmetric core of KL".

The asymmetry lives in the cubic term. Everything that makes KL weird — mode covering, mode seeking, the blowups in the tails — is a third-order-and-higher effect. The zeroth and first orders vanish by normalisation and score mean-zero. The second order is the Fisher metric, which is symmetric. The third order is where KL diverges from its reverse, and that is where all of the interesting statistical phenomena live. "Information geometry" is the study of the local Riemannian geometry (second order) plus the dual connections that encode the cubic corrections (the subject of Part 6).

A numerical check

Fix a Gaussian base point, choose a direction in parameter space, and plot three quantities as we move along that direction: the forward KL, the reverse KL, and the Fisher quadratic form. At small displacements all three lie on top of each other. At larger displacements they start to diverge — first the two KLs separate from each other (third-order effect), then both of them separate from the Fisher parabola (also third-order, different sign).

At the three curves are nearly indistinguishable. The exact ratio depends on the direction chosen (each direction has its own cubic coefficient), but at the default 45° direction the forward ratio sits about one and a quarter percent below 1 and the reverse ratio sits two tenths of a percent below. At the forward and reverse KLs fan apart from the Fisher parabola and from each other. At the asymmetry is gross and the Fisher parabola is a poor model of either KL. This is the Taylor expansion in a plot: three curves that agree at the origin and disagree only as their higher-order terms kick in.

What comes next

We now have the full Act I picture. Distributions live on a manifold; the Fisher metric is the unique ruler for distinguishability; KL is the global divergence whose second-order expansion is exactly the Fisher quadratic form; the asymmetry of KL is a third-order effect that lives above the Riemannian layer. Act II takes this static picture and makes it dynamic. We introduce connections — the machinery of parallel transport and geodesics — and discover that information geometry comes with two connections, dual to each other in a precise sense, each with its own notion of "straight line", and the duality between them is the source of every interesting computational algorithm in the rest of the series.

The bridge from static to dynamic is the α-connection family, and it is the subject of Part 6.
