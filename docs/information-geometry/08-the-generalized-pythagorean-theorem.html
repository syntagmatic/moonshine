The Generalised Pythagorean Theorem

Part 8 of 15. When an e-geodesic meets an m-geodesic at a Fisher right angle, the KL divergence decomposes additively — and the statement is not a metaphor. It is the same Pythagorean theorem you learned in grade school, transported to the space of probability distributions, and it is the reason half of statistical inference works.

Part 7 left us with a dually flat manifold, two convex potentials, two coordinate systems linked by a Legendre transform, and a closed form for KL divergence as the Bregman divergence of the log-partition function. All of that scaffolding exists to support a single result, so central to information geometry that once you see it you cannot unsee it in any projection-based statistical algorithm. That result is the generalised Pythagorean theorem, and it says: if an e-geodesic and an m-geodesic meet at a Fisher right angle, then KL divergence decomposes additively like squared Euclidean distance.

Maximum likelihood is m-projection

Here is the first payoff. Maximum likelihood estimation is exactly the m-projection we just drew, with a specific meaning of "data point" and "submanifold".

Suppose you have samples from an unknown distribution. The empirical distribution is

which places mass at each data point. Treat this empirical distribution as a point on the manifold of all distributions. The maximum-likelihood estimator for a parametric exponential family is the distribution in the family that maximises the likelihood of the data, which after a short calculation is the same as the distribution that minimises — i.e., the forward KL from the empirical to the model. That is an m-projection of the empirical onto the model family.

So the MLE sits at the foot of the perpendicular dropped from the empirical distribution onto the model family, under the Fisher metric. All the geometric structure we built in Parts 6 and 7 is directly the structure that makes MLE well-defined. The Pythagorean theorem then tells you that, for any other point in the model family, the KL you achieve by moving from the MLE to adds to the irreducible KL between the empirical and the MLE to give the total KL between the empirical and . "Irreducible" means: no amount of moving around inside the family can reduce it. The MLE is the point at which you have squeezed out every drop of forward-KL you can from a finite-dimensional model.

The empirical is typically outside the family. When your data is not drawn from an exponential family — which is every real data set — the empirical distribution is a lattice of Dirac deltas that has nothing to do with your parametric model. The best your model can do is to minimise KL to the empirical, which is exactly the m-projection. The KL at the MLE is a measure of model misspecification. In the limit where your data is drawn from a member of the family, the empirical converges to that member and the KL at the MLE goes to zero. Everywhere else, the Pythagorean decomposition is the precise bookkeeping for how much divergence is due to misspecification (the "reducible" distance from the empirical to the family, measured by KL from empirical to MLE) and how much is due to parameter error (the KL from the MLE to the competing point).

Other algorithms that are projections

Once you see maximum likelihood as m-projection, a long list of other statistical algorithms start looking like projections too. The EM algorithm (Dempster-Laird-Rubin 1977), which we meet in Part 12, is alternating projection between two flat submanifolds: an e-projection step and an m-projection step. The Pythagorean theorem then forces the KL between the two to decrease monotonically, which is the convergence proof. Variational inference is an e-projection of a target posterior onto a simpler variational family. Belief propagation on graphical models is iterated projection on a factor graph. Information bottleneck is a constrained projection. All of these rest on the same Pythagorean bookkeeping, and all of them fail gracefully (or succeed differently) when the submanifolds involved are not dually flat.

The unifying picture is the one we drew in Figure 1: a point, a submanifold, a perpendicular drop, and a decomposition of the KL divergence into two summands along the perpendicular. Every projection-based statistical algorithm is a variant of that picture, and the Pythagorean theorem guarantees that the bookkeeping is exact.

What comes next

We now have the full Pythagorean machinery for dually flat exponential families, with KL as the divergence and the e- and m-connections providing the orthogonal directions. Part 9 generalises this one step further: every strictly convex potential on an affine space induces its own Bregman divergence, its own dually flat geometry, and its own Pythagorean theorem. KL is one special case (the log-partition function as ); squared Euclidean distance is another (half the norm squared); Itakura-Saito, logistic, and a zoo of other divergences fall out of different convex choices. The Bregman generalisation is the framework that holds all of this together and the right place to stop before we switch gears to applications in Part 10.
