α-Connections and Duality

Part 6 of 15. A statistical manifold comes with not one "straight line" but three, and the right one depends on what you are trying to compute. Amari's α-connections are a one-parameter family that interpolates between them and exposes a deep duality between exponential and mixture geometries.

Act I ended with a clean picture: probability distributions are points on a manifold, the Fisher information is the metric tensor, and the KL divergence is the global object whose second-order expansion recovers that metric. If you have seen Riemannian geometry before, you know that a metric alone is not quite enough to do calculus on a manifold. You also need a connection, a rule for how to parallel-transport a vector from one point to a nearby point. Without a connection, you cannot define a geodesic, you cannot differentiate a vector field, and you cannot say what "straight" means.

On a standard Riemannian manifold, the metric determines a unique connection, the Levi-Civita connection, picked out by two requirements: it is torsion-free and it preserves the metric under parallel transport. That was the connection we implicitly used in Part 2 when we drew the Fisher geodesic between two Gaussians as a semicircle in the Poincaré half-plane. On a statistical manifold, however, the Levi-Civita connection is usually the wrong choice. Statistics uses two other connections instead, called the e-connection (for "exponential") and the m-connection (for "mixture"), and the story of Act II is the story of these two connections and their interaction.

The precise framework (Amari's α-connections, 1982 onward) gives a one-parameter family with recovering the Levi-Civita connection we already know and and corresponding to the two new ones. The two new connections are dual with respect to the Fisher metric in a precise sense we will define, and the duality is the engine behind every projection-based algorithm in statistics: maximum likelihood, EM, variational inference, belief propagation. We meet all of these in later parts. This part lays the geometric foundation.

Parallel transport, in pictures

Take a tangent vector at a point on a manifold, and ask: if I move along a curve to a nearby point , what tangent vector at represents "the same" vector I started with? On a flat sheet of paper, the answer is obvious: translate the vector without changing its direction or length. On a curved surface (the Earth's skin, say) the answer is not obvious, and different reasonable rules give different results. The rule you choose is called the connection, and the operation is called parallel transport.

Parallel transport in turn defines geodesics. A geodesic is a curve whose tangent vector is parallel-transported along itself, the curve stays "pointing in the same direction" as you trace it out. On Earth with the metric induced from 3D space, the geodesics are great circles; on the Poincaré half-plane with the hyperbolic metric from Part 2, the geodesics are semicircles. Different connection, different geodesics. The connection is the thing you have to supply, and it is separate from the metric even though one can often be derived from the other.

Why a statistical manifold has more than one natural connection. When you have only one coordinate system that feels natural, a metric determines a unique connection (the Levi-Civita one), and you can forget about connections being a choice. But a statistical manifold has two equally-natural coordinate systems hiding in it: the parameter space the physicist thinks in, and the expectation-value space the statistician thinks in. Each coordinate system wants its own geodesics to be "straight lines", and the connections that make this true are the e-connection and the m-connection. They are genuinely different connections on the same manifold, and neither is the Levi-Civita connection of the Fisher metric.

Why three different answers? Because "straight" depends on what you hold fixed. The e-geodesic holds the natural parameters fixed in a linear sense: at every point along the curve, you are taking a weighted geometric mean of the two endpoint likelihoods. The m-geodesic holds the expectation parameters fixed in a linear sense: at every point, your distribution matches the moments of a linear mixture of the endpoints. The Levi-Civita geodesic holds neither, it is the curve that minimises Fisher arc length under the parallel-transport rule induced by the metric alone, and it falls somewhere in between.

The mixture becomes bimodal in the middle, while the moment-matched Gaussian stays unimodal but fat. They are two different objects that happen to share the same first two moments at every .

The Gaussian family is e-autoparallel but not m-autoparallel. A submanifold is autoparallel with respect to a connection if its geodesics of that connection stay inside the submanifold. The Gaussian family is e-autoparallel, an e-geodesic between two Gaussians is itself a Gaussian, so the family is closed under exponential interpolation. It is not m-autoparallel, an m-geodesic (the literal mixture) leaves the family as soon as you move off an endpoint. The dual is true for a mixture family: it is m-autoparallel but not e-autoparallel. This opposition is the source of every interesting algorithm in Act II.

Dually flat manifolds

One definition more, and we are done with Part 6. A statistical manifold is called dually flat if both the e-connection and the m-connection are flat, that is, if the curvature tensors and both vanish. A flat connection is one for which you can find coordinates that make all the Christoffel symbols zero; equivalently, parallel transport along closed loops is the identity. A dually flat manifold is one on which you can find two such flat coordinate systems, one for each connection.

Dually flat manifolds are the easy case of information geometry (almost everything becomes computable) and they are precisely the exponential families. Every exponential family is dually flat; the natural parameters are coordinates in which the e-connection is flat, and the expectation parameters are coordinates in which the m-connection is flat. The two coordinate systems are related by a Legendre transform, which we meet in Part 7.

The Gaussian family you have been staring at for five parts is the canonical example: two-parameter, dually flat, with the "exponential coordinates" and the "moment coordinates" . Every calculation we did in Act I can be redone in either coordinate system, and the redoing will be easier because the curvature that made the Fisher metric interesting is a curvature of the Levi-Civita connection, not the e- or m-connection. Flip to e- or m-coordinates and the manifold looks flat.

A curvature paradox resolved. The Gaussian family has constant negative Levi-Civita curvature (Part 2), but it is dually flat, both the e-curvature and the m-curvature are zero. How can the same manifold be both curved and flat? The answer is that "curvature" is a property of the connection, not the manifold. A single manifold can carry many connections, and different ones see different geometries. The Levi-Civita connection sees a hyperbolic surface; the e-connection and m-connection see two different flat spaces stitched together by Legendre duality. Which one you want depends on the algorithm you are running.
