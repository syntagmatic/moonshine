Natural Gradient Descent

Part 10 of 15. Vanilla SGD is the right way to descend a loss landscape only if the parameter space is Euclidean. On a statistical manifold it is subtly wrong, and the fix is to pre-multiply the gradient by the inverse Fisher matrix. The resulting algorithm is parameterisation-invariant, second-order-aware, and the reason every variational-inference and reinforcement-learning paper since 2000 has the phrase "natural gradient" in it.

You have done gradient descent before. The loss is a scalar function on some parameter space, you compute its gradient, you take a step in the opposite direction, you repeat. The update rule is

and the choice of step size is the tuning knob that makes or breaks the algorithm. Everyone who has trained a neural network has a war story about a learning rate that was too big and diverged, or too small and stalled. And almost everyone who has had that experience has suspected that the problem is not really the learning rate — it is that "unit step" means different things in different regions of the parameter space, and the gradient you computed has no way of knowing it.

Information geometry is the framework that makes this suspicion formal. When your parameters are the parameters of a probability distribution, the "correct" notion of a unit step is the one given by the Fisher information metric — the same metric that made the Gaussian family hyperbolic in Part 2 and made the Pythagorean theorem work in Part 8. Using the Fisher metric to define "unit step" turns vanilla gradient descent into natural gradient descent, which was introduced by Shun-ichi Amari in 1998 and has quietly become the correct optimiser for most probabilistic models.

Worse: the problem is not actually about the loss. The loss is well-behaved, bounded below, and unimodal. The problem is that the gradient is not expressed in the units that the geometry wants. A "small" Euclidean step in near is a "huge" step in KL — it moves to a distribution that is enormously distinguishable from the starting one. The gradient was right about the direction of improvement; it was wrong about the scale.

The dynamics are smooth, the trajectory looks sensible, and the algorithm converges for a much wider range of learning rates than vanilla SGD.

Why the inverse Fisher matrix? The natural gradient is the gradient raised by the metric: . In Riemannian language, the Euclidean gradient is a cotangent vector (a one-form), and the metric is the object that turns cotangents into tangents. If you want to add the gradient to your current position — which lives in the tangent space — you need to raise it first, and raising means multiplying by the inverse metric. Ordinary SGD skips this step and implicitly treats the Fisher matrix as the identity, which is only true if the parameter space is Euclidean.

Connection to second-order methods

There is an eerie coincidence between natural gradient and Newton's method. Newton's method for minimising a loss uses the inverse Hessian of the loss:

The natural gradient uses the inverse Fisher matrix, which for a maximum-likelihood loss is the expected value of a second-derivative quantity. For correctly-specified exponential-family models, the Fisher matrix and the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood are numerically very close — they agree exactly in expectation when the model is at the truth. So natural gradient descent on an MLE problem is approximately Newton's method, and the two converge at the same rate in the neighbourhood of the optimum.

The practical upshot: natural gradient gives you second-order convergence speed without the instabilities of computing the true Hessian on a complicated non-convex loss. The Fisher matrix is always positive-definite (unlike the Hessian, which can have negative eigenvalues in non-convex regions) and it is usually cheaper to approximate. For large neural networks it has to be approximated further — block-diagonally, Kronecker-factorised, or diagonally — because computing and inverting the full matrix is in the parameter count, and that is untenable for a billion-parameter model. The variants with names like K-FAC, SFN, and KFAC are all approximations of natural gradient, and they are used in production for training deep networks where the approximate second-order direction is worth the extra cost.

Natural gradient in the wild. You may have met natural gradient under other names. In reinforcement learning, it is the update rule of "natural policy gradient" and TRPO (which is natural policy gradient with a trust region). In variational inference, it is the update used by "stochastic variational inference" with the natural-gradient version of the ELBO — because the variational posterior parameters are the natural parameters of an exponential family, and the right descent direction is the natural one. In variational quantum computing, "Quantum Natural Gradient" uses the Quantum Fisher Information Matrix to escape the "barren plateaus" that otherwise stall parameterised quantum circuits. All of these are the same algorithm specialised to different substrates.
