Information Geometry Meets Moonshine

Part 13 of 15. The pivot. Everything in the series so far has been classical applications of information geometry to classical statistics. The last three parts point at an unclaimed territory — the Moonshine ecosystem of modular forms, Monster-group symmetries, and conformal field theories — where the machinery of Acts I and II is conspicuously missing from the standard toolkit and where it has the potential to say something new.

Information geometry's natural habitat is statistics. Everything we have done in the series so far — Fisher metric, KL divergence, exponential families, Pythagorean theorem, natural gradient, EM, Voronoi clustering — is about fitting and comparing probability distributions, which is the core problem of statistics. That is a respectable place for a mathematical framework to live, and it is why Amari's book has been a staple of machine-learning literature for four decades. It is also, we are about to argue, a smaller habitat than it ought to be.

There is a different corner of mathematics where the objects you manipulate are fundamentally statistical but where no one has ever written down a Fisher metric on them. That corner is Monstrous Moonshine, the web of unexpected connections between the Monster sporadic finite simple group, modular forms, and conformal field theories that McKay stumbled into in 1978 and that Borcherds resolved in the 1990s. The sibling Modular Forms series tells that story end-to-end: Part 16 for the McKay coincidence, Part 19 for the Frenkel-Lepowsky-Meurman vertex operator algebra, Part 20 for Borcherds' Fields-Medal proof and the expansion into Mathieu and Umbral moonshine. The modern moonshine ecosystem is vast and extensively studied — but exclusively by the tools of representation theory, algebraic geometry, and string theory. The statistical toolkit, which we have spent twelve parts of this series developing, is almost never applied to it. That is the gap Part 13 is about.

What it does not have is a community of practitioners who think of it that way. The statistical reading of CFT partition functions is, as far as we can tell, essentially nonexistent. CFT people use complex analysis, string theory people use Calabi-Yau geometry, number theorists use modular forms — but nobody writes down the Fisher metric on the moduli space, because the cultures that would do it have not been looking in that direction. Part 13 is about fixing the oversight.

Partition functions are already probability distributions. Every physicist knows this — statistical mechanics starts there — but the observation usually stops short of the statistical-manifold step. Once you have written as a generating function, the set of all CFTs with a given central charge becomes a parameterised family of probability distributions indexed by the moduli , and you have a statistical manifold. The Fisher metric on this manifold is uniquely determined by Chentsov's theorem (Part 3). You can compute it. You can take its geodesics. You can project one CFT onto a submanifold of simpler CFTs. Almost nobody has.

The correspondence table

Here is the dictionary that makes the pivot concrete. On the left, the statistical object that shows up in information geometry. On the right, the Moonshine object it corresponds to under the pivot. Parts 14 and 15 will make some of these rows rigorous; most of the remaining rows are research conjectures that the series is pointing at rather than proving.

Parametric family of distributions Family of conformal field theories at varying moduli τ
Parameter space / manifold Moduli space of the CFT (e.g., complex structure of a K3 surface)
Fisher information metric Weil-Petersson or Zamolodchikov metric on the moduli space
Probability density p(x | θ) Normalised character χ_i(τ) / Z(τ) of a primary field
Log-partition function ψ(θ) log Z(τ), i.e. the free energy of the CFT
Kullback-Leibler divergence Thermodynamic length / relative entropy between modular invariants

Asymmetry of KL c-theorem: information loss under RG flow is unidirectional
Exponential family / dually flat Characters of a rational CFT with their primal/dual structure
Koszul-Souriau moment map j-function as entropic potential on the Lie algebra of the Monster

Some of these correspondences are rigorous. The Fisher metric coinciding with the Weil-Petersson metric on specific moduli spaces (rows 2-4) has been computed directly in the non-linear sigma model literature, most visibly for instanton moduli where the Fisher metric turns out to be three-dimensional hyperbolic space. We will walk through that calculation in Part 14.

Other rows are plausible but not yet proven. The identification of natural gradient descent with renormalisation group flow (row 7) has been explored informally in the holographic-entanglement and tensor-network literature, but a precise statement — "RG flow of a specific CFT equals natural gradient descent of a specific statistical loss" — has not been written down in any single paper we know of.

Finally, some rows are firmly speculative. The last row — the -function as an entropic potential in a Koszul-Souriau dually flat geometry on the Lie algebra of the Monster — is the most speculative item on the list, and Part 15 is honest about that. It is a suggestion about where to look, not a theorem ready to be cited.

What this series is not claiming. We are not asserting that Monstrous Moonshine has been "solved" information-geometrically. The conjectural content of Parts 13-15 is that the statistical-manifold reading of the Moonshine ecosystem has not been systematically done, and that doing it might produce new theorems or at least new pictures. Some of the correspondences in the table above are established results in the existing literature, some are reasonable guesses, and some are marked as speculation. We will try to keep the distinctions visible.

Why the frontier matters

You might reasonably ask: what would we gain from dragging information geometry into Moonshine? Two possible payoffs are worth naming.

The first is unification. Moonshine's existing proofs are impressive but disparate — Borcherds' generalised Kac-Moody algebras, Frenkel-Lepowsky-Meurman's VOA, various string-theoretic derivations, lattice constructions. They agree on the facts but share no single language. Information geometry is a single language — divergences, geodesics, projections — that could in principle hold all of the Moonshine correspondences in one mental model. Even if it failed to produce a new theorem, turning all of Moonshine into a sequence of "compute the Fisher metric on this moduli space" exercises would be a useful pedagogical simplification.

The second is new theorems. There are unanswered questions in Moonshine — why 24 dimensions matter for the Monster, why the Niemeier lattices sort themselves the way they do for Umbral Moonshine, what the physical meaning of the moonshine partition functions really is beyond "the answer comes out right". Some of these questions might have information-theoretic answers. If the Fisher metric on the moduli space of a K3 surface is an invariant of the K3 — and not a derivable quantity from complex structure alone — then it should obey relations that the current algebraic approach misses. Whether it does is an open question.

Neither payoff is guaranteed, and we will not try to oversell them. The series is simply pointing at a door and asking whether anyone has tried opening it.

A soft prerequisite

The rest of Act III assumes you have at least a passing familiarity with the Moonshine ecosystem's vocabulary — modular forms, vertex operator algebras, Calabi-Yau and K3 surfaces, conformal field theories, partition functions, and the Monster itself. If any of these terms are unfamiliar, you are not alone; the gap between "trained statistician" and "Moonshine-literate physicist" is enormous, and bridging it is part of what makes this territory unclaimed. The Moonshine act (Parts 16–20) of the sibling Modular Forms series covers this prerequisite material from the Moonshine side and pairs naturally with Parts 13–15 here. For now, the three parts that follow are self-contained in the sense that they define every term they use, but they will feel more vivid to a reader who has seen the objects before.

What comes next

Part 14 makes one row of the correspondence table concrete. We take the specific case of instanton moduli in a non-linear sigma model — the model of Belavin-Polyakov-Zamolodchikov-style two-dimensional conformal field theory — and compute the Fisher metric on the space of probability distributions over instanton configurations. The answer is a three-dimensional hyperbolic metric, which is also the Weil-Petersson metric on the instanton moduli space, which is also (in a sense to be explained) Euclidean anti-de Sitter space. Three different derivations, one geometric answer.

Part 15 takes the most speculative row and runs with it. We look at the -function as a potential function in a Koszul-Souriau dually flat geometry on the Lie algebra of the Monster group. This is the frontier end of the frontier, and we will be honest about where the rigor runs out.
