Kullback-Leibler Divergence

Part 4 of 15. The global companion to the Fisher metric. Non-negative, zero only when two distributions coincide, and — famously — not symmetric. The asymmetry turns out to be a feature, not a bug.

The Fisher metric is an infinitesimal object: it tells us how much two distributions differ when their parameters differ by a tiny amount. For any real application we need a global version — a single number that quantifies how far apart two given distributions are, not just in the limit of small steps. There is a natural candidate, and you have almost certainly met it before: the Kullback-Leibler divergence, defined in 1951 by Solomon Kullback and Richard Leibler in their paper On Information and Sufficiency.

KL divergence is the backbone of information theory, the loss function hiding behind maximum likelihood, and the thing you are actually minimising when you train a classifier or a variational autoencoder. It is also, notoriously, not a metric: it is asymmetric, it violates the triangle inequality, and it cares which distribution you put first. This part explains what KL is, where the asymmetry comes from, and why it is exactly the right quantity despite its rough edges — and sets up Part 5, where we will see that KL and the Fisher metric are secretly the same object at different scales.

Why the gap? Because KL weights the log-ratio by the first argument. When you put first, you are asking: "on samples drawn from , how much does the log-ratio deviate from zero?" The samples are concentrated near 's mean, where both densities are reasonably sized; the log-ratio is bounded and the average stays moderate. But if you put first, the samples spread out over the full width of , and some of them land far in the tails where 's density is essentially zero — at those samples the log-ratio is very large, because you are dividing a normal-sized number by something that vanishes. The average is dominated by those tail points, and it explodes.

The asymmetry is not a glitch. It is saying something specific and true: approximating a sharp thing with a blurry thing is easy; approximating a blurry thing with a sharp thing is hard. If your model is narrower than reality, the tails of reality are under-covered and the divergence is huge. If your model is wider than reality, the tails of the model waste probability mass but not much divergence. KL makes that asymmetry arithmetic.

Real-world applications exploit this difference routinely.

Mode covering vs mode seeking

Here is the canonical example, and the one you probably care about if you build generative models. Take a target distribution that has two separate modes — say, a mixture of two well-separated Gaussians — and try to approximate it with a single Gaussian. The approximation is necessarily bad, because the model family (single Gaussian) does not contain the target (two Gaussians). The question is: how is it bad?

If you minimise forward KL, , the answer is mode covering. Forward KL penalises any region of the sample space where the target has non-negligible mass but the model has very little — because in such regions the log-ratio blows up at points where is non-zero and is near zero. So the model is forced to place at least some density everywhere the target has density, even if that means stretching itself out to cover both modes and losing sharpness. The result is a broad, blurry Gaussian straddling both targets.

If you minimise reverse KL, , the answer is mode seeking. Reverse KL only penalises regions where the model has non-negligible mass but the target does not. So the model is free to place essentially zero density everywhere the target does not have a mode — it can safely ignore most of the sample space. The optimal solution is to collapse onto a single mode, because any attempt to spread across both modes would put some model density in the "valley" between them where the target has very little density, which would be heavily penalised. Depending on initialisation, you get one of the two modes, sharp and well-fit locally but ignoring the other mode entirely.

Figure 2 shows this dichotomy. The grey curve is a bimodal target (a 50/50 mixture of two well-separated Gaussians). The red curve is the best single-Gaussian fit under forward KL — stretched broad to cover both modes. The blue curves are the two local optima under reverse KL — sharp Gaussians each sitting on one mode and ignoring the other. Neither answer is "correct" in the sense of matching the target; they are both distortions, and they distort in opposite directions because the two KL directions care about opposite things.

In generative modelling, this dichotomy is part of why VAEs tend to blur and GANs tend to mode-collapse. Variational autoencoders maximise an evidence lower bound on the data log-likelihood, which at convergence is forward-KL flavoured (minimising up to an entropy constant) and pushes the decoder toward mode-covering, compounding the blurriness already imposed by a Gaussian reconstruction loss. GANs in the original formulation converge, under an optimal discriminator, to the symmetric Jensen-Shannon divergence, but the widely-used non-saturating training heuristic behaves more like a reverse-KL minimiser and has the mode-seeking character that shows up as mode collapse. The two pathologies are not only about KL direction — architecture and optimisation choices matter too — but the asymmetry of KL is a real and well-understood ingredient.

KL is not a metric

We have been careful to call KL a divergence and not a distance. The terminology matters: a proper metric would have to satisfy symmetry, non-negativity, identity of indiscernibles, and the triangle inequality. KL has non-negativity and identity of indiscernibles; it fails symmetry (spectacularly, as we just saw) and it also fails the triangle inequality.

A simple failure of the triangle inequality: take three Bernoulli distributions with , , and . Compute pairwise KLs and check that is not bounded by the sum of the intermediate KLs. The fact that the inequality can fail at all is enough to disqualify KL as a metric in the classical sense.

So we need a different name for the kind of function KL is. The information-geometry term is divergence, and the formal definition is: a non-negative function of two distributions that vanishes iff they coincide and is smooth enough to induce a Riemannian metric via its second-order Taylor expansion (we will see how in Part 5). KL is the canonical example. Squared Hellinger is another. Jeffreys divergence — which is the symmetrised KL, — is a third. Each has its own geometry, and the choice of divergence is a modelling decision.

Why use something that is not even a metric? Because the asymmetry carries real information that a metric would throw away. Metrics have nice mathematical properties but they also impose a symmetry that nature does not always respect. Information loss, in the physical sense — the arrow of thermodynamic time, the second law, the irreversibility of mixing — is an asymmetric phenomenon. Unmixing cream from coffee is not the reverse of mixing it. KL encodes that asymmetry in its first argument. Symmetrising it (via Jeffreys or Hellinger) is available if you want a metric, but it throws away the directional information — and for most of the classical applications, the direction is exactly what you want to keep.

What comes next

We have a global divergence, and it is asymmetric on purpose. The loose ends are two: how exactly does this global object connect to the infinitesimal Fisher metric we built in Part 2, and how does it give us a working geometry if it is not even symmetric?
