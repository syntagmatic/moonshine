Bregman Divergences

Part 9 of 15. Every strictly convex function gives you a divergence. KL is one. Squared Euclidean distance is another. The unifying object is the Bregman divergence, and all the machinery of Part 8 — Pythagorean projection, dually flat geometry — works for every Bregman divergence, with the convex generator replacing the log-partition function.

Parts 2 through 8 were a long argument that KL divergence on an exponential family is the "right" divergence for statistics, because it recovers the Fisher metric locally, it respects information-preserving transformations, and it satisfies a Pythagorean theorem for e-geodesic / m-geodesic triangles. Somewhere in that argument you may have noticed that the special structure of KL — the fact that it is built from the log-partition function of an exponential family — looked avoidable. Everything we proved used only two facts: that KL is the Bregman divergence of a convex potential, and that the convex potential is the log-partition function of the family. Drop the second fact and keep the first, and you get a much larger universe of divergences, one for every strictly convex function, and every single one of them has the same Pythagorean-theorem-style machinery underneath.

The object is called the Bregman divergence, introduced by Lev Bregman in 1967 in a paper on relaxation methods for convex programming. It sat in a corner of optimisation for two decades before Banerjee, Guha, and Dhillon (2005) and Nielsen-Nock (2009) showed that it is the natural framework for a whole family of clustering algorithms, exponential families, information-theoretic divergences, and optimisation methods. Information geometry is the part of the story that says: every Bregman divergence induces its own dually flat geometry, and picking a divergence is picking a geometry.

Each generator tells you which "shape" of error the divergence cares about. Squared Euclidean is symmetric and flat — it is the divergence of a world with no preferred direction or scale. KL is asymmetric and tail-sensitive — it penalises light-tailed model approximations of heavy-tailed targets. Itakura-Saito is scale-invariant — — which is the right property for audio power spectra, where the absolute magnitude is less important than the shape. Logistic divergence is the one that penalises predictions near the wrong classification boundary most aggressively. Each has its own use, and each is a Bregman divergence of a specific convex .

KL divergence is two things at once. It is the information-theoretic object we met in Part 4 — the expected excess description length. It is also the Bregman divergence of the negative-entropy generator, which makes it look like squared Euclidean distance's cousin. Those two viewpoints converge on the same function because the exponential family's log-partition function is the Legendre dual of the negative entropy, and Bregman divergences are invariant under the dual. Information theory and convex analysis are the same subject when you unwrap them far enough.

Clustering with Bregman divergences

One concrete application to anchor the abstraction. K-means clustering is the algorithm that starts from cluster centres, assigns each data point to its nearest centre, recomputes each centre as the mean of its assigned points, and repeats. It is a staple of unsupervised learning. You have probably seen the claim that "k-means minimises squared Euclidean distance, which is why it is bad for non-spherical clusters". That claim has a precise geometric generalisation.

Banerjee-Merugu-Dhillon-Ghosh (2005) showed that k-means works for any Bregman divergence , not just squared Euclidean. You replace "distance from point to centre" with the Bregman divergence, and you replace "centre is the mean of assigned points" with "centre is the Bregman centroid of assigned points". For squared Euclidean, the Bregman centroid is the arithmetic mean (k-means). For KL divergence, the Bregman centroid is the expectation-parameter average, which for exponential families is the moment-matched distribution. For Itakura-Saito, the centroid is the geometric mean on a log scale. In each case, the optimal centroid minimises the total divergence by exactly the same argument you used to prove that the mean minimises squared Euclidean distance, and the convergence proof comes from the Pythagorean theorem you can specialise from the generic Bregman version.

The practical upshot: if your data is a set of probability distributions, use k-means with KL divergence. If your data is audio power spectra, use Itakura-Saito. If your data is spatial points, use squared Euclidean. Each variant is the right one for its geometry, and the difference between them is exactly the choice of generator.

Natural gradient descent, the main topic of Part 10, is the special case of mirror descent where the Bregman regulariser is the Fisher-metric quadratic form — or equivalently, the local Taylor expansion of KL divergence. So natural gradient is mirror descent with (the log-partition function) as the generator, which is the generator that makes the geometry match the statistical manifold. The entire picture fits together: Bregman divergences give you a family of geometries, mirror descent is gradient descent in any of those geometries, and natural gradient descent is the special case that uses the Fisher-induced geometry.

Bregman is the unifying abstraction behind every geometry in this series. The Fisher metric is the Hessian of a Bregman generator (the log-partition function). KL divergence is the Bregman divergence itself. The natural gradient is a Bregman-regularised descent step. The e- and m-connections are the primal and dual gradient fields of the Bregman pair. Parts 2 through 8 can be read as the special case of "Bregman divergence with equal to an exponential family's log-partition function", and Part 9 is the step that generalises to arbitrary strictly convex potentials. Everything else is a specialisation.

What comes next

Act II now has its full scaffolding. Parts 6 and 7 gave us the e- and m-connections and the dually flat structure of exponential families. Part 8 proved the Pythagorean theorem that makes projection-based statistics work. Part 9 generalised the theorem to arbitrary Bregman divergences. Part 10 takes all of this and turns it into an optimisation algorithm: natural gradient descent, which is the "right way" to do gradient descent on a statistical manifold. It has been rediscovered many times in deep learning under different names, it is now standard in variational quantum computing as "Quantum Natural Gradient", and it is the right way to train most probabilistic models.
