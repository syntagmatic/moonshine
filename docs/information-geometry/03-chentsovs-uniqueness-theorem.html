Chentsov's Uniqueness Theorem

Part 3 of 15. The Fisher metric is not one choice among many. It is the only Riemannian metric on a statistical manifold that is invariant under information-preserving transformations of the data, and therefore the only one measuring distributions rather than their labels.

Part 2 wrote down the score function, took its outer product, and called the result a metric. Why that metric, and not any of the other reparameterisation-invariant Riemannian metrics on the Gaussian half-plane?

Chentsov's 1972 monograph Statistical Decision Rules and Optimal Inference answers: there is no other choice.

The theorem

Chentsov's theorem (1972). On the manifold of probability distributions over a finite sample space, the Fisher information metric is the unique Riemannian metric (up to an overall constant scalar factor) that is invariant under Markov morphisms, i.e., under information-preserving stochastic transformations of the data.

Sufficient statistics, coarse-grainings, relabellings, and noisy channels are all Markov morphisms.

Invariant means: pushing two distributions forward via the same morphism preserves their distance (if no information was lost) or decreases it. Chentsov proved the Fisher metric is the only Riemannian metric satisfying this for every Markov morphism on finite sample spaces.

Why invariance is non-negotiable

A statistical distance is supposed to measure distinguishability — how much a single draw can tell you about which of or produced the data. That cannot depend on outcome labels: the distinguishability of vs. is the same whether faces are called heads/tails, 0/1, or up/down.

Invariance under relabelling alone is mild and leaves many candidates. Chentsov demands invariance under all information-preserving transformations, and only the Fisher metric (up to a scalar) survives.

Compare with Euclidean distance on the simplex. It is smooth, symmetric, satisfies the triangle inequality, and vanishes only when the distributions coincide. It is also wrong for statistics, because it is not invariant under information-preserving transformations.

What Chentsov actually proved, in one sentence

Chentsov's proof asks which Riemannian metrics on the simplex are preserved by every Markov kernel. The answer is the Fisher metric and its scalar multiples. Campbell (1986) extended the result to positive measures, and Ay-Jost-Le-Schwachhöfer (2017) handles continuous sample spaces. Modern treatments recast it as the naturality of a functor from finite sample spaces into smooth manifolds.

The consequence: every distance, geodesic, and metric visualisation in the next twelve explainers is forced by invariance, not chosen aesthetically.

Scalar ambiguity is harmless. The theorem pins the metric down up to a constant. We pick the scalar that matches the expected-squared-score definition from Part 2. Quantum information geometers sometimes scale by to match the Bures metric on pure states; the geometry is the same.
