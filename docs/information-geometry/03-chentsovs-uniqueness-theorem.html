Chentsov's Uniqueness Theorem

Part 3 of 15. The Fisher metric is not one choice among many. It is the only Riemannian metric on a statistical manifold that is invariant under information-preserving transformations of the data, and therefore the only one measuring distributions rather than their labels.

Part 2 gave us the Fisher metric and a satisfying picture: the Gaussian manifold turns out to be the Poincaré half-plane, geodesics are semicircles, and everything is invariant under reparameterisation. A careful reader should feel a residual doubt at this point. We wrote down the score function, took its outer product, called the result a metric, and declared victory. But why that metric? Couldn't we have written down other Riemannian metrics on the Gaussian half-plane, ones that are also invariant under reparameterisation, also diffeomorphism-compatible, also formally legitimate, and called those the metric instead?

The answer is: no, you couldn't, and the reason is a theorem proved by the Russian mathematician Nikolai Nikolaevich Chentsov (often transliterated Čencov) in 1972, in his monograph Statistical Decision Rules and Optimal Inference. The theorem is the hidden foundation of all of information geometry. Without it, the Fisher metric is "a convenient choice". With it, the Fisher metric is the only choice.

The theorem

Chentsov's theorem (1972). On the manifold of probability distributions over a finite sample space, the Fisher information metric is the unique Riemannian metric (up to an overall constant scalar factor) that is invariant under Markov morphisms, i.e., under information-preserving stochastic transformations of the data.

Two phrases need unpacking. "Markov morphism" is a fancy name for a stochastic map that acts on probability distributions. Concretely: you have a sample space , a new sample space , and a conditional distribution that tells you, for each , how to draw a new outcome . Applying a sufficient statistic to your data is a Markov morphism (a deterministic one). Coarse-graining several states into one is a Markov morphism. Relabelling outcomes is a Markov morphism. Observing the data through a noisy channel is a Markov morphism. In all these cases, the resulting distribution on the new sample space is a mixture of the original distribution with itself, weighted by the kernel.

"Invariant" means: if and are two distributions on , and you push them forward to and on via the same Markov morphism, then the Riemannian distance between them should either be preserved (if no information was lost) or should decrease (if some was). This is a sharp, checkable condition, and Chentsov proved that in finite sample spaces, the Fisher metric is the only Riemannian metric that satisfies it for every possible Markov morphism.

Why invariance is non-negotiable

Step back for a moment and ask what we want a statistical distance to mean. The purpose of a distance function on the space of distributions is to quantify how distinguishable two distributions are, in the sense that matters for inference: if you are trying to guess which of or produced the data, how much can you learn from a single draw? That quantity cannot depend on how you label the outcomes in your sample space, because the labels are arbitrary. A coin does not know whether its faces are called "heads and tails" or "0 and 1" or "up and down"; the distinguishability of vs. is the same regardless.

So any statistical distance we take seriously must be invariant under relabelling at minimum. That is a mild condition, it rules out a few pathological candidates but leaves many survivors. Chentsov asks us to take a stronger invariance: not just relabelling, but invariance under all information-preserving transformations of the data, which is a much bigger class. And the remarkable result is that only one metric (up to the scalar ) survives this stronger test.

The contrast with Euclidean geometry on the simplex is instructive. Think of a distribution on outcomes as a point in the probability simplex , a subset of . The naive Euclidean distance between two such points, , is a perfectly respectable function. It is smooth, symmetric, satisfies the triangle inequality, and gives 0 only when the distributions coincide. It is also wrong for statistics, because it is not invariant under information-preserving transformations. Let us make that concrete.

That is the canonical statistical distance on the simplex, and it is the finite-sample-space version of the Fisher metric we derived for the Gaussian in Part 2. Now let us see what it does.

The split operation takes every probability mass on a state and divides it in half, assigning each half to two new sub-states, so three bars becomes four. This is an information-preserving transformation: nothing was lost, because the original state is exactly recoverable as the sum of the two new sub-states.

Watch the two distances. The Fisher-Rao distance is identical before and after the split: the theorem guarantees it. The Euclidean distance, by contrast, decreases every time you split a state, because the split redistributes mass over more coordinates without any geometric justification, the denominator of the squared differences changes, and the sum shrinks. The Euclidean distance is measuring the coordinate representation, not the underlying distinguishability. The Fisher-Rao distance is measuring the distinguishability directly and so cannot notice that the sample space has been relabelled.

Euclidean distance on the simplex is measuring labels, not distributions. When you split a state into two equal halves, the two new sub-states are indistinguishable from each other but they both exist as separate coordinate axes. The Euclidean metric treats them as full-fledged dimensions and adds their squared deviations independently, diluting the total distance. The Fisher-Rao metric never sees them as separate because they do not carry separate information about the parameter, and so it gives exactly the same answer as before. This is the punchline of Chentsov's theorem, observable in a single click.

Fisher-Rao distance on the simplex, drawn

Figure 2 shows the 2-simplex (the triangle of all three-outcome distributions) with Fisher-Rao isodistance contours drawn from a movable reference point. The contours are curves of constant Fisher-Rao distance from the reference, and they are what "circles of radius at this centre" actually look like on a statistical manifold.

Notice that the contours near a corner of the simplex (where one of the probabilities is close to 1 and the others are close to 0) are compressed, a small Euclidean step near a corner corresponds to a big Fisher-Rao step, because a distribution with one dominant outcome is very easy to distinguish from a slightly different one. Near the centre of the simplex, where all three outcomes are equally likely, the contours are relatively dilated, you can change the probabilities a fair bit before the distribution becomes meaningfully different. This is the finite-state analogue of the effect from Part 1: the ruler stretches where distinguishability is low and contracts where distinguishability is high.

The exact contour shape is the finite-state analogue of the hyperbolic Poincaré geometry we drew for the Gaussian. In fact, after the substitution, the contours are spherical caps, circles on the 2-sphere centred at the reference, intersected with the positive orthant. The "compression near corners" is what the sphere looks like near the edge of that orthant.

What Chentsov actually proved, in one sentence

Chentsov's 1972 proof works in the concrete language of statistical decision theory: he considers Markov kernels between finite sample spaces and asks which Riemannian metrics on the resulting simplices are preserved by every such kernel. The answer, after a short calculation, is the Fisher metric and its scalar multiples. Anything else fails invariance under at least one Markov morphism. Modern treatments recast the argument in category-theoretic language, naturality of a functor from finite sample spaces into smooth manifolds, and extend it beyond finite sample spaces; Campbell (1986) extended Chentsov's result to positive measures, and Ay-Jost-Le-Schwachhöfer (Information Geometry, 2017) gives the full continuous-sample-space treatment. We will not redo any of these proofs.

What matters for us is not the proof but the consequence. Every time in the next twelve explainers we draw a distance, a geodesic, or a Fisher-metric visualisation, we are implicitly relying on Chentsov's theorem to say "this is the right picture, not one picture among many". Without the theorem, everything we do is up to an aesthetic preference. With it, the geometry is forced on us by invariance alone.

Scalar ambiguity is unavoidable, and harmless. Chentsov's theorem pins the metric down up to a constant multiplier. In practice we pick the scalar to make the Fisher matrix agree with the expected-squared-score definition from Part 2, and that choice gives the formulas we are using. Different fields sometimes pick different scalars, quantum information geometers often scale by to match the Bures metric on pure states, for example, but the overall geometry (curvature signs, geodesic shapes, uniqueness claims) is the same in every convention.

What comes next

We have a metric, we know it is unique, and we know it measures distinguishability. Part 4 asks: what is the global version of this infinitesimal ruler? If the Fisher metric tells us how fast distributions move under small parameter changes, what is the analogous quantity for large changes, for distributions that are not close to each other? The answer is the Kullback-Leibler divergence, which has its own story, famously asymmetric, famously non-metric, famously foundational in information theory. We will see in Part 5 that locally, KL is the Fisher metric: it is the finite-distance object whose Taylor expansion recovers the infinitesimal ruler we have been working with.
