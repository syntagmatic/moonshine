The Fisher Information Metric

Part 2 of 15. The right ruler for a statistical manifold exists, is unique, and — for the Gaussian family — makes the parameter plane look exactly like the Poincaré half-plane of hyperbolic geometry.

At the end of Part 1 we were stuck with a problem: the parameter labels are a terrible way to measure how far apart two distributions are. We need a ruler that respects distinguishability — a way of saying, at every point on the manifold, "a step in this direction of this size is a change of so-much in the underlying probability distribution". The right ruler is called the Fisher information metric, and deriving it takes less work than you would expect.

The recipe goes like this. Start with the log-density at a point. Take its gradient with respect to the parameters — this is called the score function and it measures how much the log-likelihood would change if you wiggled the parameters. Square it. Average over all possible samples at that point. You now have a matrix whose entries tell you, in intrinsic units, how fast the distribution changes as you move in any given direction. That matrix is the Fisher information matrix, and it is the metric tensor of the statistical manifold. Let us build it slowly.

At other points the score has a small variance and the point is in a region of low information.

This is the form you probably have seen in a machine-learning class, where it appears as the Gauss-Newton approximation to the true Hessian of the loss. In information geometry it shows up as the natural metric tensor on the manifold.

Two perspectives on the same matrix. From the outer-product definition, the Fisher information is a measure of how much the score varies across samples — how much one draw tells you about the parameter. From the Hessian definition, it is a measure of how sharply the log-likelihood is curved near the maximum — how sensitive the fit is to small parameter changes. The two definitions agree because they are two sides of the same object: the local geometry of the log-likelihood.

Fisher ellipses: the ruler made visible

The Fisher matrix is a bilinear form, and the standard way to visualise a bilinear form is as an ellipse. At each point , the set of coordinate displacements for which the quadratic form equals a chosen constant is an ellipse in the tangent plane. We draw one such ellipse at each point on a grid and we get a Fisher ellipse field — a visual proxy for the metric.

Here is that field drawn across the Gaussian manifold. The ellipses are small where the Fisher information is big and large where it is small, and that is exactly backwards from what a naive reader expects. Drag a point around and watch the ellipse at your location stretch and shrink.

A large Fisher ellipse means you can move a lot in coordinate space without being distinguishable. A small ellipse means even a tiny coordinate move corresponds to a real statistical change. Both are saying the same thing: the ellipse is the unit ball of the Fisher metric, and its size in coordinate space is inversely proportional to the statistical sensitivity at that point.

The Fisher ellipse is a ruler. To measure how "big" a direction in tangent space is, divide the coordinate length of by the length of the ellipse axis in that direction. The answer is how many "distinguishability units" you are moving. Big ellipse means many coordinate units per distinguishability unit — cheap to move. Small ellipse means few coordinate units per distinguishability unit — expensive to move.

The factor of two is a uniform rescaling of lengths by , so the Gaussian Fisher manifold is isometric to the ordinary Poincaré half-plane up to that constant stretch. The punchline is that the Gaussian family is a hyperbolic surface of constant negative Gaussian curvature — the same curvature sign and qualitative geometry as classical hyperbolic space, just with distances scaled so the sectional curvature is halved.

This is a famous and completely unexpected fact. You started with something innocuous — a two-parameter family of probability distributions — and by writing down its Fisher matrix you discovered it is the canonical model of non-Euclidean geometry, the space that Lobachevsky and Poincaré studied for reasons having nothing to do with probability. The hidden hyperbolic structure was there all along, waiting for anyone who cared to measure distances the statistically meaningful way.

The consequences are immediate and visual. In hyperbolic geometry, geodesics — the shortest paths between points — are not straight lines. In the Poincaré half-plane they are semicircles whose endpoints lie on the boundary, plus the degenerate case of vertical lines. Translated back to our picture, this means that the shortest path between two Gaussian distributions, as measured by Fisher information, is a curved arc, not a straight line in parameter space.

Drag either endpoint of the geodesic in the figure above and watch the Fisher geodesic (purple, semicircle) and the Euclidean line (amber, straight) trace different paths. The length reported underneath each path is its total Fisher arc length — the number you get by integrating along the path. The purple path is always shorter or equal to the amber path. Notice which way the geodesic curves: it bulges away from the axis, bending upward into the low-information part of the manifold. This is the opposite of what a Euclidean eye expects, and the reason is simple — near small the Fisher metric is enormous, so the geodesic detours through larger- territory where coordinate motion is cheap in Fisher units. Trading a longer coordinate path for a shorter Fisher path is the whole point of a geodesic.

The Fisher geodesic is the correct statistical interpolation between two Gaussian distributions. If you want to morph one Gaussian into another along the "most economical" path — losing the least information, spending the least distinguishability budget — you follow the semicircle, not the straight line. This is the reason the straight-line interpolation between two distributions is almost always wrong for anything that matters.

Straight-line interpolation is parameter lying. When you linearly interpolate in parameter space, you are tracing a path that the metric never endorsed. The path is an artifact of your coordinate choice, not a property of the distributions. The Fisher geodesic is the path that depends only on the distributions themselves, and it almost never coincides with the straight line in any given parameterisation.

This is a good sanity check but also a philosophical one. If the Fisher distance depended on the parameterisation, it would be a property of our labels for the distributions, not the distributions themselves, and it would be useless as a measure of real statistical distinguishability. The whole enterprise of information geometry rests on the fact that this invariance holds — and Part 3 will show that it is in fact the unique Riemannian metric for which this invariance holds, in a sense made precise by Chentsov's theorem.

What comes next

We now have a working ruler for the statistical manifold: the Fisher information metric, derived from the score function, invariant under reparameterisation, and — for the Gaussian family — turning the parameter plane into hyperbolic space. We can measure distances, draw geodesics, and tell the difference between a "big" move and a "small" move without reference to arbitrary coordinates.

Part 3 asks a question we glossed over: why this metric, and not any of the other metrics we could have written down? The answer is Chentsov's uniqueness theorem, which pins the Fisher metric down as the only Riemannian metric on a statistical manifold that is invariant under information-preserving transformations of the data. It is a short, beautiful argument, and it is where the Fisher metric stops being "a convenient choice" and becomes "the only possible choice".
