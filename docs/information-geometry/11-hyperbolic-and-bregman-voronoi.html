Hyperbolic and Bregman Voronoi Diagrams

Part 11 of 15. A Voronoi diagram cuts a space into cells by nearest-neighbour membership. On a statistical manifold, the cells warp into shapes that a Euclidean eye would call impossible — and the shapes are exactly right for clustering distributions.

Act II gave you the tools. Act III puts them to work, starting with the most visually gratifying application of information geometry there is: Voronoi diagrams on statistical manifolds. A Voronoi diagram is one of the oldest structures in computational geometry — given a set of site points in a space, partition the space into cells such that each cell consists of the points closer to one site than any other. The cells are separated by perpendicular bisectors of pairs of sites, and the whole diagram packs a surprising amount of information about the sites into its shape.

The twist when the space is a statistical manifold is that "nearest" no longer means squared Euclidean. The right distance is a divergence — KL, Fisher-Rao, or something more exotic — and the cell shapes change completely when you change it. For exponential families the construction was worked out by Nielsen-Boissonnat-Nock in 2007 and is called the Bregman Voronoi diagram. For location-scale families, the picture is hyperbolic: the cells warp and stretch as they approach the boundary of the half-plane, in exactly the same way that classical hyperbolic Voronoi cells warp near the boundary of the Poincaré disk. The hyperbolic Voronoi structure is the shape the underlying statistical manifold forces on any clustering algorithm that respects its geometry.

Three divergences, three diagrams

Figure 1 draws a Voronoi diagram on the Gaussian half-plane for a set of movable sites, under three divergences: squared Euclidean (which pretends the plane is flat), KL divergence (the statistically-correct asymmetric choice), and Fisher-Rao distance (the Riemannian-geodesic hyperbolic choice). All three produce partitions of the same plane with the same site locations, but the cells look nothing like each other.

The Euclidean diagram is the one you are used to. Cells are convex polygons; boundaries are straight line segments; nothing warps as you approach the boundary of the half-plane. That is exactly the bug: Euclidean distance has no idea that should feel much bigger near than near . It treats a site at and a site at as equally "reachable" from any other site.

The KL diagram is the Bregman Voronoi diagram for the KL divergence, which for Gaussians has the closed form you already know. Its cells are not convex — they bend, following the asymmetry of KL. Where the Euclidean diagram has a straight bisector between two sites, the KL diagram has a curved one whose shape depends on which side of "forward vs reverse" you pick. Nielsen-Boissonnat-Nock's construction gives this diagram as a Laguerre power diagram on the dual expectation-coordinates, but for our figure we just compute "nearest site under forward KL" at every grid cell directly.

The Fisher-Rao diagram uses the actual Riemannian geodesic distance (Part 2's -times-arccosh formula). This is the hyperbolic one — the cells warp toward the boundary just like cells in a Poincaré-half-plane Voronoi diagram, because that is literally what they are. A single cell at small extends only a short distance in but a large distance in ; a cell at large is the opposite. The stretching reflects the Fisher metric's refusal to treat as a flat coordinate.

The cell shape encodes the geometry. You can read the metric off a Voronoi diagram by looking at how a cell deforms. Cells near high-information regions (small ) are small in coordinate terms but carry the same "statistical mass" as cells near low-information regions (large ). Every Voronoi diagram is a discretised picture of its metric, and flipping between the three diagrams in Figure 1 is the same thing as looking at the Gaussian manifold through three different rulers.

Bregman Voronoi diagrams, constructed

For a dually flat exponential family, the construction is a beautiful piece of convex geometry. Start with the log-partition function from Part 7 — the convex potential whose gradient is the map from natural to expectation parameters. The Bregman divergence for this potential is exactly the KL divergence (Part 9), so "KL Voronoi diagram" and "Bregman Voronoi diagram for " are the same object.

Nielsen-Boissonnat-Nock showed that this diagram is equivalent to a power diagram in the dual expectation coordinates, where each site is replaced by a weight derived from . Power diagrams in turn are built from half-spaces — in Euclidean terms, "the set of points closer to site than to site under the weighted distance" — which can be computed in time for sites using standard algorithms. So the Bregman Voronoi diagram is asymptotically no harder to compute than the Euclidean one, despite looking much more complicated.

The equivalence is a special case of a beautiful Legendre-duality trick. In the primal coordinates, cells are bounded by curves of constant KL to a pair of sites. In the dual coordinates, those curves become the straight-line bisectors of the weighted Euclidean power diagram. The Legendre transform you met in Part 7 turns the non-Euclidean geometry of KL into the Euclidean geometry of power diagrams, and Voronoi diagrams become a computation in the dual space. The same Legendre transform makes the Pythagorean theorem of Part 8 look like the ordinary one, and the two tricks are closely related: both exploit the fact that dually flat spaces have a hidden Euclidean structure in the dual coordinates.

Hilbert simplex geometry: the categorical case

The Gaussian half-plane is the canonical location-scale example. For distributions over a finite number of outcomes — categorical distributions living on the probability simplex — the natural geometry is different, and the corresponding Voronoi diagrams deserve their own name. Since the Fisher metric on the -simplex is isometrically embeddable onto the positive orthant of a sphere of radius 2 (via ), a Voronoi diagram for categorical distributions under Fisher-Rao distance becomes a spherical Voronoi diagram — cells bounded by great-circle arcs, as familiar to cartographers as to information geometers.

An alternative is Hilbert geometry, which uses the Cayley-Klein cross-ratio on the interior of a bounded convex domain to define a metric. On the probability simplex, Hilbert distance is a quick-to-compute proxy for Fisher-Rao distance that behaves well numerically and respects the simplex's natural symmetries. Nielsen and Sun (2017) gave a Hilbert-geometry clustering algorithm for categorical distributions that runs in linear time per iteration and is now the workhorse of distributional clustering in NLP and topic modelling. It is the same Voronoi-diagram-as-clustering story, specialised to the simplex.

For more exotic settings — multivariate autoregressive time series, positive-definite covariance matrices, Kähler manifolds of complex parameters — the natural geometry is Siegel space, the space of symmetric positive-definite complex matrices with its own Hermitian metric. Voronoi diagrams on Siegel space are used in modern time-series clustering and in the analysis of quantum channels. The pattern is always the same: identify the natural geometry of the statistical space, compute a Voronoi diagram with respect to that geometry, and use the cells as a clustering primitive.

Why this matters for clustering

The k-means algorithm you know is a Voronoi clustering algorithm: at each iteration, it computes a Voronoi diagram (Euclidean) with the current cluster centres as sites, assigns each data point to its cell, and updates each centre to the arithmetic mean of its assigned points. The arithmetic mean is the Euclidean centroid — the point that minimises the sum of squared Euclidean distances to the assigned points.

Bregman k-means, introduced by Banerjee et al. 2005, is the same algorithm with the Bregman divergence of a convex playing the role of squared Euclidean distance. The Voronoi diagram is Bregman, and the centroid is the "Bregman centroid" — the point that minimises the sum of Bregman divergences. For KL divergence, the Bregman centroid is the expectation-parameter arithmetic mean, which for exponential families is the moment-matched distribution. For Itakura-Saito, the centroid is the geometric mean on a log scale. For squared Euclidean, it is the ordinary mean, and Bregman k-means collapses to classical k-means. Every variant inherits from the Bregman framework the same convergence guarantee: each step decreases the total divergence, and the algorithm halts at a fixed point.

If your data is a set of probability distributions (one per document, per genome, per user), using classical k-means is statistical malpractice. The correct tool is Bregman k-means with KL (or Fisher-Rao, or Hilbert) as the divergence, and the Voronoi diagrams you just drew are exactly the cells that algorithm produces.

The "right" centroid is not always the arithmetic mean. The centroid under KL divergence is the distribution whose moments equal the arithmetic mean of the moments of the assigned points — which for Gaussians is the usual mean and variance, but for non-Gaussian exponential families is something different. The Itakura-Saito centroid is the geometric mean. The Fisher-Rao centroid is a spherical average on the -sphere. Each Bregman divergence has its own notion of "average", and the correct notion depends on what your data is. Getting this wrong silently biases your clustering. Getting it right costs nothing extra — the Bregman framework handles it automatically.
