Hyperbolic and Bregman Voronoi Diagrams

Part 11 of 15. A Voronoi diagram cuts a space into cells by nearest-neighbour membership. On a statistical manifold, the cells warp into shapes that a Euclidean eye would call impossible, and the shapes are exactly right for clustering distributions.

A Voronoi diagram partitions a space by nearest site. On a statistical manifold, "nearest" is a divergence — KL, Fisher-Rao, or something more exotic — and the cell shapes change completely with the divergence.

For exponential families, Nielsen-Boissonnat-Nock (2007) constructed the Bregman Voronoi diagram. For location-scale families, the cells warp toward the boundary of the half-plane, mirroring the classical hyperbolic Voronoi diagram on the Poincaré disk.

Three divergences, three diagrams

Figure 1 draws a Voronoi diagram on the Gaussian half-plane for a set of movable sites, under three divergences: squared Euclidean (which pretends the plane is flat), KL divergence (the statistically-correct asymmetric choice), and Fisher-Rao distance (the Riemannian-geodesic hyperbolic choice). All three produce partitions of the same plane with the same site locations, but the cells look nothing like each other.

The KL Voronoi diagram has curved bisectors that bend with the asymmetry of KL. Forward and reverse KL produce different diagrams.

The Fisher-Rao diagram uses the Riemannian geodesic distance from Part 2. Cells warp toward the boundary because the half-plane is hyperbolic. Small- cells extend short distances in but long ones in ; large- cells the reverse.

Cell shape encodes the geometry. Every Voronoi diagram is a discretised picture of its metric.

Hilbert simplex geometry: the categorical case

For categorical distributions on the probability simplex, the Fisher metric embeds isometrically onto the positive orthant of a sphere of radius 2 via . Fisher-Rao Voronoi diagrams become spherical Voronoi diagrams with great-circle arcs.

Hilbert geometry on the simplex uses the Cayley-Klein cross-ratio. Nielsen and Sun (2017) built a linear-time-per-iteration clustering algorithm on it, now standard in distributional clustering for NLP.

For positive-definite covariance matrices and Kähler manifolds of complex parameters, the natural setting is Siegel space, used in time-series clustering and quantum-channel analysis.

Why this matters for clustering

K-means is a Voronoi clustering algorithm: each iteration computes a Euclidean Voronoi diagram on the current centres and updates each centre to the centroid (arithmetic mean) of its cell.

Bregman k-means (Banerjee et al. 2005) is the same algorithm with a Bregman divergence of instead of squared Euclidean. The centroid becomes the Bregman centroid: arithmetic mean for squared Euclidean, expectation-parameter average for KL, geometric mean for Itakura-Saito.

For data that is a set of probability distributions (one per document, per genome, per user), classical k-means is the wrong tool. Use Bregman k-means with KL, Fisher-Rao, or Hilbert as the divergence.
