EM as Alternating Projection

Part 12 of 15. The Expectation-Maximization algorithm is a geometric thing in disguise. Its E-step is an m-projection, its M-step is an e-projection, and its monotone convergence is a one-line consequence of the Pythagorean theorem we proved in Part 8.

The EM algorithm, introduced by Dempster, Laird, and Rubin in 1977, is the standard tool for fitting latent-variable models — Gaussian mixtures, hidden Markov models, topic models, factor analysis, probabilistic PCA, and a hundred other things. You probably know how to implement it: alternate between computing soft assignments (the E-step) and updating parameters to maximise a weighted likelihood (the M-step), and the algorithm is guaranteed to increase the log-likelihood at every iteration until convergence. The monotone-improvement guarantee is what makes EM reliable in practice, and it is proved in most statistics textbooks via a Jensen-inequality argument that is correct but not particularly illuminating.

Information geometry offers a different proof — shorter, more illuminating, and embarrassingly visual. Amari pointed out in the 1990s that the E-step and M-step are both projections: the E-step is an m-projection and the M-step is an e-projection, in the dual-geometry sense we developed in Part 6. Alternating between m- and e-projections traces out a zig-zag path between two dually flat submanifolds of a larger statistical manifold, and the Pythagorean theorem of Part 8 says that each projection step reduces a certain KL divergence — which is equivalent to increasing the log-likelihood. Monotone convergence is then a corollary, not a separate argument. This is EM as it should be understood.

The setup

We have observed data and we want to fit a parametric model . The model has latent variables — the cluster assignments in a mixture, the hidden states in an HMM, the source genes in a biological model — and the parameters are such that the complete joint distribution is easy to write down, while the marginal that we actually want to fit is hard because it requires summing or integrating out .

Direct maximum-likelihood on the marginal is hard. EM works by introducing an auxiliary distribution over the latent variables and iteratively improving two things:

  • E-step. Given the current parameters , pick to be the posterior . This is a distribution over the latent variables that is consistent with both the data and the current parameters.
  • M-step. Given the current auxiliary , pick to maximise the expected complete log-likelihood . This is a weighted maximum-likelihood update where the weights come from .

The standard textbook proof that each iteration increases uses Jensen's inequality applied to the logarithm. We will not redo it. Instead we will redraw the setup as a geometry problem and read the convergence off the picture.

Two dually flat submanifolds

Consider the manifold of all joint distributions over . This is the manifold the whole EM dance lives on. Inside it, there are two special submanifolds:

  1. The model family : the set of joint distributions of the form for in the parameter space. This submanifold is e-flat — it is an exponential family whose natural parameters are and whose e-geodesics (linear in natural parameters) stay in the family.
  2. The data submanifold : the set of joint distributions whose marginal over matches the empirical data distribution . For each assignment of weights to the latent variables, is a joint distribution whose -marginal is the empirical one. This submanifold is m-flat — it is the set of joints whose marginal is pinned to a given point, and it is closed under m-geodesics (mixtures).

The two submanifolds intersect in the single point that would be "the model that exactly reproduces the data" — which in general does not exist, because the model family is too small to contain the empirical. The EM algorithm is trying to get as close as possible to while staying on , by alternately projecting one onto the other.

Two flat submanifolds, two projections. The model family is e-flat, which means it is closed under e-geodesics. The data family is m-flat, which means it is closed under m-geodesics. The projection from one onto the other must be the orthogonal projection in the dual sense: m-projection onto (because the projection must land on an e-flat submanifold), and e-projection onto (because the projection must land on an m-flat submanifold). Any other choice would fail to have a unique minimiser.

E-step = m-projection

Here is the first of the two reinterpretations. The E-step, reframed, is: given the current model parameters , find the point on (the data submanifold) that is closest in KL to the current model distribution . This is the m-projection of the model onto the data submanifold — the m-geodesic from the model drops a perpendicular onto the data submanifold, and the foot of the perpendicular is the new auxiliary .

A short calculation shows that the m-projection foot is exactly , the posterior over latent variables given data under the current parameters. This is the standard formula for the E-step. Every textbook writes it as "compute the posterior"; the geometric reading is "drop an m-perpendicular from the model onto the data submanifold". They are the same operation.

M-step = e-projection

Now the dual step. The M-step says: given the current auxiliary , find the point on (the model family) that is closest in KL to . This is the e-projection of the data-submanifold point onto the model family — the e-geodesic drops a perpendicular onto , and the foot of that perpendicular is the new parameter vector .

A short calculation shows that the e-projection foot is exactly the parameter that maximises the expected complete log-likelihood under . Standard textbooks write "maximise "; the geometric reading is "drop an e-perpendicular from the data-submanifold point onto the model family". Same operation.

Monotone convergence, geometrically

Here is the payoff. Each E-step reduces the KL distance from the model to the data submanifold (because that is what an m-projection does). Each M-step reduces the KL distance from the data-submanifold point to the model family (because that is what an e-projection does). The total KL between the current and the current can only decrease, and the Pythagorean theorem of Part 8 then says that the log-likelihood can only increase. Monotone convergence of EM is a one-line consequence of the alternating-projection interpretation. No Jensen's inequality, no clever bounding arguments — just two projections and the Pythagorean theorem.

Notice how the first step usually moves the components decisively toward their targets — the m-projection jumps across to a much better auxiliary, and the e-projection snaps the parameters into something nearly right. Subsequent steps are smaller and smaller, with the log-likelihood curve flattening. After ten or so iterations you are sitting essentially at the MLE of the true generating distribution. You can also reset and re-generate the dataset.

The "zigzag" path in parameter space is exactly the alternating-projection trajectory: each E-step moves you perpendicular to the current , and each M-step moves you back onto . The Pythagorean theorem guarantees that each zig and each zag reduces a KL divergence, and the algorithm cannot go backwards. It can stall at a local maximum if the initialisation is pathological, because EM is a local optimiser — the Pythagorean theorem gives monotone convergence, not global convergence.

What EM is not

One subtlety worth stating plainly. The alternating-projection picture describes EM as it applies to exponential-family mixtures with complete-data likelihoods in exponential form. That covers Gaussian mixtures, Bernoulli mixtures, Poisson mixtures, multinomial mixtures (i.e. LDA's core step), and HMMs with exponential-family emissions. It does not cover every use of "EM" in the wild — there are variants that relax the exponential-family assumption, variants that use stochastic or variational approximations to the E-step (Stochastic EM, Variational EM), and variants that replace the M-step with a partial gradient step (Generalised EM). Each of these breaks part of the clean geometry and gets weaker or different convergence guarantees.

The picture also relies on the model family being dually flat, which for exponential families is automatic and for everything else is not. Part 9's Bregman divergences give you a more general version where "dually flat" gets replaced by "flat with respect to the chosen Bregman generator", and the EM algorithm can be generalised accordingly — this is the basis for the "Bregman EM" variants used in signal processing and information-theoretic clustering. We will not develop the general case here, but it is a direct extension of what we just drew.

EM is not the only alternating projection in statistics. Variational inference is e-projection of a target posterior onto a variational family — same picture, single projection instead of alternating. Belief propagation on a graphical model is iterated projection on a factor graph — many projections, loopy variant. Mean-field approximations, Gibbs sampling (in its deterministic-update guise), and convex clustering are all alternating-projection or single-projection schemes. Once you have the geometric interpretation, whole classes of algorithms collapse onto the same mental model: two families, drop a perpendicular, decrease a divergence.
