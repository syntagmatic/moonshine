Parameterised Distributions as a Manifold

Part 1 of 15. A probability distribution is not a list of numbers. It is a point, and the set of all Gaussians is a two-dimensional curved surface whose shape we are about to learn how to draw.

The first lesson of information geometry: a set of coordinates is not the thing it labels. Ask a statistician what a Gaussian distribution is and they write down . These two numbers are not the distribution; they are a label, and the same distribution could be written as (natural parameters), (moment parameters), or in many other ways.

The set of all Gaussian distributions is a two-dimensional curved surface. The set of all Bernoulli distributions is a one-dimensional interval. The set of all mixtures of two Gaussians is a four-dimensional manifold with a kink at the boundary. Each space has its own geometry, and that geometry governs how statistical algorithms behave on it.

What does it mean to call a parameterised family a manifold, and what do you see when you look at the two-parameter Gaussian family from that angle?

A historical framing

C. R. Rao's 1945 paper derived the Cramér-Rao bound and noted, almost as an aside, that the Fisher information matrix defines a Riemannian metric on the space of distributions. The bound became famous; the geometric observation sat dormant for thirty years until Chentsov (1972) proved this is the unique metric invariant under information-preserving transformations (Part 3). Amari's 1985 monograph turned the pair of results into a discipline.

A parameterised family of distributions is a manifold whose points are the distributions themselves. Parameters are local coordinates on that manifold, the way latitude and longitude are coordinates on Earth. The geometry does not depend on the coordinate chart.

Why the parameters are the wrong ruler

If the manifold is curved, coordinate distance is not real distance. A step of 0.01 in one coordinate can be enormous at one location and imperceptible at another — same as how a 0.1 step in learning rate means something different at lr=0.01 versus lr=10.

The blue filled curve is a Gaussian; the red dashed curve is another Gaussian with the same coordinate offset . Left: visibly different — half as tall, twice as wide. Middle: still distinct. Right: almost identical, indistinguishable without a large sample. Same coordinate step, three different statistical realities.

The headline number in each panel is KL divergence. The leftmost is about 200x the rightmost despite identical coordinate change.

The correct notion of distance is distinguishability, not parameter change. Two distributions are close iff they are hard to tell apart from samples.

The point of information geometry is to build a ruler that measures moves on the manifold by distinguishability rather than by arithmetic on the coordinate labels. That ruler exists, it is unique, and it is the Fisher information metric. We meet it in Part 2.
