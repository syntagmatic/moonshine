Parameterised Distributions as a Manifold

Part 1 of 15. A probability distribution is not a list of numbers. It is a point — and the set of all Gaussians is a two-dimensional curved surface whose shape we are about to learn how to draw.

The first lesson of information geometry is that you have been confusing a set of coordinates for a thing. Ask a statistician what a Gaussian distribution is and they will write down two numbers, . These numbers are not the distribution. They are a label you assigned to the distribution, and you could have chosen a different label — you could have written the same distribution as , using the so-called natural parameters, or as , using moment parameters, or in a hundred other ways. The distribution itself is the underlying shape. The labels are bookkeeping.

This may feel like hair-splitting. It is not. Once you grant the distinction, a whole mathematical landscape opens up. The set of all Gaussian distributions is a two-dimensional curved surface. The set of all Bernoulli distributions is a one-dimensional interval. The set of all mixtures of two Gaussians is a four-dimensional manifold with a sharp non-exponential kink at the boundary. Each of these spaces has its own geometry — its own notion of distance, of curvature, of "straight lines" — and that geometry is what governs how statistical algorithms behave on it. This series is the story of that geometry.

We begin at the beginning: what does it mean to call a parameterised family a manifold, and what do you see when you squint at the simplest example, the two-parameter Gaussian family, the right way?

A historical framing

The insight is usually credited to the Indian statistician C. R. Rao, who in 1945 published a short paper, Information and the accuracy attainable in the estimation of statistical parameters, deriving what is now called the Cramér-Rao bound and — almost as an aside — showing that the Fisher information matrix defines a Riemannian metric on the space of distributions. The bound became famous; the geometric observation sat mostly dormant for thirty years. Cencov (also transliterated Chentsov) picked it up in 1972, proving that the Fisher metric is not just a natural metric but the only metric invariant under information-preserving transformations (we return to this theorem in Part 3). In 1985 Shun-ichi Amari published Differential Geometrical Methods in Statistics, the monograph that turned Rao's observation and Chentsov's uniqueness theorem into a fully-fledged discipline. Everything in the next fourteen explainers rests on work these three people did.

The idea is worth stating plainly before any formalism:

A parameterised family of distributions is a manifold whose points are the distributions themselves. The parameters are local coordinates on that manifold, the way latitude and longitude are local coordinates on the Earth's surface. The manifold has an intrinsic geometry that does not depend on which coordinate chart you picked, in exactly the way that the shape of the Earth does not depend on whether you measure locations in degrees or in miles from Greenwich.

Saying it out loud is the easy part. Believing it requires a picture. Let us draw the picture.

"Differentiable" says that if you move smoothly in parameter space the distribution you represent changes smoothly too — no sudden jumps, no discontinuities. "One-to-one" says that two different labels never refer to the same distribution, which rules out pathological parameterisations where, say, and produce identical distributions.

which is two-dimensional. Every point in that half-plane corresponds to exactly one Gaussian density, and every Gaussian density corresponds to exactly one point. The correspondence is the whole story, and it is a remarkably tight one: the distribution is the point, and there is no information about the distribution that is not already encoded in its location on the half-plane.

The two pictures are equivalent, but they train very different intuitions. The left picture makes you think of a distribution as a shape you can sample from. The right picture makes you think of it as a location you can move.

The distribution is the point. Not the label you attached to it, not the list of moments, not the sample histogram — all of those are projections, summaries, or approximations. The manifold is the set of all the distributions you could possibly write down in the given family, and each one is a single indivisible element of that set. Once you accept that, everything that follows is just the natural geometry of living on such a set.

Why the parameters are the wrong ruler

Now we meet the first uncomfortable fact. If the manifold is curved, then coordinate distance is not real distance. Moving by 0.01 in one coordinate direction can mean something enormous at one point on the manifold and something imperceptible at another. You have experienced this already if you have ever tuned a hyperparameter: a step of 0.1 in learning rate does something very different at learning rate 0.01 than at learning rate 10. The statistical equivalent is much starker.

In the leftmost panel, the two curves are visibly different — the red curve is half as tall and twice as wide, which is the kind of shift you could spot confidently from a handful of samples. In the middle panel, the curves are still distinct but closer. In the rightmost panel, the two curves lie almost on top of each other — you would need a large sample to tell them apart at all. Yet the coordinate step is identical in all three cases.

The headline number in each panel is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the two distributions. KL measures how much information is lost when you mistake one distribution for the other, and it is the quantity that really ought to be governing "distance" on the manifold. The leftmost panel has a KL value roughly two hundred times larger than the rightmost, even though the parameter change is identical. This is the failure of coordinate distance, laid out as a three-panel exhibit.

The correct notion of distance on a statistical manifold is distinguishability, not parameter change. Two distributions are close if they assign similar probabilities to similar outcomes — that is, if they are hard to tell apart from samples. They are far if they assign wildly different probabilities to the same events. Every sensible measure of distance on the manifold must degenerate to distinguishability in some form, or it is lying to you about what "close" means.

And here is the punchline of Part 1: the whole point of information geometry is to build the right ruler — the ruler that measures moves on the manifold by distinguishability rather than by arithmetic on the coordinate labels. That ruler exists, it is unique, and it has a name: the Fisher information metric. We meet it in Part 2.

Dimension is the first invariant

Before we leave Part 1, one more observation that often goes unspoken. A manifold has an intrinsic dimension, and that dimension is independent of the coordinate system. No matter how you choose to parameterise the Gaussian family — as , as , as natural parameters , or as any other smooth invertible reparameterisation — the manifold is two-dimensional, because two is the smallest number of labels you need to pick out a unique Gaussian.

That matters because it tells you, before you have done any calculation, how many directions you can move in. A Gaussian has two degrees of freedom. A Bernoulli distribution, , has one. A categorical distribution on outcomes has , because the probabilities must sum to 1. An unconstrained multivariate Gaussian in dimensions has , counting the means plus the free entries of the symmetric covariance matrix. These are not parameterisation choices — they are intrinsic facts about the manifold itself.

Dimension also tells you what kind of thing the manifold can be. A one-dimensional manifold is a curve: locally it is just an interval, and it cannot be "curved" in the interesting sense (curvature requires at least two directions to bend). A two-dimensional manifold is a surface, and surfaces can be genuinely curved — think of the difference between a flat sheet of paper and the skin of a balloon. That is why the Gaussian family is the simplest example where information geometry has anything visible to say: at one dimension lower, there is no geometry to speak of; at two dimensions, the Fisher metric has room to bend and the picture comes alive.

Why two dimensions is the sweet spot. One-dimensional families are too simple — the geometry is trivial. Three-dimensional and higher families are too complicated to draw on a screen. The Gaussian family, at exactly two dimensions, is the smallest family where the curvature of the Fisher metric is visible, and it is the canonical example we will return to again and again. When we get to the Poincaré half-plane in Part 2, it will be this exact picture, drawn again with a different metric overlaid on top.

What comes next

We have done two things. First, we have agreed that a parameterised family of distributions is a manifold, and that the manifold's points are the distributions themselves. Second, we have agreed that coordinate distance is the wrong way to measure how far apart two distributions are, because the same coordinate change can correspond to vastly different statistical distinguishabilities. The missing piece is a ruler that respects distinguishability — a Riemannian metric on the manifold, built out of the distributions themselves rather than the coordinate labels.

That ruler is the Fisher information metric, and Part 2 is devoted to meeting it. We will derive it from first principles, show that for the Gaussian family it produces a metric of constant negative curvature — the Poincaré half-plane — and use it to draw geodesics between Gaussians that would otherwise look like arbitrary curves. By the end of Part 2 the half-plane we just drew will be equipped with its true intrinsic geometry, and the coordinate-distance ruler will have been quietly retired for good.
