Robot Arms

Inverse kinematics, finding joint angles that reach a target, usually has many solutions. With joint angles and end-effector position on the same parallel axes, brushing the target picks them out.

Collision Courses treated two aircraft as one point in an 8-dimensional state space. A robot arm gives another such space. With N revolute joints it has N joint angles, so each configuration is a point in N-dimensional configuration space, and forward kinematics maps it to the end-effector position. This part asks the inverse question, which configurations put the end effector on a given target. In parallel coordinates a configuration is a polyline across N joint-angle axes. Adding axes for the end-effector position lets one polyline carry both the input and the output of forward kinematics.

Inverse kinematics as visual search

In parallel coordinates, inverse kinematics is a search. Sample many configurations, draw them as polylines, and keep the ones that pass through the target region on the end-effector axes.

Drag the target in the arm view (or Tab to it and use the arrow keys), or adjust link lengths and tolerance.

Figure 1. A planar 3-link arm. Every sampled configuration that reaches within tolerance of the target appears as a polyline on the three joint-angle axes.

For targets deep in the reachable workspace, many configurations succeed and the family is dense. Near the boundary of the workspace it thins, and beyond it no sample reaches the target.

Configuration Space Sampling

Plotting uniformly random configurations across all five axes, three joint angles and two end-effector coordinates, shows how joint space maps to the workspace.

Brush on the x or y axes to select configurations that reach a region of the workspace.

Figure 2. 500 random configurations on 5 axes.

Brushing the end-effector axes and reading off the joint-angle axes solves inverse kinematics by selection. The boundary of the reachable workspace is an envelope in the sense of article 3, and asking which configurations reach a target is a containment query as in article 6.

Joint Limits

Real joints have limits; an elbow cannot rotate a full 360 degrees. In parallel coordinates a limit is a bounded range on an axis, with the forbidden part shaded.

Configurations that enter a shaded region violate a constraint. As the valid set shrinks, the reachable workspace shrinks with it, sometimes considerably.

Drag the red boundary lines on the PC axes to adjust joint limits, or toggle limits on/off.

Figure 3. Joint limits and the reachable workspace, with limits toggled on or off.

Without limits, every joint angle is available. With limits, part of each solution family becomes physically impossible, and the shading marks that part.

Redundancy: 2-Link vs. 3-Link

A 2-link arm has two degrees of freedom for a two-dimensional target, so the problem is exactly determined. Most reachable positions have two solutions, "elbow up" and "elbow down."

A 3-link arm has one degree of freedom more than the target needs, which makes it redundant. Each reachable target has a continuous family of solutions, along which the end-effector stays fixed while the joints move.

Drag the target in either panel, or Tab to it and use the arrow keys.

Figure 4. Redundancy comparison. Left: a 2-link arm produces two polylines (elbow up, elbow down) for most targets. Right: a 3-link arm produces a continuous family. The extra joint angle is the self-motion degree of freedom.

In the plot, the 3-link family is a band of polylines with the same end-effector values. It is a one-dimensional manifold, the arm's self-motion manifold, and at each solution its direction is the null space of the arm's Jacobian. In general, away from singular configurations, N joints reaching a K-dimensional target leave a solution manifold of dimension N − K, and more joints give a motion planner more solutions to choose from.
