Inside or Outside

Inselberg's hstar duality turns containment in a convex set into a visual test in parallel coordinates. A point is inside exactly when its line, extended past the axes, stays within the band swept by the boundary.

Standard point-in-convex-region tests use half-plane intersections, linear programming, or signed area. The parallel-coordinates equivalent is purely geometric.

The hstar is the family of curves in the parallel-coordinates plane corresponding to the boundary of a convex set. The containment test reads it directly: extend an interior point's polyline past the axes into a full line and it stays inside the band; an exterior point's line leaves it somewhere, though not always between the axes.

The Hstar: Boundary Becomes Envelope

Recall from Surfaces You Can't See that curves in Cartesian space produce families of line segments in parallel coordinates, and those families have envelopes. The hstar is the specific envelope that arises from the boundary of a convex set.

For a bounded convex region (a circle, say), every boundary point maps to a PC segment. The family sweeps out a region bounded by two envelope curves pinching at the endpoints — a generalized hyperbola, hence hstar (h*).

The envelope of this family satisfies two conditions simultaneously: the family equation and the derivative with respect to the parameter vanish. For the circle, this produces the characteristic bowtie shape, widest at the height of the center and pinching to points at the extremes of the circle's vertical extent.

The Containment Test

Here is Inselberg's result, stated precisely:

The test. Put the X1 axis at t = 0 and the X2 axis at t = 1, so a point P = (p1, p2) is the line y(t) = (1 − t)p1 + t p2, and its polyline is the piece with 0 ≤ t ≤ 1. For a closed convex set S, the lines of all its points fill a band: at each t, y ranges over the interval from the smallest to the largest value of (1 − t)x1 + t x2 on S. P lies in S if and only if its whole line, for every real t, stays inside that band.

The reason is the separating-line theorem. Each t measures S and P along one direction, (1 − t, t). If P is outside S, some direction separates them, and the line of P leaves the band at the corresponding t. Directions with both components positive correspond to t between the axes; the others correspond to t outside [0, 1]. So the part of the test you can see between the axes is necessary but not sufficient. Convex Polygons and Piecewise Hstars

When the convex set is a polygon, the band is bounded by straight pieces. The extreme value of a linear function on a polygon is always taken at a vertex, so each piece of the band's boundary is the line of one vertex. The lines of all the points on one edge pass through a single point, the dual of that edge's line from The Duality, and the band's boundary turns a corner there.

The more vertices the polygon has, the more closely its hstar approximates the smooth bowtie of a circle. A triangle produces a hstar with sharp corners. A dodecagon is nearly smooth. In the limit, a polygon with infinitely many vertices is a circle, and the hstar converges to the smooth envelope.

Ellipses and the Hyperbolic Bowtie

The name hstar comes from its connection to the classical hyperbola. For a circle, the envelope is symmetric. But an ellipse with eccentricity greater than zero produces an asymmetric bowtie where the envelope curves are segments of actual hyperbolas.

Intersections and Hulls

The test turns set operations into band operations. A point lies in S1 ∩ S2 exactly when its line stays in both bands, so testing an intersection means testing against the intersection of the bands. (The band of S1 ∩ S2 itself can be narrower than that intersection, but the two tests accept the same points.) Going the other way, the largest and smallest values over the convex hull of S1 ∪ S2 are the larger and smaller of the two sets' values, so when the sets overlap, the band of the hull is the union of the two bands.

Figure 4. Two overlapping circles. The first view shades their intersection and, in parallel coordinates, the region inside both bands. The second shades the convex hull of their union and the union of the two bands, which is the hull's band.

Three Dimensions: Hstars Between Each Axis Pair

In three dimensions, a convex set has a band between axes 1 and 2 and another between axes 2 and 3. Each band is the two-dimensional test applied to a projection: the band between X1 and X2 tests whether (x1, x2) lies in the shadow of the set on that coordinate plane. Being inside both bands is therefore necessary, and it is enough for a box, but not for a general convex set: a point can sit in both shadows and still miss the set. A tetrahedron shows it.

For a tetrahedron in 3D, each face is a triangle. The hstar between each adjacent pair of axes is the piecewise-linear envelope of the face projections.

Visual Signatures of Convexity

The hstar reveals more than containment. It reveals whether the set is convex in the first place. Inselberg showed that convex boundaries produce envelope curves that are smooth (no cusps), while non-convex boundaries produce envelopes with cusps or self-intersections.

This provides a visual convexity test: examine the hstar. If the envelope is cusp-free, the boundary is convex. If cusps appear, the set has concavities. The location of the cusp in PC tells you where the concavity occurs on the original boundary.

The purely geometric test. The containment test requires no linear programming, no half-plane intersection, no signed area computation. In the plane it reduces containment to a visual question: does the extended line stay inside the band? The algebraic test, a system of inequalities, and the geometric one accept exactly the same points.

In the same limitation holds: the band between each pair of adjacent axes tests one two-dimensional shadow, and shadows on adjacent coordinate planes do not determine a general convex set. Inselberg's representation of convex hypersurfaces in higher dimensions uses more than these pairwise bands for exactly this reason.
