Polytopes in Four Dimensions

The six regular 4-polytopes drawn as polyline families next to their wireframes, their edges read off as indexed points, and what parallel coordinates can and cannot show about their symmetry, duality and combinatorics.

A regular 4-polytope cannot be seen directly. A stereographic wireframe shows a tangle of edges. Parallel coordinates show one polyline per vertex, and the rules of the earlier articles turn edges into points.

Each vertex is a point in , so with four axes it becomes a polyline with three segments. An edge is a segment of a line in , and from The Duality a line in four dimensions is recorded by three indexed points, one between each pair of adjacent axes, where the polylines of any two of its points cross.

Edges as indexed points

Each indexed point is the crossing of the two endpoint polylines between one pair of adjacent axes. Regularity pushes the indexed points into symmetric clusters that the wireframe hides. When two endpoints share a coordinate on both axes of a pair, as adjacent vertices of the tesseract do on all but one axis, their segments coincide there and that indexed point is not defined.

The indexed points record every edge, and so the whole edge graph, but the picture is not an invariant of the polytope. It depends on where the polytope sits in coordinates and on the order of the axes; rotate the polytope and the indexed points move and regroup. Two different polytopes can have similar-looking polyline bundles, and the same polytope can look very different after a rotation.

Symmetry

A symmetry permutes the vertices and preserves the edges, so it permutes the polylines. Rotate a polytope in one coordinate plane and, at special angles, every vertex lands where another vertex was: the polyline bundle returns to its starting pattern. Figure 4 detects those angles by comparing the rotated vertex set with the original, rather than assuming them. With these coordinates, the tesseract (vertices (±1, ±1, ±1, ±1)) and the 24-cell (permutations of (±1, ±1, 0, 0)) both return every quarter turn in each coordinate plane. The full symmetry groups are much larger, of order and , but most of their rotations are not in a coordinate plane.

Duality

Every convex polytope has a dual, with a vertex for each cell of the original: vertices swap with cells and edges with faces, and the Schläfli symbol reverses, . The tesseract has 16 vertices, 32 edges, 24 square faces and 8 cubic cells; its dual, the 16-cell , has 8 vertices, 24 edges, 32 triangular faces and 16 tetrahedral cells.

Figure 5. A polytope (blue) and its dual (red) shown in wireframe projection (left) and parallel coordinates (right). In the PC view, the dual's polylines interleave with the original's. A self-dual polytope's dual is a rotated and rescaled copy of it, but since rotation changes the polyline picture, the two patterns need not look alike. Drag the wireframe to rotate.

Among the six regular 4-polytopes, two are self-dual. The 5-cell is, as every simplex is. The 24-cell is the other: 24 vertices and 24 octahedral cells, 96 edges and 96 triangular faces. Among regular polytopes in three or more dimensions, it is the only self-dual one that is not a simplex, and it has no analogue in any other dimension.

One thing the crossings between axes do not record is the edge structure itself. Whether the segments of two polylines cross between axes i and i + 1 depends only on whether their i-th and (i + 1)-th coordinates come in opposite orders. That is a fact about coordinates and the axis order, and it changes when the polytope is rotated or its axes are permuted, whether or not the two vertices share an edge. The edges live in the indexed points of vertex pairs, not in which polylines happen to cross.
