The Pareto Front

When no solution wins on every criterion, the interesting set is the one where nothing can improve without something else getting worse.

A car design wants high fuel efficiency, fast acceleration, high safety, low price and large cargo space, and no single design maximizes all five. The set of designs where no objective can be improved without worsening another is the Pareto front (Vilfredo Pareto).

Formally, a solution dominates a solution if is at least as good as on every objective, and strictly better on at least one:

A solution is Pareto-optimal if no other solution dominates it. In a two-objective scatterplot the Pareto front is part of the boundary of the feasible region. All axes here are oriented so that up is better.

Two objectives

In parallel coordinates, a solution that dominates another has a line lying above it at both axes. So a Pareto-optimal solution is a line with no other line above it at both ends.

Hover or tap a point in the scatterplot or a line in the PC view to highlight the corresponding solution in both views.

Figure 1. 200 solutions with two objectives. Left: scatterplot with the Pareto front in red. Right: the same solutions in parallel coordinates.

If the front were a smooth curve, its points would map to a family of lines whose envelope is the dual curve, as in article 3. With 200 discrete candidates the red lines trace a rough version of that envelope.

Car design trade-offs

Take 100 car designs scored on fuel efficiency, acceleration time, safety rating, price and cargo space. Fuel efficiency trades off against acceleration, and price against safety. A scatterplot matrix of the five would need 10 panels and still miss three-way interactions. Parallel coordinates show all five at once.

Hover or tap any polyline to see the design's values on all five objectives.

Figure 2. 100 car designs with 5 objectives. Red polylines are Pareto-optimal, gray ones are dominated.

The slopes between axes show trade-off rates. A steep segment from fuel efficiency to acceleration means a large sacrifice in speed for a small gain in mileage, and a shallow one means a mild trade-off.

Interactive Dominance Testing

Dominance is another containment test, in the spirit of article 6: a solution is dominated exactly when it lies inside the cone of some Pareto-optimal solution. In parallel coordinates, a dominated polyline never rises above the dominating one on any axis. Selecting a solution splits the others three ways:

  • Green: solutions that dominate the selected one
  • Red: solutions the selected one dominates
  • Gray: solutions that trade off with the selected one (neither dominates)

Click any polyline to select it, or focus the chart and use the arrow keys.

Figure 3. 15 solutions with 4 objectives, with the selected one in blue. A Pareto-optimal solution never has any green.

Adding Objectives

Adding objectives grows the front. With two objectives only a small fraction may be non-dominated. With ten, nearly everything is Pareto-optimal, because it becomes unlikely that one solution beats another on every objective.

Weighted-Sum Selection

In the end a decision-maker has to choose one solution. A common approach is the weighted sum: give each objective a weight and maximize the total. Different weight vectors pick different points on the Pareto front. Weighted sums can only reach points on the convex hull of the front, so a Pareto-optimal design in a dent of the front is never chosen for any weights.

Drag the weight sliders, or click a red polyline (or use the arrow keys on the chart) to search for weights that make it the winner. The weighted-sum optimum is in blue. Dashed red lines are Pareto-optimal designs that sit in a dent of the front.

Figure 5. The car designs from Figure 2 with adjustable weights.

With k objectives a scatterplot matrix needs panels, and radar charts become unreadable beyond a handful of solutions. In parallel coordinates each new objective adds one axis.
