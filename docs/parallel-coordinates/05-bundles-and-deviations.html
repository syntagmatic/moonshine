Bundles and Deviations

A cluster shows up in parallel coordinates as a tight bundle of polylines, and an outlier as a polyline that breaks away from one at the axes where it is unusual.

Article 4 was about which pairs of variables sit next to each other. This part reads the polylines themselves, looking for groups of similar points and for points that don't fit.

Similar points produce similar polylines, so a group of points forms a visible bundle whose width reflects the group's spread and whose path traces its center. An outlier is a polyline that leaves its bundle at particular axes, and since each axis is one dimension, those axes name the dimensions where it is unusual.

Tight bundles

The UCI Wine dataset has 178 wines from three grape cultivars grown in the same region of Italy, each measured on 13 chemical properties. The cultivars form three bundles that separate on several dimensions, most clearly flavanoids, color intensity, proline and od280/od315 dilution.

A scatterplot matrix would need 28 panels for the 8 variables shown here. Parallel coordinates shows all eight variables at once, with the three classes as three bundles heading in different directions.

Hover or tap a polyline to isolate it. Drag vertically on an axis to brush it; tap the axis again to clear.

Figure 1. Wine data, eight of the 13 measured features. Cultivar 1 (blue) runs high on proline and flavanoids, cultivar 3 (orange) is high on color intensity and low on od280/od315, and cultivar 2 (green) has the lowest alcohol and color intensity.

The bowtie

Points clustered in an ellipse between two adjacent variables map to segments that fill a hyperbolic "bowtie." Its outline comes from the envelope construction of article 3: each point on the ellipse's boundary gives a segment, and as the point goes around the ellipse, those segments have a generalized hyperbola (an h-star) as their envelope.

The waist comes from the convergence point of a line . Within a cluster, the slopes between pairs of points are similar, so their convergence points pile up in one place. The stronger the correlation, the narrower the waist.

Figure 2. An elliptical cluster (left) and its bowtie (right). As the green dot traces the ellipse boundary, its segment sweeps across the parallel coordinates, and the envelope of those segments is the bowtie.

A perfectly correlated cluster (r = 1) sends every segment through one convergence point, and the bowtie collapses to a pinch. As the correlation weakens, the convergence points spread and the bowtie widens. The sign sets where the waist sits: to the right of the right axis for positive correlation, between the axes (a crossing pattern) for negative. Data with several clusters has one bowtie per cluster, and they separate in parallel coordinates about as well as the clusters separate in the data.

Deviations from the bundle

An outlier often has one or two unusual values and is ordinary everywhere else. Its polyline follows the bundle on most axes and breaks away at the unusual ones, which tells you which dimensions are responsible.

Drag the red dots on the axes or use the sliders to adjust the outlier's deviation in each dimension.

Figure 3. A cluster of 80 points (blue) and one outlier (red). Each slider sets the outlier's deviation on one dimension, in standard deviations.

Move one slider and the red line leaves the bundle at that axis alone. Push two adjacent sliders and the break spans a wider stretch. Push two that are far apart and you get two separate breaks in an otherwise ordinary polyline.

Figure 4. Manufacturing measurements on six dimensions. The dashed gray line is the mean; strokes on each axis show the selected part's deviation, the bars give its squared z-scores, and the table lists the numbers, with the largest contributors highlighted.

The total distance says how unusual a part is but not where. An outlier at D = 8.3 might owe all of it to one dimension (a spike defect) or spread it over three (a drift defect), and the two call for different fixes. The distance also depends on the reference distribution. With several clusters, a point at the edge of cluster A might sit squarely inside cluster B, so its distance means something only relative to the right one.

Brushing

Brushing finds both bundles and deviations. Drag a range on any axis to highlight the polylines passing through it, and the highlighted subset shows its structure across the other axes. Restrict every axis to its normal range and the polylines left out are the outliers.

Go back to Figure 1 and hide the class colors. Brushing flavanoids high picks out cultivar 1, and brushing color intensity high picks out cultivar 3, so the three clusters can be recovered by hand. Article 7 treats brushing geometrically.
