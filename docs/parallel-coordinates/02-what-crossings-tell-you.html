What Crossings Tell You

The crossings between two adjacent axes follow from the point-line duality, and they show how those two variables are correlated.

The previous article showed that a Cartesian line maps to a convergence point at , whose horizontal position depends only on the slope. Data that roughly follows a line inherits that behavior, so the way segments cross between two adjacent axes says something about how those two variables are correlated.

Negative Correlation and the X-Pattern

Negatively correlated variables follow a line with negative slope, and a negative slope puts the convergence point between the axes: when , the denominator is greater than 1, so lies between 0 and 1.

Segments that run from high on the left to low on the right have to cross the ones running the other way, which makes an X. The stronger the correlation, the more tightly the crossings gather around the convergence point, and at they all pass through it.

Positive Correlation and Parallel Flow

With a positive slope the convergence point sits to the right of the right axis, and the segments run high-to-high and low-to-low in a near-parallel flow. At they are exactly parallel. For steeper slopes () the point wraps around to the left of the left axis, so the segments do converge, but outside the strip between the axes.

That gives a quick visual test for any adjacent pair of axes. Lines crossing between the axes mean negative correlation, lines running parallel mean positive correlation, and a tighter pattern means a stronger one.

Reading a Real Dataset

The Iris dataset has four measurements on 150 flowers from three species, and its adjacent axis pairs look quite different from each other. Between petal length and petal width the segments run nearly parallel, a strong positive correlation. Between sepal width and sepal length the pattern is more scattered, a weaker relationship.

Coloring by species splits each pattern into three. The correlation within a species can differ from the pooled one, and the picture shows where.

The segments between two axes are the pairs of values themselves, one segment per data point, so together they carry the full joint distribution of those two variables. The crossings are where that distribution becomes easy to see: they pile up where many pairs share a linear trend and thin out where the data has none.

Correlation versus Independence

Correlated data gives structured crossings, tight for strong correlations and looser for moderate ones. Independent data scatters them across the space between the axes.

Shuffling one variable while holding the other fixed destroys the correlation, and the structure dissolves into noise. This is a visual version of a permutation test for independence.
