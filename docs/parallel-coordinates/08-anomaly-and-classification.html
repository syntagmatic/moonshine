Anomaly and Classification

Two applications of one idea: drift away from a bundle, or jump between bundles. Same perceptual mechanism, different baselines.

Multivariate profile detection is one thesis with two flavors. A chemical reactor running normally produces readings whose polylines fall inside a tight bundle; when something goes wrong, an aberrant polyline escapes that bundle. A patient arriving with symptoms produces a polyline that belongs to one of two overlapping bundles, healthy or diseased; the diagnosis is the assignment. In both cases the perceptual task is the same, ask where a polyline sits relative to a reference population, but the reference differs. Process control has one nominal bundle and asks whether a new profile has drifted. Diagnosis has two competing bundles and asks which one a new profile joins.

This explainer walks both cases end to end. Industrial chemistry first, where the reader plays operator and drags sensor readings out of spec. Then medicine, where the reader moves sliders on a hypothetical patient and watches a nearest-centroid classifier flip between diagnoses. The closing section returns to the shared mechanism: drift and classification are the same geometric question with different reference sets.

Drift: Process Control

A chemical reactor is instrumented with sensors everywhere. Inlet temperature, outlet temperature, pressure, flow rates, chemical concentrations, catalyst level, pH. Each sensor produces a time series. A control room operator needs to monitor all of these simultaneously, detect when something drifts out of specification, and figure out which variable is the root cause.

Each time step is a single observation across all variables, which maps to a polyline. The full history of normal operation produces the nominal bundle. Deviations appear as polylines that escape the bundle, and the pattern of the escape points toward the fault.

The faded bundle represents 200 historical readings during normal operation. The bold polyline is the current state of the reactor, draggable on any axis. This is an operator's dashboard: one glance reveals whether the process is healthy or stressed.

Figure 1. Chemical process monitor. The faded bundle is 200 normal readings. The bold polyline is the current state, draggable on each axis. Dashed lines mark spec limits (roughly ±3σ of the nominal distribution for each sensor). Axis labels turn red when the current reading exceeds spec.

Normal is a cluster, faults are outliers. Normal operation is a cluster in 8D space, and the bundle makes it visible. Faults are polylines that deviate from the bundle on specific axes; the pattern of deviation encodes the nature of the fault.

Drift Detection

Not all faults happen suddenly. Some processes drift over time: a catalyst slowly deactivates, a heat exchanger fouls, a sensor calibration slips. In a table of numbers, a slow drift in one or two variables out of eight is easy to miss. In parallel coordinates it becomes visible if you color the readings by time.

A simulated drift causes outlet_temp to shift upward by 20°C and concentration_B to shift downward by 0.08 mol/L. The time coloring makes the drift visible as a gradient across the bundle on the affected axes, while unaffected axes show blue and red interleaved.

Fault Propagation and Axis Ordering

Variables in a chemical reactor are not independent. Inlet temperature drives outlet temperature. Temperature affects reaction kinetics, which determines concentrations. Placing causally related variables on adjacent axes makes these chains visible as smooth, parallel bundles between the axes. Placing unrelated variables adjacent produces a tangled mess.

A greedy correlation algorithm starts with the variable pair that has the highest absolute Pearson correlation, then repeatedly extends from either end by appending the variable most correlated with the current endpoint. The result places causally related sensors adjacent, making the process chain legible.

Drag axis labels to reorder manually, or click "Suggest order" to let the correlation algorithm arrange them.

Figure 3. Axis reordering by pairwise correlation. The algorithm places strongly correlated variables adjacent, making the causal chain inlet_temp → outlet_temp → concentration_A → concentration_B visible as smooth bundles. The small r= annotations between axes show the Pearson correlation of the adjacent pair.

Causal chains become visible. When axes are ordered by correlation, transitions between adjacent axes are smooth. In a drift event, you can read the chain: which variable shifted first, which followed, and which was unaffected. This is how operators trace a fault from symptom back to root cause.

Alarm Regions and Brushing

An alarm fires when a variable exceeds its limit. But a single alarm tells you nothing about the system state across other variables. Brushing an axis selects the alarm readings on that axis and immediately shows which other axes are also abnormal for those readings. This is the multi-dimensional view that a threshold alarm on a single variable cannot provide.

Ten alarm readings (red) are constructed to violate limits on one or more axes with realistic fault patterns (e.g., an outlet_temp spike paired with a concentration_B drop), against 200 normal readings in gray.

Classification: Diagnosis

Switch domain. A patient arrives with a set of numbers: blood pressure, heart rate, cholesterol, ST depression on the electrocardiogram. Each number alone is ambiguous, a resting blood pressure of 140 is normal for one person and alarming for another. Diagnosis is inherently multidimensional: the doctor evaluates the entire profile.

The geometry shifts too. Process control had one nominal bundle and asked is the new polyline inside it? Diagnosis has two bundles (healthy, diseased) and asks which one does the new polyline join? The bundles overlap on some axes and separate on others. The gap between them is the classification boundary, the geometric trace of the decision surface separating the classes.

Discriminating Dimensions

Not all axes contribute equally to distinguishing the classes. We can quantify each axis's discriminating power using Cohen's d, the difference in class means normalized by the pooled standard deviation:

A Cohen's d near zero means the two classes overlap almost completely on that axis. A large absolute d means the axis separates the classes well. Rules of thumb call 0.2 a small effect, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. Sorting by separation places the most discriminating dimensions adjacent, concentrating the visual separation.

Figure 6. Parallel coordinates with a bar chart of Cohen's d separation scores. The axes with the highest bars are the most diagnostically useful. Sorting by separation groups the discriminating axes together, making the class boundary more visible.

Axis order matters for diagnosis, too. The same principle that helped us trace a fault chain in process control helps us see a classification boundary here: when the most discriminating axes are adjacent, the crossing patterns between classes become more pronounced. Correlation ordering finds causal chains; Cohen's-d ordering finds discriminative chains. Both are instances of the same axis-ordering game.

Classifying a New Patient

Given the two class bundles, a new patient can be classified by asking: which bundle is closer? A nearest-centroid classifier computes the Euclidean distance (on standardized axes, each feature divided by its pooled standard deviation) to each class centroid and assigns the patient to the closer group.

Drag the dots on the axes or use the sliders to adjust the patient's measurements.

Figure 7. A new patient's polyline (bold black) against the faded class bundles. Adjust the sliders or drag the dots directly on the axes to move the polyline and watch the nearest-centroid prediction update. The decision boundary is the hyperplane equidistant from the two centroids in standardized 6D space.

The decision surface is not visible as a single line; it is the implicit boundary embedded in the gap between the two bundles. Moving the patient's profile continuously across that gap makes the prediction flip. The flip is geometric, not statistical: the polyline crossed a hyperplane, and the hyperplane's trace in parallel coordinates is exactly the region where the bundles meet.

Young patients who develop heart disease tend to have more extreme values on the discriminating axes, because disease at a young age is rarer and requires stronger risk factors. Older patients show more overlap between classes: age itself becomes a risk factor, blurring the distinction on other axes. This is the geometric expression of a well-known clinical principle, the diagnostic profile is age-dependent.

One Mechanism, Two Baselines

Both halves of this explainer answered the same question, where does this new polyline sit relative to a reference population?, with different answers plugged in for "reference."

In process control the reference is a single nominal bundle. The question reduces to a one-sided inside/outside test, and the answer is binary in a permissive way (inside is fine; outside is an alarm whose shape still has to be read). In diagnosis the reference is two competing bundles. The question becomes a two-sided proximity test, and the answer is categorical (closer to A or closer to B). A nearest-centroid rule over standardized axes is the simplest realization; more sophisticated classifiers (LDA, QDA, SVMs) replace the centroid with a richer summary of the bundle but play the same geometric game.

A third case hides in plain sight: classification with >2 classes is just diagnosis with more bundles, and anomaly detection with multiple known fault modes is diagnosis with "normal" as one of the bundles. The same plot carries all of them. What changes is how many reference bundles you draw and what question you ask of the new polyline against them.
