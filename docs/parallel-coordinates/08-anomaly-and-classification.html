Anomaly and Classification

Monitoring a chemical process and diagnosing a patient both come down to reading where a new polyline sits against bundles of known cases: one normal bundle for drift, two for diagnosis.

Part 7 selected regions of data space by brushing. This part uses bundles and brushes on two practical questions. Process control has one nominal bundle and asks whether a new profile has drifted out of it. Diagnosis has two competing bundles and asks which one a new profile joins. Both are answered by where a polyline sits relative to a reference, first in a chemical reactor, then in heart disease data.

Drift: Process Control

A chemical reactor has sensors for inlet and outlet temperature, pressure, flow rates, concentrations, catalyst level and pH. The operator watches all of them for drift and has to find the cause when something goes wrong.

Each time step is a polyline across all variables. The full history of normal operation forms the nominal bundle; faults are polylines that leave it, and the axes they leave on point to the cause.

The Chemical Process Monitor

Drag the blue dot on any axis (or Tab to it and use the arrow keys) to change the current reading. Axis labels turn red when the value moves outside the normal bundle.

Figure 1. Chemical process monitor. The faded bundle is 200 readings from normal operation, and the bold polyline is the current state. Dashed lines mark spec limits (roughly ±3σ of the nominal distribution for each sensor). Axis labels turn red when the current reading exceeds spec.

Normal operation is a cluster in 8D space, and the bundle is how that cluster looks here. A fault leaves the bundle on particular axes, and which axes it leaves on says what kind of fault it is.

Drift Detection

Some processes drift slowly, as when a catalyst deactivates or a heat exchanger fouls. A slow drift in two of eight variables is easy to miss in a table and easier to see when readings are colored by time.

A simulated drift causes outlet_temp to shift upward by 20°C and concentration_B to shift downward by 0.08 mol/L. On those two axes the colors sort into a gradient across the bundle. On the others, blue and red stay interleaved.

Figure 2. Readings colored blue (early) to red (late). Toggle to compare with a stable process.

Fault Propagation and Axis Ordering

Reactor variables depend on each other. Inlet temperature drives outlet temperature, which sets the kinetics, which determines the concentrations. Placing causally related variables next to each other gives smooth bundles, and unrelated neighbours give a tangle. The greedy ordering from Axis Order starts with the pair of highest |Pearson r| and extends either end with the most correlated remaining variable.

Drag axis labels to reorder manually (or Tab to a label and use the arrow keys), or click "Suggest order" to let the correlation algorithm arrange them.

Figure 3. Axis reordering by pairwise correlation. The chain inlet_temp → outlet_temp → concentration_A → concentration_B shows up as smooth bundles. The small r= annotations between axes show the Pearson correlation of the adjacent pair.

With the axes in this order, a drift event can be read along the chain: which variable shifted first, which followed, and which stayed put. Operators use this to trace a fault from the symptom back to its cause.

Alarm Regions and Brushing

An alarm fires when one variable exceeds its limit, and says nothing about the others. Brushing the alarm range on that axis shows which other axes are abnormal for the same readings.

Ten alarm readings (red) are constructed to violate limits on one or more axes with realistic fault patterns (e.g., an outlet_temp spike paired with a concentration_B drop), against 200 normal readings in gray.

Drag vertically on any axis to brush (or Tab to an axis and use the arrow keys). Polylines passing through the brushed range are highlighted.

Figure 4. Normal readings (gray) and alarm readings (red). Try brushing the top of outlet_temp.

Classification: Diagnosis

A patient arrives with a blood pressure, a heart rate, a cholesterol level and an ST depression. Each number alone is ambiguous, so the doctor reads the whole profile. The question is now which of two bundles the polyline joins, and the gap between the healthy and diseased bundles is the classification boundary.

Heart Disease Profiles

The data below is the Cleveland Clinic portion of the UCI Heart Disease dataset: 299 patients from 1988, leaving out four whose vessel count is missing. The six axes are age, resting blood pressure, serum cholesterol, maximum heart rate achieved during exercise, ST depression induced by exercise (oldpeak), and the number of major coronary vessels visible on fluoroscopy. Blue is no heart disease, red is disease present.

The bundles aren't cleanly separated on every axis. Age and cholesterol overlap heavily. Max heart rate, oldpeak, and the major-vessel count separate the classes: healthy patients reach higher heart rates, disease patients have higher oldpeak, and major-vessel count sits at 0 for four in five healthy patients but for only a third of the disease patients.

Discriminating Dimensions

Cohen's d measures how well one axis separates the classes: the difference in class means divided by the pooled standard deviation.

When d is near zero, the classes overlap almost completely on that axis. Rules of thumb call 0.2 a small effect, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. Sorting the axes by |d| puts the most discriminating ones side by side.

Figure 5. 299 patients from the Cleveland heart disease data. The two classes form overlapping but distinguishable bundles. Below, a bar chart of Cohen's d for each axis.

With the discriminating axes adjacent, the crossing patterns between classes are easier to see. In the reactor, correlation ordering put causally linked variables side by side; this ordering groups the axes that tell the classes apart.

Classifying a New Patient

The simplest way to classify a new patient is to ask which bundle is closer. A nearest-centroid classifier computes the Euclidean distance (on standardized axes, each feature divided by its pooled standard deviation) to each class centroid and assigns the patient to the closer group.

Drag the dots on the axes or use the sliders to adjust the patient's measurements.

Figure 6. A new patient's polyline (bold) against the faded class bundles, with the nearest-centroid prediction. The decision boundary is the hyperplane equidistant from the two centroids in standardized 6D space.

Moving the profile across the gap between the bundles flips the prediction at the moment the patient's point crosses the hyperplane equidistant from the two centroids.

Age changes the clinical picture: the same cholesterol level carries different risk at 35 than at 70. Brushing the age axis picks out a subgroup and shows how the diagnostic profiles shift.

Max heart rate separates the classes much better under 50 than over 60, where the two bundles nearly coincide on that axis. Maximum heart rate falls with age for everyone, which squeezes the gap. Oldpeak separates about equally well at every age.

Comparing the Two Tasks

Process control compares a polyline with one nominal bundle and asks whether it is inside. Diagnosis compares it with two bundles and asks which is closer. Nearest centroid is the simplest version of the second; LDA, QDA and SVMs replace the centroid with richer summaries of each bundle. Multi-class classification adds more bundles, and anomaly detection with several fault modes is diagnosis with "normal" as one of them.
