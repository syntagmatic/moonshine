The Duality

A point in Cartesian space becomes a polyline in parallel coordinates, and a line becomes a point. The rest of the series builds on this inversion.

In Cartesian coordinates, a point sits where its values meet, which limits the display to two or three dimensions. Parallel coordinates draw the axes side by side and connect the values with line segments, so an N-dimensional point becomes a polyline with N−1 segments.

Alfred Inselberg worked this out as a mathematical duality in the 1980s. Points map to lines, lines map to points, and the mapping preserves geometric structure.

Line to Point

Now take points that all lie on one Cartesian line. Each maps to a segment in parallel coordinates, and all of those segments pass through a single point, called the convergence point or indexed point.

Where the Convergence Point Falls

Because the denominator is , the sign and size of the slope decide where the point lands, and so its horizontal position tells you what kind of relationship the two variables have.

Slope: 0.5

Figure 4. The convergence point as the slope changes.

A negative slope (m < 0) puts the convergence point between the axes, and the segments cross in an X; at m = −1 it sits exactly at the midpoint. At m = 0 it lands on the right axis, with every segment fanning from a different height on the left to the same height on the right. For 0 < m < 1 it moves to the right of the right axis and the segments run roughly parallel. At m = 1 they are exactly parallel. Past that, for m > 1, the point reappears to the left of the left axis and the segments cross again, in a different pattern from the negative case.

The Projective Perspective

At m = 1 the formula divides by zero, and the convergence point sits at infinity. As the slope approaches 1 the point runs off to the left or right, and at m = 1 it wraps around through infinity. The duality lives in the projective plane, where points at infinity are ordinary points, so this case needs no special treatment.

From Two Dimensions to N

In N dimensions a line is fixed by two of its points. With N axes those two points give two polylines, which cross once between each pair of adjacent axes, N−1 times in all. These crossings are the line's indexed points.

A line in N dimensions has 2(N−1) degrees of freedom, and N−1 indexed points with two coordinates each carry exactly 2(N−1) numbers. The encoding loses nothing.
