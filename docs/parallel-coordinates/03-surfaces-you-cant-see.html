Surfaces You Can't See

A hyperplane in N dimensions can't be drawn directly, but its image in parallel coordinates has a recognizable structure, and curves and surfaces leave traces that carry their geometry.

The duality sends points to polylines and lines to convergence points. It also extends to hyperplanes, curves and surfaces.

A smooth curve is determined by its tangent lines. Each tangent maps to a convergence point, and as the tangent rolls along the curve its convergence point traces a new curve in parallel coordinates, the dual curve. That same curve is also the envelope of the segments that represent the original curve's points: the curve they all touch.

With equal coefficients the polylines form a symmetric fan. When one coefficient is much larger than the others, the polylines pinch through a narrow band on that axis. A negative coefficient reverses the slope of the segments across its axis.

One linear equation removes one degree of freedom from N variables, and that single constraint is enough to give the polylines visible structure where unconstrained data would scatter.

Cusp-Inflection Duality

Under this duality, cusps and inflections trade places. A cusp in Cartesian space (a sharp point) becomes an inflection in the dual curve, and an inflection (where curvature changes sign) becomes a cusp.

The reason is that point and tangent swap roles. At an inflection the point moves on smoothly but the tangent stops turning and turns back, so the tangent's convergence point stalls and reverses, which is a cusp. At a cusp the point stalls and reverses while the tangent keeps turning, which gives an inflection in the dual.

Figure 3. A curve in Cartesian space and its dual in parallel coordinates. The cuspidal cubic gives an inflection; the cubic with an inflection gives a cusp.

Convexity Detection

A smooth convex curve has no inflection points, so its dual has no cusps. A smooth closed curve that is not convex must have inflections somewhere, and they show up as cusps. A glance at the dual tells you whether the shape is convex.

Figure 4. A shape and its dual in parallel coordinates. The ellipse gives a smooth curve; the star gives a cusp at each of its inflection points.
