Symplectic Integrators

Part 4 of 15. A Hamiltonian flow preserves a 2-form on phase space; a good integrator must preserve a discrete cousin of that 2-form or it will hemorrhage energy. Forward Euler loses, RK4 loses slowly, Verlet wins, and the reason is a three-line algorithm whose Jacobian is a product of shears.

A molecular-dynamics run simulates Newton's laws for millions of particles over billions of time steps. Run it for long enough and something has to give. If the integrator does not respect the geometric structure of the underlying equations, what gives is the thing the physics was trying to say: energy drifts, orbits unwind, the numerical system describes a world that is heating up or cooling down for no physical reason at all.

The discipline that fixes this is called geometric integration. Its central insight is that Hamilton's equations do not merely describe how points move in phase space; they describe a flow that preserves a particular antisymmetric 2-form. Any numerical scheme that preserves a discrete analogue of that form will conserve a slightly-shifted energy exactly, and will therefore have bounded energy error for all time. Any scheme that does not will have energy error that grows without bound.

This is the cleanest illustration in the series of the principle the title advertises: choose a discretisation that honours the symmetry, and Noether's theorem survives in discrete form. A careless discretisation breaks time-translation symmetry of the discrete flow and energy drifts. A careful one does not.

Hamilton's equations, in one line

Fix a smooth function H(q, p) on phase space ℝ2n, with coordinates q (position) and p (momentum). Hamilton's equations of motion are

For a particle of mass m in a potential V(q), take H = p2/(2m) + V(q); then dq/dt = p/m and dp/dt = −V′(q), which is Newton's F = ma. The Hamiltonian formulation is exactly equivalent, but it exposes a structure that the Newtonian form does not: the flow on phase space preserves an antisymmetric bilinear form, the symplectic 2-form

Preserving ω means: for any two infinitesimal displacements δ1 and δ2 in phase space, the oriented area ω(δ1, δ2) = δ1q · δ2p − δ1p · δ2q is the same before and after the flow. Equivalently, the Jacobian M of the flow satisfies MT J M = J, where J is the standard symplectic matrix with +I and −I off the diagonal. In one degree of freedom ω is just signed area in the (q, p) plane, and preservation is Liouville's theorem: phase-space volume is conserved.

Any symplectic map has Jacobian determinant 1 (because det MTJM = (det M)2 det J). But determinant 1 is strictly weaker than symplectic: volume-preservation allows shearing, twisting, all manner of deformation. Symplecticity is a stronger, more geometric constraint that kills exactly the failure modes we want to rule out.

Three integrators on one pendulum

Pick the simplest non-trivial Hamiltonian system: the pendulum, with H(q, p) = p2/2 − cos q. Its level sets are the closed curves that any physics textbook shows: small oscillations near (0, 0), separatrices at energy 1, rotations for energy > 1. The true flow preserves every one of those level sets exactly, because H is the first integral.

Three candidate integrators, each with a single time step h.

Forward Euler is the textbook first try. Two lines. Obviously first-order accurate. Easy to differentiate: the Jacobian is

whose determinant is 1 + h2 cos q. At a stable equilibrium (small q, cos q ≈ 1) the determinant is strictly greater than 1, so phase-space volume inflates on every step. Liouville's theorem is violated by a factor that compounds geometrically. The orbits spiral outward.

RK4 is the workhorse of scientific computing. Fourth-order accurate per step, so the per-step error is O(h5) and global error is O(h4). But its Jacobian is not symplectic either; the order-4 accuracy buys you very small coefficients in the energy drift, but the drift is still there, and on long runs it accumulates.

Velocity Verlet is three lines. A half-kick to momentum, a drift to position using the new momentum, another half-kick. It is only second-order accurate, so short-term it is worse than RK4. But its Jacobian is a product of three shears:

Each factor is a shear matrix, a linear map of the form I + nilpotent, whose determinant is exactly 1 and which preserves ω exactly. A product of symplectic maps is symplectic. So Verlet's update map is exactly symplectic, not approximately, not to leading order in h: exactly, for any h, as a rational function of h. That is the thing that matters.

Simplicity is the point. Verlet is three lines. It has no free parameters. It uses only one force evaluation per step (reusing the end-of-step force as the start-of-next-step force). It is time-reversible. It is explicit (no nonlinear solve). And it preserves the symplectic 2-form exactly because each of its three sub-steps is a shear, and shears are area-preserving in the most elementary way imaginable.

RK4 has four force evaluations per step, higher per-step accuracy, and yet on long runs it loses to a scheme that throws away everything except the right geometric structure. The moral: for Hamiltonian dynamics, the form of the update matters more than its order of accuracy.

Why Euler inflates, why Verlet does not

The difference is not about accuracy per step. For small h and short runs, RK4 is strictly more accurate than Verlet. The difference is structural.

Forward Euler's one-step map q, p ↦ q + hp, p − h sin q has Jacobian

with det = 1 + h2 cos q. Near the stable equilibrium cos q > 0, so det > 1 and the map stretches the plane. A small cloud of initial conditions of area A0 becomes a cloud of area A0(1 + h2)n ≈ A0 · exp(n h2) after n steps. The orbit itself, which is a closed curve in the true flow, unwinds into an outward spiral. Energy, which is roughly quadratic near equilibrium, grows exponentially.

Velocity Verlet's one-step map is the composition of three shears. Writing K(p) = (h/2)(−sin q) for the half-kick and D(q) = hp for the drift, the update is
p ← p + K; q ← q + D(p); p ← p + K. Each sub-step leaves one coordinate alone and shifts the other, so each sub-step has Jacobian determinant 1 and is symplectic. Their composition is symplectic. The Jacobian of the full step Verlet(q, p) has det = 1 exactly, not just to leading order in h. Phase-space area is preserved step by step, period.

Liouville's theorem, in miniature. The statement "a Hamiltonian flow preserves phase-space volume" is not a suggestion or an asymptotic claim; it is a theorem about the continuous-time equations. A numerical integrator either preserves the discrete analogue or it does not. The moment you use a non-volume-preserving method, you are no longer simulating a Hamiltonian system; you are simulating some other dynamical system that happens to agree with the Hamiltonian one to order hk for a few steps.

This is why symplectic integrators are non-negotiable in molecular dynamics, orbital mechanics, accelerator physics, and lattice gauge theory. Over a 10-billion-step Hamiltonian Monte Carlo run, "non-symplectic but high-order" is a losing bet to "low-order but symplectic".

The shadow Hamiltonian

Now the punchline. Verlet does not conserve H exactly (it can't, because it is only a second-order approximation to the true flow). What it conserves is a slightly-shifted Hamiltonian Ĥ, the shadow Hamiltonian, whose series expansion in the time step is

This is not an approximation claim. It is a theorem due to Benettin, Giorgilli, Hairer and others: a symplectic integrator with step h exactly conserves some smooth function Ĥ (strictly: conserves it up to exponentially small terms that depend on the analyticity radius of H). Since Ĥ differs from H by O(h2), and since Verlet conserves Ĥ, the apparent energy error H(qn, pn) − H(q0, p0) oscillates inside a band of width O(h2) for all time, not just for the first few steps.

This is the bounded-vs-unbounded picture. Under Euler, H drifts because the integrator does not preserve any nearby Hamiltonian at all; it is simulating a dissipative system, not a Hamiltonian one. Under RK4, there is no exactly-conserved shadow Hamiltonian because RK4 is not symplectic; the energy error is O(h4) per step but it still drifts over long runs. Under Verlet, the shadow Ĥ pins the trajectory to one of its level sets, and those level sets are everywhere close to the level sets of H.

Discrete Noether, in one slogan. Continuous time-translation symmetry of a continuous Hamiltonian implies conservation of H. Discrete time-translation symmetry of a symplectic discrete flow implies conservation of a shadow Ĥ that is close to H. The symmetry is preserved under discretisation if and only if the discretisation is symplectic, and the conserved quantity is then a slight deformation of the continuous one.

Fail to use a symplectic scheme and you have broken the discrete time-translation symmetry: your update map is different from the map you would get by stepping in two half-steps, then re-stepping, then composing. Noether gives you nothing in return.

Why not just use RK4 with a smaller step?

A natural reaction is: if RK4 has O(h4) per-step error and Verlet has O(h2), surely we could take h so small in RK4 that the accumulated drift over the run we care about is negligible.

The trouble is that "the run we care about" in modern molecular dynamics, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, and gravitational N-body simulation is very long. Think 109 to 1012 steps. The RK4 drift per step is O(h5), but it accumulates without cancellation, so the total drift after N steps is O(N h5). For that quantity to be small when N = 1010 you need h smaller than any practical value. Verlet's drift is zero no matter how many steps you take; its apparent error is a bounded oscillation of amplitude O(h2), regardless of N.

Put differently: RK4 gives you better accuracy per step and worse conservation over the run. Which one matters depends entirely on how long you run. Above a few thousand steps Verlet already wins on almost any Hamiltonian problem; above a million, the question is not close.

There are higher-order symplectic integrators too: Forest–Ruth (fourth-order), Yoshida's explicit families of arbitrary even order, McLachlan's optimised fourth-order schemes. They are all compositions of Verlet-like building blocks with carefully chosen coefficients. All of them conserve some Ĥ; all of them have bounded energy error; all of them inherit the shadow-Hamiltonian theorem. Second-order Verlet is the simplest representative and the one that runs in production.

The rule is the same every time: figure out what group the continuous problem is invariant under, then demand that the discretisation respect that group exactly, not approximately. Where the group is time-translation and the invariant is the symplectic 2-form, the payoff is what this explainer built. Where the group is a finite point group and the invariant is isotropy, the payoff is Navier–Stokes on a hexagonal lattice. Where the group is SO(3) and the invariant is rotational equivariance, the payoff is a neural network that generalises from one orientation of the training data to all others.

The technical content differs. The algebraic shape is identical.

Takeaways

  • Hamilton's flow preserves a 2-form. ω = dq ∧ dp. Phase-space volume is conserved (Liouville); level sets of H are invariant.
  • Forward Euler inflates volume. Jacobian determinant 1 + h2 cos q > 1 near equilibrium. Orbits spiral outward; energy drifts without bound.
  • RK4 is accurate per step but not symplectic. Energy drift per step is tiny but accumulates. Over long runs, a first-order symplectic scheme beats it.
  • Verlet is three lines and exactly symplectic. Half-kick, drift, half-kick. Each sub-step is a shear; the product is symplectic; the Jacobian determinant is exactly 1.
  • Shadow Hamiltonian theorem. A symplectic integrator with step h exactly conserves some Ĥ = H + h2H2 + …. The apparent energy error H(t) − H(0) is bounded for all time, not just for short time.
  • Discrete Noether. Time-translation symmetry of the discrete flow, encoded as symplecticity, yields exact conservation of Ĥ, the discrete cousin of Noether's theorem that an un-symmetric discretisation throws away.
  • Simplicity is the point. The scheme that wins is not the most accurate per step; it is the one that respects the geometry. The rest of the series is variations on that theme for different groups.
