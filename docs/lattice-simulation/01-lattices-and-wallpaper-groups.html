Lattices and Wallpaper Groups

Part 1 of 6. Five kinds of lattice, seventeen kinds of periodic pattern, and the few rotations and reflections a simulation on a grid gets to keep.

A cellular automaton puts a cell on every point of ℤ²; a lattice gauge theory puts link variables on the edges of ℤ⁴. Both replace the continuum by a lattice, the integer combinations of a basis:

The continuum looks the same after any rotation. A lattice looks the same after only a few, and a simulation on it inherits those few.

Five Bravais lattices

Every lattice has the half turn x ↦ −x, one mirror brings a perpendicular one, and a four- or six-fold lattice is square or hexagonal, both of which have mirrors. So the full point group of a lattice is C2, D2, D4 or D6. D2 fits two ways: the shortest vectors along its mirrors generate the lattice (rectangular) or only half of it (centred rectangular, with a rhombic cell). That makes five types.

Decorating the lattice

A motif at every site can add symmetries the bare lattice lacks: rotation centres between sites, mirrors, and glide reflections, a reflection followed by a half-period slide along the line.

The symmetry group G of a periodic pattern is a wallpaper group. Dividing out its translations T leaves a finite point group P of linear parts, in which a glide counts as a plain reflection:

Where do seventeen groups come from?

Pairing each point group with each way it can sit on a lattice gives thirteen symmorphic groups, each with a point fixed by a copy of the whole point group. Carrying reflections out as glides adds pg, pmg, pgg and p4g; on centred lattices it adds nothing, since cm and cmm already have glides. Fedorov listed the seventeen in 1891.

Reading the seventeen patterns

The names are Hermann–Mauguin symbols: p or c for the cell, the highest rotation order, then m, g or 1 for what lies along and across the main directions. The easy pair to swap is p3m1 and p31m. In p3m1 the mirrors are perpendicular to the shortest lattice vectors and pass through every three-fold centre; in p31m they are parallel, and some centres sit between mirrors.

Figure 4. The orbit of one F; mirrored copies are lighter. Solid blue: mirrors. Dashed orange: glides that are not mirrors. Lens, triangle, square, hexagon: 2-, 3-, 4-, 6-fold centres. pm and pg, pmm and pgg, p4m and p4g share a point group; only mirrors become glides.

Can you name a group?

Look for the highest rotation, then mirrors, then glides, which are the hard part: a row matches the reflected next row only after a half-step slide.

What does a simulation inherit?

A rule that treats every neighbour alike commutes with its lattice’s point group, so a symmetric start stays symmetric (Part 3 watches this in Life). A stencil or velocity set reaches the continuum through its moments, and the rank-k moment is isotropic when its directional form is the same at every angle:

Mk has angular harmonics up to k, and a turn by 360°/n kills every harmonic that is not a multiple of n. So a square set is isotropic at rank 2 but not rank 4, and a hexagonal set passes rank 4 and fails at rank 6. Rank 4 is the one a fluid’s viscosity needs (Part 4). Square grids stay common because they are easy to index and extend to three dimensions, where the same count gives 14 Bravais lattices, 32 point groups and 230 space groups.
