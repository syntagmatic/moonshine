Lattices and Wallpaper Groups

Part 1 of 6. The plane has five kinds of lattice and seventeen kinds of periodic pattern. The finite group of symmetries at a lattice site decides what a simulation built on that lattice can get right.

A cellular automaton puts a cell on every point of the integer grid ℤ². A molecular dynamics code wraps its particles into a box and repeats the box. A lattice gauge theory puts its link variables on the edges of ℤ⁴. Each of these has replaced the continuum by a lattice: a discrete subgroup of ℝn that spans the space, which is the same thing as the set of integer combinations of a basis:

The basis vectors are the primitive vectors, and they are far from unique: any integer change of basis with determinant ±1 generates the same set of points. The parallelogram they span is a primitive cell. Its area, |a1 × a2|, does not depend on which basis you picked. Two lengths and an angle describe a basis, and the basis is a choice. What belongs to the lattice itself is its symmetry.

The point group of a lattice

The point group of a lattice is the set of rotations and reflections about a site that map the lattice onto itself. Written in the lattice basis, such a map sends integer coordinates to integer coordinates, so its matrix has integer entries and, in particular, an integer trace. A rotation by θ has trace 2 cos θ whatever basis it is written in. That gives the crystallographic restriction:

so the only rotation orders a lattice can carry are 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6. There is no five-fold lattice, which is why a quasicrystal cannot be periodic.

A lattice imposes more than the restriction. The half turn x ↦ −x maps every lattice to itself, so every lattice has at least C2. A mirror composed with the half turn gives the perpendicular mirror, so one mirror line brings a second. A lattice with a four-fold rotation has two perpendicular shortest vectors of equal length, which makes it square, and a square has mirrors. A lattice with a three- or six-fold rotation is hexagonal, and a hexagonal lattice has mirrors too. Only four groups survive as the full point group of a lattice: C2, D2, D4 and D6, of orders 2, 4, 8 and 12.

There are five Bravais types, not four, because D2 can sit on a lattice in two different ways. Take the shortest lattice vector along each of the two mirror lines. If those two vectors generate the whole lattice, the lattice is rectangular. If they only generate half of it, there is an extra lattice point at the centre of every rectangle they span, and the lattice is centred rectangular: equivalently, it has a primitive cell that is a rhombus. No rotation or reflection turns one kind into the other, so they count separately. Oblique, rectangular, centred rectangular, square and hexagonal are the five Bravais lattices of the plane.

Decorating the lattice

A lattice is not yet a pattern. Put a motif at every lattice point and the pattern can acquire new symmetries: rotations about points that are not lattice sites, mirror lines, and a fourth kind of rigid motion. Every isometry of the plane is a translation, a rotation, a reflection, or a glide reflection: reflect across a line, then translate along it.

Two glides in a row give a translation by v, which has to be a lattice vector, so the glide's shift along its axis is half a lattice period. Composing a glide with lattice translations along the same axis changes its shift by whole periods, and a half period never reaches zero, so no choice of translation turns a genuine glide into a mirror.

The symmetry group G of a periodic pattern is a wallpaper group: a discrete group of isometries of the plane whose translations form a lattice T. The translations are a normal subgroup, and what is left after dividing them out is a finite group P, the point group of the pattern:

An element of P records only the linear part of a symmetry: a glide contributes a reflection to P, and a rotation about some point off the origin contributes the same rotation as one about the origin. P maps T to itself, so the crystallographic restriction applies, and P is one of the ten crystallographic point groups of the plane:

The pattern's point group is a subgroup of its lattice's point group, and it can be much smaller. A pattern of F's on a square lattice has only translations if the F's all face the same way.

Thirteen plus four

Counting the groups comes in two steps. First, pair each point group with each way it can act on a lattice. C1 and C2 act on any lattice. D1 and D2 need a rectangular lattice, and can sit on it primitive or centred. C4 and D4 need a square one. C3, D3, C6 and D6 need a hexagonal one, and D3 fits in two inequivalent orientations, with its mirrors either perpendicular or parallel to the shortest lattice vectors. That gives thirteen pairings, and each gives one symmorphic group, in which some point of the plane is fixed by a copy of the whole point group: p1, p2, pm, cm, pmm, cmm, p4, p4m, p3, p3m1, p31m, p6 and p6m.

Second, a reflection in the point group can be carried out in the pattern by a glide instead of a mirror. On a primitive rectangular or square lattice this gives four new groups: pg (from pm), pmg and pgg (from pmm), and p4g (from p4m). In each of these, no point is fixed by the whole point group. On a centred lattice the swap produces nothing new, since cm and cmm already contain glides halfway between their mirrors. Thirteen symmorphic groups and four non-symmorphic ones make seventeen. Fedorov enumerated them in 1891 and Pólya drew them again in 1924. Every periodic pattern in the plane has one of them as its symmetry group.

Reading a name

The short names are Hermann–Mauguin symbols. The leading letter is the cell: p for primitive and c for the centred rectangular cell, with its extra point in the middle. The digit is the highest rotation order. The letters after it describe the symmetry along, and then across, the main lattice directions: m for a mirror, g for a glide, 1 for nothing. So p4m has four-fold rotations and mirrors on both the axes and the diagonals, while p4g has glides perpendicular to the axes and mirrors only on the diagonals, which miss the four-fold centres. The awkward pair is p3m1 and p31m. In p3m1 the mirrors are perpendicular to the shortest lattice vectors and pass through every three-fold centre; in p31m they run parallel to them, and some three-fold centres sit between mirrors. Name the group

To name a group from a picture, find the highest rotation order, then the mirror lines, then any glide axes that are not mirrors. Glides are the hard part, because nothing marks them: you have to see that a row of motifs matches the reflected next row after a half-step slide. What a simulation inherits

A lattice update rule that treats every neighbour alike inherits the symmetry of its lattice. Conway's Life counts live cells among the eight Moore neighbours without caring which is which, so on the square grid it commutes with every element of p4m. The FHP lattice gas on a triangular grid commutes with p6m. The first consequence is equivariance: if the rule commutes with a symmetry and the initial state has it, every later state has it too. Symmetry can be gained along the way but never lost, and Part 3 watches this happen in Life.

The second consequence shows up in the continuum limit. When a lattice gas or a lattice Boltzmann scheme is coarse-grained into a fluid, its momentum flux involves the fourth moment of the velocity set, Σi wi ciα ciβ ciγ ciδ. For the viscous term to come out the same in every direction, that tensor has to be isotropic, which in two dimensions reduces to a single condition:

Whether a velocity set meets that condition depends on its point group. Any fourth-rank symmetric tensor invariant under D6 is isotropic already, so a hexagonal velocity set passes for free. D4 allows one more invariant, the "cubic" tensor with components only when all four indices are equal, so a square velocity set passes only if its weights happen to cancel that term. The HPP gas has four unit velocities and no weights to tune: Σ cx4 = 2 while 3 Σ cx2cy2 = 0, and its fluid is anisotropic. FHP's six velocities give 9/4 on both sides. D2Q9 stays on the square lattice and adds the diagonal shell with weight 1/36 against 1/9 on the axes, which gives 1/3 on both sides. The square lattice can carry isotropic hydrodynamics if it gets a second speed and the right weights. The hexagonal lattice needs neither. Part 4 works through the tensor for HPP and FHP, and Part 5 builds the D2Q9 scheme.

From 17 to 230

The same anatomy in three dimensions gives 14 Bravais lattices, 32 crystallographic point groups and 230 space groups, of which 73 are symmorphic. Screw axes, a rotation followed by a fractional translation along the axis, join the glides as the non-symmorphic ingredients. Every crystal structure has one of the 230 as its symmetry group, and a simulation on a cubic grid inherits the symmetry of the cubic lattice in the same way a two-dimensional rule inherits p4m.
