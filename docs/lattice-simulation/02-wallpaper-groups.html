Wallpaper Groups

Part 2 of 15. The five Bravais lattices are only half the story. A wallpaper group layers a finite point group on top of the translations, and occasionally a glide reflection, giving the full list of discrete symmetries of the Euclidean plane. The list ends at seventeen, and every periodic pattern you have ever seen is one of them.

The last explainer classified the translation subgroup — the two-vector lattice that a periodic pattern repeats along. That classification has five answers in the plane. But a lattice is not yet a pattern: nothing has been drawn on it. Once we place a motif on the lattice, new symmetries enter the picture — rotations around a lattice site, mirror axes through it, glides that translate-and-reflect. The full symmetry group of the decorated pattern is, in general, larger than the translation group alone.

In two dimensions the list of possible full-symmetry-groups is surprisingly short. Fedorov enumerated them in 1891 and Pólya rediscovered the count in 1924. There are exactly seventeen, known to crystallographers as the wallpaper groups and to mathematicians as the two-dimensional crystallographic groups. Every repeating pattern in the plane — every tiled floor, every brocade, every Escher print, every CA rule on a regular grid — has a wallpaper group, and that group is one of these seventeen.

From lattice to group

A wallpaper group G is a discrete subgroup of the isometry group of ℝ² that contains translations in two linearly independent directions. The translations form a normal subgroup T isomorphic to ℤ² — the Bravais lattice from the previous explainer. The quotient G/T is a finite group P, called the point group of the wallpaper group. Algebraically:

Read this right-to-left: pick a Bravais lattice T, pick a point group P compatible with it (from the list allowed by the crystallographic restriction), and specify how elements of P lift to rigid motions of the plane — with or without a half-translation attached. Each choice produces a wallpaper group. Most combinations give the same group up to conjugation; what is left after the dust settles is the list of seventeen.

An element of G is a rigid motion of the plane. Every rigid motion is either a rotation, a reflection, a translation, or a glide reflection: reflect across a line, then translate parallel to that line. A glide whose translation distance equals exactly half a lattice period has order-two squared behaviour — glide twice and you get a lattice translation — which is why glides cannot be reduced to pure reflections. They are the reason that pm (pure mirror) and pg (pure glide) are different groups, and that the count is seventeen rather than fewer.

The orbit of a motif under G is the set of all copies produced by applying every element of G to it. A pattern is nothing more than the orbit of a single asymmetric shape under a wallpaper group: pick the motif, pick the group, and the pattern is determined. Different groups acting on the same motif produce entirely different tilings — a symmetric star versus a spinning pinwheel versus a sliding chevron. The figure below lets you feel this directly.

The crystallographic restriction, one more time

Every rotation in the point group P must send the lattice to itself, and by an integer-trace argument the only allowed rotation orders in 2D are 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6. No 5-fold, no 7-fold, no 8-fold. The allowed point groups are thus the eleven subgroups

of which not every one actually arises as a wallpaper-group point group on some Bravais lattice. After matching each candidate point group against each Bravais lattice, and then accounting for glide reflections (which can double the group: pm → pg, pmm → pmg → pgg, p4m → p4g), the count lands at seventeen.

How to read a group name

Crystallographic notation for wallpaper groups (called "IUC" or "Hermann–Mauguin" notation) is a compact string like p4g or cmm that encodes four pieces of information. The leading letter is the centring: p for primitive (lattice = unit cell) and c for centred (there is an extra lattice point at the centre of the conventional cell, as with the centred rectangular Bravais type). The first digit after it is the maximum rotation order — 1, 2, 3, 4, or 6. Subsequent letters describe mirrors (m) and glides (g) along the principal directions.

So p4m reads "primitive lattice, 4-fold rotation, mirrors along the axes"; p4g reads "primitive lattice, 4-fold rotation, glides along the diagonals". Both have the same point group D4; they differ only in whether the non-rotation generators are pure mirrors or glides. p1 reads "primitive lattice, 1-fold rotation (i.e. identity), no mirrors or glides" — the most degenerate group, translations only.

The five-to-seventeen inflation. Each Bravais type carries its own roster of wallpaper groups: the oblique lattice supports only p1 and p2; rectangular supports pm, pg, pmm, pmg, pgg; centred rectangular supports cm and cmm; square supports p4, p4m, p4g; hexagonal supports p3, p3m1, p31m, p6, p6m. The crystallographic restriction forces 3-fold and 6-fold rotations to sit only on the hexagonal lattice, and 4-fold only on the square. So the seventeen-fold classification is really a count of compatible pairings between translation subgroups and point groups, modulated by glide choices.

What simulations inherit

A lattice-based simulation whose update rule is local and isotropic automatically has some wallpaper group as a symmetry. Conway's Life on the square lattice with a Moore neighbourhood has p4m: the rule looks the same under 90° rotation and under reflection through any grid axis or diagonal. The FHP lattice gas has p6m: six velocities, hexagonal symmetry, reflection through any axis. Once we know the wallpaper group of the rule, we immediately know a symmetry of every solution — the evolution of an initial condition is fibred by orbits of the group, and a symmetric initial condition cannot spontaneously break its own symmetry without external noise.

The harder lesson is the reverse. A rule designed for a specific wallpaper group can fail to reproduce the right continuum physics if that group's rank-4 isotropic-tensor space is too small. Recovering isotropic Navier–Stokes from a lattice Boltzmann method needs the point group of the lattice to admit a one-parameter rank-4 isotropic tensor; on a square lattice this fails, and on a hexagonal lattice it succeeds, because D4 ≠ D6 at the tensor level even though both look "symmetric enough" by eye. This is the subject of Explainers 7 and 8.

From 17 to 230

In three dimensions the analogous count is 230: the number of crystallographic space groups. The jump from 17 to 230 is huge but structural — same anatomy, more dimensions to arrange it in. A 3D space group is a discrete subgroup of the Euclidean group Iso(ℝ³) containing a rank-3 translation subgroup; the quotient is a 3D point group (32 possibilities, the crystallographic point groups); combinations with glide planes and screw axes give the 230. Every atomic crystal ever observed lives in one of these, and every lattice-based 3D simulation — molecular dynamics, lattice QCD, voxel CFD — is implicitly sitting inside one.

Takeaways

  • A wallpaper group = lattice + point group + glide. Formally, a discrete subgroup of Iso(ℝ²) with a rank-2 translation subgroup. The short exact sequence 1 → T → G → P → 1 says: pick a lattice, pick a compatible point group, decide which reflections become glides.
  • There are exactly seventeen. Fedorov 1891, Pólya 1924. The bound comes from the crystallographic restriction (rotation orders 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 only) and the finite list of compatible lattice–point-group pairings.
  • Glides are the subtle bit. A glide reflection (reflect, then translate parallel by half a lattice period) squares to a translation, so it is a genuinely new generator. Without glides the count would be eleven, not seventeen.
  • Each Bravais lattice hosts a specific sub-list. Oblique hosts 2; rectangular 5; centred rectangular 2; square 3; hexagonal 5. Total 17.
  • Simulations inherit their wallpaper group. An isotropic local rule on a 2D lattice has some wallpaper group as its symmetry, and the group determines which continuous-limit tensors are reachable. The choice of group is not cosmetic; it sets what physics the discretised rule can reproduce.
