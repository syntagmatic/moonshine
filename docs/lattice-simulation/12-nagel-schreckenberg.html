Nagel-Schreckenberg Traffic

Part 12 of 15. Four rules, one integer per car, a one-dimensional lattice — and a phase transition from smooth flow to self-organising jams that propagate backwards at a fixed speed. The 1992 model that showed a desktop computer could produce traffic dynamics no continuum equation could.

Every simulation so far in this series has leaned on a two- or three-dimensional lattice to do its work. For our last stop before Act V switches to the lattice-as-tool, we drop down to the minimum possible dimension. A single line of cells, each either empty or holding one car. An integer velocity per car. Four update rules, executed in parallel across the whole lattice. That is the entirety of the Nagel-Schreckenberg model, and it is enough to produce a non-equilibrium phase transition with exactly the phenomenology real highway traffic exhibits.

The payoff is a space-time diagram. Plot car positions on the x-axis, with time running downwards, and the dark bands that appear when the density crosses a critical value all tilt the same way, backwards relative to the direction of motion. A jam, on a highway, is not a wave of stuck cars translating with the traffic. It is a density perturbation travelling the wrong way down the road at a fixed speed — roughly one cell per time step in the classical model. You can watch it happen on your screen.

The lattice and the state

The road is a one-dimensional lattice of L cells, indexed x = 0, 1, …, L - 1, with periodic boundaries so we can run indefinitely without worrying about inflow and outflow. Each cell holds at most one car. If a car sits at cell x, it carries an integer velocity v(x) chosen from

The state of the simulation is therefore a function s: ℤL → {∅, 0, 1, …, vmax} — for each cell, either empty or the velocity of the car currently there. The density ρ = N/L is the fraction of cells occupied.

The four rules

Every time step, every car looks at the gap d to the next car ahead (number of empty cells between them, so d = 0 if the next car is right in front) and updates its velocity and position according to:

  1. Accelerate.If v < vmax, set v ← v + 1. Drivers prefer to go faster.
  2. Slow down to avoid collision.If v > d, set v ← d. Never move into an occupied cell.
  3. Randomise.With probability p, and only if v > 0, set v ← v - 1. Stochastic noise: the inattentive brake-tap.
  4. Advance.Move the car v cells forward: x ← x + v (mod L).

Algorithm 1. The canonical Nagel-Schreckenberg update. All four rules are applied to every car simultaneously, using the pre-update state to compute gaps. A parallel update is important: the model becomes a deterministic cellular automaton at p = 0, and the randomisation in rule 3 is the only source of stochasticity.

Remove rule 3 and you get a deterministic cellular automaton with two conservation laws (cars and their identity). Leave rule 3 in and the dynamics becomes stochastic, the free-flow phase acquires a shortened mean velocity, and the jammed phase acquires non-trivial life-times. The random term is not cosmetic — it is what turns the model from a toy into a model of real traffic, because real drivers are not perfect.

The flow, the density, the fundamental diagram

Average over all cars and all time steps once the simulation has settled. The flow is the number of cars passing any fixed cell per time step:

Plot flow against density and you get the fundamental diagram of traffic. Empirically, on any real highway, it is a triangular curve: flow rises linearly from zero at ρ = 0, peaks at some critical density ρc, and falls linearly back to zero at ρ = 1 (bumper-to-bumper). Below the peak, every additional car adds throughput. Above it, every additional car subtracts.

At p = 0 the Nagel-Schreckenberg model reproduces this shape exactly. In the free-flow regime every car reaches vmax and the flow is J = vmax · ρ. The peak is at

Above that density the cars are closer together than vmax cells and the available gap becomes the rate-limiting factor; the flow falls as J = 1 - ρ. For p > 0 both branches bow downward, the peak shifts left, and a hysteresis region opens up — the same density supports both a free-flowing stationary state and a jammed one depending on history. That bistability is one of the known signatures of a first-order non-equilibrium phase transition.

Reading the space-time diagram

The space-time diagram is the observation worth remembering. Each horizontal slice is the state of the road at one instant. Stack slices over time and trajectories of individual cars become lines; a car moving at velocity v traces a line with slope dx/dt = v. At low density every trajectory is a steep, clean diagonal — cars all at their top velocity, widely spaced.

Above ρc, something qualitatively different happens. Groups of cars collect behind a stopped car; those behind them also have to stop; eventually the stopped car at the front gets a lucky acceleration and pulls away, but the tail of the jam is now one car further back than it was a tick ago. The rear of the jam has propagated one cell backwards in one time step. Do this thousands of times and you see a dark band with slope -1 in the space-time diagram, a macroscopic structure made of microscopic transitions.

The quantity measured from that slope is the jam-wave speed:

It is a remarkable number. It is independent of vmax. It is nearly independent of p. It comes purely from the cellular-automaton kinematics: one car at the rear of a jam needs exactly one time step to register that the car ahead has moved, shift its own state from occupied-with-v = 0 to empty, and let the jam boundary recede by one lattice spacing. Empirically on real highways it is also constant, measured by induction loops at roughly 15-20 km/h backwards regardless of weather, drivers, speed limit, or lane count.

The jam-wave is a non-equilibrium collective mode. No single car is moving backwards. Every car on the road is either stopped or moving forwards. Yet the macroscopic density perturbation moves backwards at a fixed speed, set by the lattice spacing divided by the update period. This is exactly what happens in real traffic, and the fact that a desktop simulation with four one-line rules reproduces it is the reason Nagel & Schreckenberg is the standard introduction to cellular automata in sociophysics.

Mean velocity in the free-flow phase

For ρ low enough that cars rarely encounter each other, rule 2 almost never fires. Rule 1 pushes every car towards vmax. Rule 3 knocks v down by one with probability p each step. The stationary mean velocity is therefore the fixed point of ⟨v⟩ ← ⟨v⟩ + 1 (capped at vmax) minus p every step, giving

at least in the idealised limit ρ → 0. The free-flow branch of the fundamental diagram is then J = ρ · ⟨v⟩free, linear with slope vmax - p/(1 - p). Set p = 0.2 and that slope is exactly 4.75 for vmax = 5 — a 5% penalty from the noise. You can watch it emerge in the plot above by sliding ρ low and reading ⟨v⟩ off the info box.

The phase transition in one dimension

A stock argument in equilibrium statistical physics says that one-dimensional systems with short-range interactions cannot undergo phase transitions at non-zero temperature. The argument fails for Nagel-Schreckenberg because this is not an equilibrium system. There is no Hamiltonian, no detailed balance, no thermodynamic limit in the Boltzmann sense. What we have is a stochastic Markov chain on a finite state space with an invariant measure that changes its qualitative character as ρ crosses ρc.

At p = 0 the transition is sharp and exactly solvable: below ρc = 1/(vmax + 1), every car can maintain vmax; above it, the steady state is a rigid crystal of cars separated by vmax empty cells with occasional defect lines. At p > 0 the transition broadens into a crossover with a measurable critical density that shifts with p, and numerical evidence suggests it belongs to a non-standard universality class distinct from the ASEP exclusion-process family. The fact that a non-equilibrium 1D lattice model has universal critical content at all is the deeper interest: the discretisation creates phenomena that no mean-field continuous ODE of traffic flow (LWR kinematic-wave theory, Aw-Rascle, etc.) can produce without hand-inserting a jam front.

Takeaways

  • Four rules on a 1D lattice. Accelerate, brake-for-safety, randomise, advance. Parallel update. That is the whole Nagel-Schreckenberg model; everything else is emergent.
  • Phase transition at ρc. For p = 0 the critical density is exactly 1/(vmax + 1). For p > 0 the peak shifts left and the curve bows downward, the signature of noise-enhanced jamming.
  • Jam waves travel backwards. The space-time diagram shows dark bands with slope -1 lattice sites per time step, independent of vmax and nearly independent of p. Real highways show the same constant; it is a robust feature of the discrete-lattice kinematics.
  • Free-flow mean velocity. At low density, ⟨v⟩ ≈ vmax - p/(1 - p). The noise directly costs flow even when the road is empty.
  • 1D is enough for a non-equilibrium transition. Equilibrium 1D short-range systems cannot order; this system is not in equilibrium, the argument does not apply, and the lattice discretisation captures structure that continuum kinematic-wave traffic equations cannot produce without an externally inserted shock.
