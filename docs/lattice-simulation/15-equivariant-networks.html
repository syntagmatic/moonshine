Equivariant Networks

Part 15 of 15, series finale. A standard convolutional layer is translation-equivariant by construction: shift the input and the feature map shifts with it. Cohen and Welling showed in 2016 how to generalise the construction to any finite group — rotations, reflections, permutations, gauge transformations — by replacing convolution over Z2 with convolution over the group. Symmetry stops being something the network has to learn and starts being part of the computational substrate.

A deep learning engineer and a lattice physicist, it turns out, have been worrying about the same problem for decades. The physicist wants a discretisation of Navier–Stokes that preserves rotational isotropy; the engineer wants a classifier that recognises a cat at every orientation. Both are asking: how do I bake a symmetry group into a finite, discrete computation so that it is exactly honoured rather than approximately learned? The answers converge. A lattice gauge theorist keeps gauge invariance by putting link variables on the edges of the lattice and multiplying them in gauge-covariant ways. An equivariant-network designer keeps rotation invariance by putting feature maps on the group itself and convolving over it. Different jargon, same move.

Translation-equivariant convolution is the case everyone already knows. A standard CNN layer convolves an input x with a learned filter ψ by sliding ψ across every position of a 2D grid and taking an inner product. If you shift the input by a vector v, the output shifts by v as well:

This is why a cat detector trained on cats in the centre of the image still fires on cats in the corner. The detector was never "at the centre" in the first place — it was at every position at once, by construction of the convolution. Translation equivariance is the single property that makes a CNN data-efficient compared to a fully connected network. Translate the training set tenfold by random crops, and a fully connected network has to learn ten times the weights; a CNN has already accounted for it.

What equivariance means, formally

Let G be a group acting on a space X (say pixels) and on another space Y (say feature maps). A map φ from X to Y is called G-equivariant if

for every group element g and every input x. In words: acting before and acting after give the same answer. The map commutes with the group action. Equivariance is strictly stronger than invariance: invariance demands φ(g · x) = φ(x), which throws away the orientation entirely. An equivariant feature map still knows where the orientation is — it just rotates itself to match.

For a vanilla CNN, the group G is the translation group Z2, acting on input images by shifts and on feature maps by the same shifts. The convolution operation is equivariant by construction. Cohen and Welling (2016) asked the obvious question: what if G is bigger? What if it includes rotations by 90°, so that rotating the input produces a rotation of the feature map as well? This is no longer free. Ordinary convolution is not rotation-equivariant — as you will see below, a rotated input produces a distorted feature map, because the filter was learned at one orientation only.

Here f is a function on the group (the feature map) and ψ is a filter that is also a function on the group. The value of the output at group element g is obtained by matching ψ against f along the coset h−1g, summed over all h in G. When G is the translation group Z2, the group operation is vector addition, h−1g becomes g − h, and the formula collapses to ordinary convolution. When G is a roto-translation group Z2 ⋊ C4, the group operation includes a 90° rotation; the sum now runs over every position and every rotation, and the output feature map lives on the whole group rather than on just the plane.

The mechanical consequence: a layer that was a stack of C feature maps becomes a stack of C · |G| feature maps, one for each group element. For G = C4 this quadruples the storage at a given channel count. The compensation is that the effective receptive field is quadrupled for free — rotation invariance is being built into the weights rather than learned from the data.

An equivariance-breaking ablation. Take a standard CNN trained to classify rotated MNIST digits; randomly rotate test images by 90°; accuracy collapses by tens of points. Retrain with random rotation augmentation; accuracy recovers, but at the cost of a larger effective dataset and a fatter model. Replace the convolutions with group convolutions over C4; accuracy recovers at the original model size with no augmentation, because equivariance is now a structural guarantee rather than a learned approximation. Steerable CNNs and the continuous case

Discrete groups give discrete feature maps. What if we want continuous rotation equivariance — every angle, not just multiples of 90°? Cohen, Geiger, Köhler and Welling (2018) generalised the construction to SO(2) and higher continuous groups. The trick is representation theory. A continuous filter on SO(2) can be expanded in the basis of irreducible representations — for SO(2) this is simply the Fourier basis, sines and cosines of integer multiples of the rotation angle. A filter represented in this basis can be rotated by any angle in closed form, without resampling, by multiplying each Fourier coefficient by the appropriate complex exponential.

The punchline: the filter is "steerable." You learn the Fourier coefficients once, and the filter at every rotation is free. The cost in storage is the number of irreps you keep; the benefit is that equivariance is exact at every angle, not just at a finite subgroup. The same construction extends to SO(3) with spherical harmonics in place of Fourier modes, and this is exactly the technology behind the SE(3)-equivariant networks used for molecular property prediction.

Data efficiency

Equivariance is not just an aesthetic preference; it is a data-efficiency multiplier. A standard CNN has to see every relevant orientation of every pattern in the training data, and its weights have to learn, redundantly, the same feature detector at each orientation. A C4-equivariant CNN has a single detector plus three rotated copies generated by the group action; each training example provides four effective supervision signals instead of one. Empirically, the break-even dataset size is 4–10× smaller for the same accuracy on rotation-invariant tasks. Where equivariance changes the game

The applications stretch far outside digit classification. In medical imaging, histological slides have no preferred orientation — a tumour cell looks like a tumour cell at every angle — and rotation-equivariant networks achieve clinical-grade accuracy with a fraction of the usually enormous labelled datasets, because the network does not have to rediscover the same morphology at every orientation. In molecular property prediction, the atoms of a molecule have no canonical labelling and the molecule itself has no canonical orientation in 3D; SE(3)-equivariant networks of the kind introduced by Thomas, Smidt and colleagues (2018) and matured into modern architectures like Tensor Field Networks, SE(3)-Transformers, and Allegro are now the default for force-field learning, where they win by a wide margin on data efficiency and on extrapolation to larger systems. In weather and climate modelling, fields live on the sphere; spherical CNNs with rotation equivariance under SO(3) have replaced planar convolutions in several operational systems, since a weather pattern at the pole should be recognised by the same operator that recognises it at the equator.

For our purposes — the physics of simulation — the most telling application is learning dynamics on a lattice gauge theory. The configuration space is a collection of link variables in a compact Lie group, and the physics is invariant under the gauge group acting at every site. Hard-coding that symmetry into a network, via the equivariant-convolution construction over the gauge group, gives guaranteed charge conservation in the learned dynamics. The alternative — hoping the network learns gauge invariance from finite training data — breaks down exactly at the long trajectories where we most need reliability. Bake the symmetry in and the conservation law follows for free: this is Noether's theorem transposed from the continuum action of a classical field theory to the discrete parameters of a learned model.

The finale: one idea in many costumes

Symmetry and discretisation, from lattice to learner

Every explainer in this series was a different costume for the same pair of ideas. In Act I we started as crystallographers: a Bravais lattice is a free abelian group of rank n, its point group only takes five values in 2D and fourteen in 3D, and gluing opposite faces turns a finite box into a torus — a lattice in a space plus a quotient by the lattice is already a full geometry. In Act II we graduated to dynamics: a symplectic integrator conserves phase-space volume because its update map is a group element of Sp(2n); a symmetry-reduced MD code exploits a point group to throw away redundant atoms, because the asymmetric unit times the group rebuilds the whole molecule exactly. Noether was already in the room.

In Act III the lattice pushed back. Conway's Life inherits the D4 of the square grid and cannot leave a symmetric attractor without outside help. HPP on the square lattice fails because its point group's rank-4 invariant tensor has one parameter too few to support isotropic viscosity; FHP on the hexagonal lattice succeeds because D6 is just big enough. Lattice Boltzmann generalises: pick a velocity set whose point-group representation supports the tensors Navier–Stokes needs, and the simulation is self-consistent to the order you kept. Ising closes the act as the canonical lattice model of broken symmetry — a global Z2 that the Hamiltonian respects and the ground state does not.

In Act IV the lattice became a graph and the point group became an automorphism group. Twin vertices resist every leader-based controller for the same algebraic reason that a centred rectangular lattice refuses to distinguish its two sublattice positions. Spatial Lotka–Volterra sees a directed-percolation phase transition invisible to the mean-field ODE, because the transition lives in a statistical-mechanical sense that only the lattice supports. Nagel–Schreckenberg traffic has its own non-equilibrium phase transition on a one-dimensional lattice, with jams as the broken-symmetry phase.

In Act V the lattice became engineering: a spatial hash is a lattice chosen for query speed, an octree is an adaptive lattice chosen for query speed at variable density, a gyroid is a group orbit chosen for printable mechanical strength. And in this final explainer the lattice became a parameter structure — the weight tensor of a neural network is indexed not just by position but by a symmetry group, and a rotation of the input produces an exact permutation of the feature maps rather than a noisy approximation. Noether at training time.

The thread is that there is nothing in simulation that is not, at bottom, a choice of lattice and a choice of group. The lattice chooses what "a point in space" is allowed to mean. The group chooses which differences between points are physically meaningful and which are artefacts of the labelling. Every bug in every simulation ultimately falls into one of two categories: the lattice was too coarse (resolution artefacts, non-convergence), or the group was wrong (isotropy failure, energy drift, charge leakage, dataset-size blowup). The craft is picking the two together so they agree on what the world looks like.

A finite computer cannot see ℝn. What it can do — what the whole series has been about — is pick a subgroup small enough to fit in memory and a symmetry group large enough to carry the physics that matters. When those two choices are made well the simulation, in a very precise sense, is not an approximation of the physics: it is the physics, carried out on a substrate the physics is also willing to live on. That is the whole game.

Takeaways

  • Equivariance commutes with group action. A map is G-equivariant if acting by g before or after gives the same result: φ(g·x) = g·φ(x). Invariance is the degenerate case where g·φ(x) = φ(x) for all g.
  • Standard convolution is translation-equivariant. Every CNN in production exploits this; the rest of the architecture is gravy on the invariance of the discrete translation group.
  • Group convolution generalises it. Replace the sum over lattice shifts with a sum over group elements. Feature maps become functions on the group; a layer that was C channels becomes C · |G| channels.
  • Steerable CNNs handle continuous groups. Expand filters in irreps (Fourier on SO(2), spherical harmonics on SO(3)) and rotation by any angle is a closed-form weight transformation.
  • Data efficiency. Equivariance multiplies the effective training set by |G|; empirical gains of 4–10× are typical on rotation-invariant tasks.
  • The simulation and the learner share a grammar. Pick a lattice for your substrate and a group for your symmetry; everything downstream — conservation laws, isotropy, data efficiency, extrapolation — follows from that pair of choices.

That closes the series. The sibling series — Invariance: Noether, Emergence, and the Exceptional Atlas — pick up adjacent threads: what a symmetry is in the abstract, what structure emerges when simple rules are iterated, and where the exceptional lattices and groups live.
