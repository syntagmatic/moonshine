HPP vs FHP: Why Hexagons

Part 4 of 6. Hardy, Pomeau and de Pazzis's square-grid lattice gas (1973) has a viscosity that depends on flow direction; Frisch, Hasslacher and Pomeau's hexagonal one (1986) obeys Navier–Stokes. One rank-4 tensor accounts for the difference.

Part 3 showed that a rule treating its neighbours alike keeps the symmetry of its lattice. For a lattice gas there is a second question: whether that symmetry is enough for the gas to behave like an isotropic fluid. A lattice gas is close to the simplest possible model of a fluid. Particles sit on the sites of a regular lattice, each carrying one of a small set of discrete velocities, one for every edge leaving the site. A time step has two phases. In streaming, each particle hops along its velocity to a neighbouring site. In collision, the particles at each site scatter off each other by a local rule that conserves mass and momentum. The whole state is a Boolean table of which links are occupied.

Averaged over many sites and steps, does this model behave like a real fluid? A Chapman–Enskog expansion is how you find out: it defines coarse-grained density and momentum fields from the Boolean microdynamics and derives the partial differential equation they obey. To get incompressible Navier–Stokes, the convective term has to come out right, and that term depends on a single rank-4 tensor built from the lattice's velocity set. On a square lattice the tensor is anisotropic; on a hexagonal lattice it is isotropic.

The two lattices and their rules

HPP uses a square lattice with four velocity directions (±x, ±y), the nearest-neighbour edges of ℤ². FHP uses a triangular (hexagonal-symmetric) lattice with six velocity directions at 60° apart. A Boolean occupation variable says whether each directed link at each site is occupied; the Pauli-style exclusion principle forbids two particles with the same velocity at the same site.

HPP collisionssquare, 4 velocities
A head-on pair along x becomes a head-on pair along y, and vice versa. Every other configuration passes through unchanged. Mass, momentum and (since all speeds are equal) kinetic energy are conserved.
FHP collisionshex, 6 velocities
Head-on pair rotates by +60° or −60° (chosen randomly); three particles symmetric under 120° rotation reverse all three. Mass and momentum conserved. Head-on collisions alone would conserve momentum along each of the three lines separately, three conserved numbers where momentum accounts for two; the three-body rule removes the spurious third.

Both rules are lookup tables on the possible incoming configurations at a site, and both conserve mass and momentum exactly. What separates them is the lattice geometry.

The tensor on each lattice

Sum ca,ica,jca,kca,l over the four HPP velocities (±1, 0), (0, ±1). Each velocity has a single non-zero component, so a product cicjckcl vanishes unless all four indices are equal. The sum is 2δijδjkδkl, a purely "diagonal" term that no combination of δδ pairings reproduces, so the HPP tensor is not of the isotropic form.

Sum the same quantity over the six FHP velocities ca = (cos πa/3, sin πa/3) for a = 0, …, 5. The cross terms average away by the 6-fold symmetry, leaving exactly the two-parameter isotropic form with A = 3/4 and B = 3/4 and no diagonal term.

HPP is non-zero only in the two entries where i=j=k=l (xxxx and yyyy); the xxyy and xyxy entries an isotropic tensor needs are empty. FHP's pattern depends only on which δ pairings are formed, as an isotropic tensor's must.

In group-theoretic terms, the square lattice's point group, C4v (or D4), has a two-dimensional subspace of invariant rank-4 symmetric tensors. One basis vector is the isotropic δδ-combination. The other is the all-equal-indices tensor, invariant under D4 but not under the full rotation group O(2). The hexagonal lattice's point group, D6, has only the first: the six 60° directions average the extra term away. Moving from HPP (1973) to FHP (1986) removed that one extra dimension.

A shear wave on each lattice

The same tensor controls viscosity, which gives a way to watch it act. A shear wave is a velocity field that points along some direction t and varies sinusoidally along the perpendicular direction n, like layers of fluid sliding past each other. The velocity never points along its own gradient, so there is no advection, only viscous decay: the amplitude falls as exp(−νk2t), where k is the wavenumber and ν the shear viscosity. The shear stress that drives the decay is proportional to the tensor component Tntnt, the fourth moment taken twice along n and twice along t.

For FHP that component is 3/4 whatever the orientation, because the tensor is isotropic. For HPP it is sin22θ, where θ is the angle between n and the x axis. A wave whose layers line up with the grid has Tntnt = 0 and nothing to damp it. Microscopically, no HPP velocity has both an x and a y component, and a head-on collision turns an x-pair into a y-pair at the same site, so the total y-momentum in each column of the lattice never changes. Tilt the wave 45° and it decays like an ordinary viscous flow.

HPP works as designed. It conserves mass and momentum, and its long-wavelength limit is some hydrodynamic equation. That equation is not Navier–Stokes: its viscosity depends on the orientation of the flow, and its convective term carries the same anomalous piece. The broken symmetry sits in the fourth moment of the velocity set, where no coefficient can tune it away.

The extra invariant of the square lattice

One naive fix is to add diagonal velocities to HPP: eight directions instead of four. That is the geometry of lattice Boltzmann's D2Q9 stencil, the subject of Part 5. Its point group is still D4. What the stencil gains is two shells of velocities (length 1 and length √2), which give enough independent weights to solve a system of moment constraints and make the fourth-moment tensor isotropic to the required order. FHP is isotropic without tuning because its six directions at 60° apart form a single orbit of D6, and the sum over any orbit of a group containing a rotation of order > 4 is automatically isotropic to fourth order.

Representation theory makes this precise. The symmetric rank-4 tensors in 2D form a 5-dimensional vector space (the number of ways to put 4 indistinguishable indices into 2 slots). Decompose that space into irreducible representations of the point group and count how often the trivial representation appears. Under D4 it appears twice: there are two independent D4-invariant symmetric rank-4 tensors, which as quartic polynomials are (x2 + y2)2 and x4 + y4. Under D6 it appears once, so up to scaling the only D6-invariant symmetric rank-4 tensor is the isotropic one. The second D4 invariant is the anisotropy.

Simulation details

Both lattices in Figure 2 are periodic, so streaming permutes the occupied links and conserves mass and momentum exactly. The HPP lattice is 64 × 64. The FHP lattice is 64 × 74 with odd rows shifted by half a spacing, which makes its physical height 74 × √3/2 ≈ 64 as well. Each site stores its occupied links as bits and collides through a lookup table: HPP's head-on rule, and FHP-I's head-on rule with a random ±60° choice plus its three-body rule. At step zero each link is occupied with probability d(1 + 2ca·u), which gives mean momentum ρu for a shear field u of amplitude 0.3. Each wave has one period across the box, so on the square lattice the aligned and 45° waves have wavenumbers differing by √2, which dividing the decay rate by k2 removes.

The measured viscosities vary by around ten per cent between restarts, because each run averages only four small copies of each lattice. Below a link density of 0.2 the two FHP waves separate, and the wave along x decays faster. At low density a particle travels several sites between collisions, the wave is no longer long compared with that distance, and the expansion behind the fourth-moment argument stops being accurate.

Part 5 turns to lattice Boltzmann, which replaces the Boolean occupations with real-valued populations and uses a system of moment constraints in place of FHP's single orbit, a system that can be solved on the square lattice.
