HPP vs FHP: Why Hexagons

Part 4 of 6. Hardy, Pomeau and de Pazzis's square-grid lattice gas (1973) has a viscosity that depends on flow direction; Frisch, Hasslacher and Pomeau's hexagonal one (1986) obeys Navier–Stokes. One rank-4 tensor accounts for the difference.

Part 3 showed that a rule treating its neighbours alike keeps the symmetry of its lattice. A lattice gas asks whether that symmetry is enough for the gas to flow like an isotropic fluid. Particles sit on the sites of a lattice, each with one of a few discrete velocities, at most one particle per velocity per site. Each step, every particle hops one link along its velocity, then the particles at each site scatter by a local rule that conserves mass and momentum. A continuum fluid is symmetric under every rotation. The square lattice keeps four of them and the hexagonal lattice six; the question is whether the averaged flow can tell.

Two lattices, two collision rules

HPP: square, 4 velocities
A head-on pair along x leaves as a head-on pair along y, and vice versa. Every other configuration passes through.
FHP-I: hexagonal, 6 velocities
A head-on pair turns by 60°, left or right at random. Three particles at 120° reverse.

Both rules conserve mass and momentum exactly, and both can conserve more than a fluid does. No HPP velocity has both an x and a y part, and a collision swaps an x-pair for a y-pair on the same site, so the y-momentum of every column never changes: one extra conservation law per column and per row. FHP with head-on collisions alone keeps the momentum Da along each of its three lines separately, three numbers where momentum has two. Because c0 − c1 + c2 = 0, the combination Q = D0 − D1 + D2 carries no momentum at all, and nothing but the three-body rule can change it.

What the fourth moment of the velocities must look like

Averaged over many sites and steps, a Chapman–Enskog expansion turns the Boolean dynamics into equations for density and momentum. Both the convective and the viscous parts of the momentum flux come out built from one tensor, the fourth moment of the velocity set:

For the flow equations to be rotation-invariant this tensor must be isotropic. In two dimensions the only fully symmetric isotropic rank-4 tensor is

which is the same in every orthonormal frame. So the test is to rotate the frame: take a unit vector n at angle θ and its perpendicular t, and write T in components along them. An isotropic T gives the same numbers at every θ, with Tnnnn = 3Tntnt. Tntnt is the component that turns a shear along n into stress along t.

Adding diagonals does not raise the point group: the eight-direction stencil is still D4. It adds a second speed, and with it a weight that can be tuned. That is the route Part 5 takes.

Why six directions and not four

For a regular polygon of n unit velocities, the rank-k moment along n is f(θ) = Σa cosk(θ − 2πa/n). Expanded in harmonics cos mθ (with m ≤ k and of the same parity), the sum over the polygon kills every harmonic unless n divides m. For rank 4 on the square, m = 4 survives: the second D4-invariant quartic, x4 + y4 beside (x2 + y2)2. Under D6 only (x2 + y2)2 is left.

Measuring the viscosity with a shear wave

A shear wave is a velocity along t that varies as a sine along n. It has no advection, so it only decays, as exp(−νk2t) for wavenumber k, and fitting that decay measures the shear viscosity ν for that orientation.

HPP's viscosity is zero on both axes and largest at 45°, and no collision table on these four velocities can change that: their fourth moment has the wrong shape whatever happens at the sites. FHP's points stay within about 10% of the Chapman–Enskog line but are not quite flat. Seed after seed, waves near a lattice direction (0°, 63.4°) decay a little faster than the one 30° off (90°). That sixfold pattern is the rank-6 row of Figure 3. It enters the decay rate at order k4, so it fades at longer wavelengths; the Chapman–Enskog value is the long-wavelength limit.

All runs are periodic and seeded, and start with each link occupied with probability d(1 + 2ca·u) for a shear field u of amplitude 0.3.
