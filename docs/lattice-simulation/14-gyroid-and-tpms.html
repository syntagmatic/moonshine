Gyroid & TPMS

Part 14 of 15. Triply periodic minimal surfaces are the lattice made flesh. A single trigonometric equation cut at level c produces an infinite, zero-mean-curvature sheet that tiles three-space with the translation group Z3. Aerospace 3D-printers fabricate these surfaces directly; the unit cell is the part.

The first thirteen explainers treated the lattice as a stage, the set of sites on which a simulation runs. This one flips the relationship. The lattice is no longer a discretisation of some pre-existing continuum; it is the geometry of a manufactured object. A metal-powder 3D-printer builds a part whose inside is a periodic foam, and the foam is the orbit of a single implicit surface under translation by Z3. The lattice has become the thing being made.

The mathematical object underneath is a triply periodic minimal surface. A surface in ℝ3 is minimal when its mean curvature vanishes everywhere: at every point the two principal curvatures cancel, so the surface is locally a saddle of equal but opposite bending. It is triply periodic when it is invariant under three linearly independent translations, so it carries a lattice as part of its definition. A TPMS is to a 3D lattice what a doubly periodic minimal surface (a catenoid array, for instance) is to a 2D lattice: an infinite sheet, perfectly soap-film-balanced in every cell.

Three surfaces dominate. H. A. Schwarz found two of them in 1865: Schwarz P (Primitive) and Schwarz D (Diamond). The third, the gyroid, was discovered more than a century later by Alan Schoen at NASA in 1970 while he was looking for strong, lightweight panels for spacecraft. The gyroid's defining feature is negative: it contains no straight lines and no planar reflection symmetries. That absence turns out to make its mechanical response more isotropic than either of its older siblings, which is why gyroid-infilled parts now show up inside turbine blades, satellite brackets, and heat exchangers.

The three implicit equations

All three classical TPMS admit approximate implicit equations built from low-frequency Fourier modes on the cubic lattice. They are not exact (the true Schwarz P surface is a transcendental object) but the zero-level sets of these trigonometric polynomials are visually indistinguishable from the true surfaces at printable resolutions.

Schwarz D (Diamond), diamond-cubic topology
Gyroid (Schoen, 1970), chiral, no mirror planes

In each case x, y, z are measured in units of 2π divided by the lattice constant a, so the equation is automatically (2π)-periodic in all three directions. The Fourier support of the P surface sits on the six reciprocal-lattice vectors of length one (the {100}-family). The D surface uses the eight {111} vectors. The gyroid uses twelve {110} vectors. The smaller the reciprocal-lattice set, the smoother the real-space surface.

Level-set offset and the one-parameter family

Replacing the right-hand side 0 with a constant c generalises each equation to a one-parameter family of isosurfaces. At c = 0 the zero-mean-curvature minimal surface appears. For c ≠ 0 the surface is no longer minimal but is still periodic, still smooth away from a small number of critical c-values, and still divides space into two disjoint regions with a well-defined volume fraction. Printed gyroid parts almost never use c = 0 directly. Instead, they use the pair of offsets ±t and fill the slab

with solid material. The result is a connected skin of thickness 2t centred on the minimal surface: a sheet-based TPMS lattice. Alternatively, one can fill an entire half-space f(r) > c and leave the other half empty, a graph-based TPMS lattice. The sheet variant looks like a honeycomb; the graph variant looks like two interpenetrating tree networks separated by the surface. Both variants are used; the choice depends on which mechanical property (stiffness, energy absorption, permeability) the part needs to optimise.

How a TPMS lives on the lattice

All three surfaces are invariant under integer translations of the cubic lattice Z3. The space group that fixes the minimal surface is larger than translation alone: Schwarz P belongs to Pm3̄m (the full symmetry of the cube), Schwarz D to Pn3̄m, and the gyroid to Ia3̄d. The gyroid's space group is chiral: it contains 4-fold screw axes and threefold inversion points but no mirror planes. A left-handed gyroid is topologically distinct from a right-handed one; the two are mirror images that cannot be superimposed by any rigid motion.

This is where the lattice and group pillars of the series meet one more time. The gyroid is the orbit of a small surface patch (a fundamental domain of the space group, occupying 1/96 of a unit cell for Ia3̄d) under the action of that space group. Each of the 96 copies tiles one cell; each of ℤ3's infinitely many translations tiles a different cell. Everything else is combinatorics.

Why a Fourier polynomial can be a minimal surface. The true Schwarz P surface satisfies the mean-curvature equation exactly, which is a non-linear PDE with no closed-form solution. But it has an expansion in plane waves whose wavevectors lie on the reciprocal lattice. Truncate that expansion at the lowest non-zero frequency (six waves for P, twelve for the gyroid) and the zero level set of the truncation is already within a fraction of a percent of the true surface. The approximation is good because minimal surfaces are extremely smooth: the mean-curvature operator is elliptic, so high-frequency components decay fast. The implicit equation is therefore the surface's lowest-order Fourier fingerprint on the reciprocal lattice.

Why gyroid in practice

Of the three classical TPMS, the gyroid gets most of the engineering attention. Four properties make it dominant:

1. Low bending energy. Zero mean curvature means the surface is locally a balanced saddle. Applied stress redistributes as membrane tension rather than bending, which is mechanically expensive for thin-walled structures. The gyroid's solid skin deforms primarily in stretching, which is why its stiffness-to-weight ratio beats strut-based lattices of the same mass.

2. Near-isotropic response. The gyroid's space group is Ia3̄d, whose rotation subgroup contains the full 24-element rotation group of the cube. By the same tensor argument from Act III, that point group is large enough to force the macroscopic elastic tensor to be nearly isotropic: only two independent elastic moduli, like an amorphous solid. Strut-based lattices with cubic symmetry show roughly 2× Young's-modulus variation with loading direction; gyroid foams show less than 10%.

3. Manufacturable. The gyroid contains no overhangs steeper than 45° in typical build orientations, so a metal powder-bed printer can print the whole structure without support material. Strut lattices usually need sacrificial supports that waste material and leave surface roughness where they are broken off.

4. Enormous surface-to-volume ratio. For the same mass of material, a gyroid exposes 2–3× more surface area than a foam of randomly-packed struts. Heat exchangers and catalyst beds want that ratio maximised; the gyroid is at or near the mathematical optimum.

Volume fraction, tuned continuously

Two knobs control the printed geometry. The lattice constant a sets the feature size: the typical channel width and wall thickness both scale linearly with a. The level-set offset t sets the volume fraction φ, the fraction of each cell that is solid. For a sheet-based gyroid, φ is approximately 0.5 at t = 0 (the minimal surface itself is infinitely thin, but dilating it by any t > 0 immediately claims half the cell volume), rising to 1 as t saturates the cell. The first-order approximation

is accurate to a few percent over the useful printing range. Aerospace designers use a spatial field t(r) that varies slowly across the part, giving thin walls where stress is low and thick walls in load paths: a functionally graded lattice. The feature size a(r) can vary too, but more carefully, because abrupt a-changes create seams between non-matching cells.

Cross-sections and the minimal-surface signature

A specific feature is worth looking for in Figure 1. For the P surface at z = 0, the zero level set is a single closed curve: one connected component per cell. For the gyroid at the same z, the zero level set is a pair of interlocking wavy curves that do not touch. This is the signature of the two interpenetrating channel networks: network A and network B are disconnected inside each cell but individually connected through the whole lattice. Walk z from 0 to 1 and the two curves twist around each other, which is the chirality of the space group showing up as a visible rotation of the cross-section with depth.

Where this closes the series

Most of the explainers before this one treated the lattice as a computational scaffold: a way to approximate a continuous problem with a finite one. The gyroid turns that relationship around. Here the lattice is physical (the unit cell is built, not sampled) and the continuous object (a minimal surface) is approximated by its Fourier expansion on the reciprocal lattice. The algebra is the same either way. A periodic function is a sum of plane waves whose wavevectors live on the reciprocal lattice, and the symmetry group of the surface selects which wavevectors appear.

Act I introduced the lattice as a free abelian group of rank n. Act V's closing explainer, group-equivariant neural networks, will take the same group and bake it into the weight tensor of a learning machine. The gyroid sits between the two: the lattice as object, printed in metal, used in flight.

Takeaways

  • TPMS = triply-periodic minimal surface. Zero mean curvature, invariant under three independent translations, cut from a single implicit equation whose Fourier support lies on the reciprocal lattice.
  • Three classical surfaces. Schwarz P (1865, Fourier {100}), Schwarz D (1865, {111}), gyroid (Schoen 1970, {110}). The gyroid was discovered last and has no straight lines, no planar mirrors, and the best mechanical isotropy.
  • Level sets tune volume fraction. f(r) = ±t gives a sheet-TPMS solid of thickness 2t; f(r) > c gives a graph-TPMS filling one side of the surface. Volume fraction scales linearly with t near the minimal surface.
  • Lattice tiles seamlessly. The surface is an orbit of a fundamental patch under the space group; translations by Z3 tile the full infinite structure with no discontinuities at cell boundaries.
  • Why the gyroid ships. Low bending energy, near-isotropic stiffness (inherited from the cubic rotation group), support-free printability, and the highest surface-to-volume ratio of any known regular cellular solid.
  • The lattice is the part. A 3D-printed aerospace bracket in-filled with a gyroid is a physical realisation of the translation group Z3 acting on a minimal surface patch: crystallography turned into load-bearing metal.
