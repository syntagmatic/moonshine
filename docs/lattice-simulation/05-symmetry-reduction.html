Symmetry Reduction

Part 5 of 15. A molecule with point group G has only |atoms|/|G| independent atoms, the asymmetric unit. Compute forces on that unit, propagate the rest with the group action. For a Td fullerene this is a twenty-four-fold saving for free.

The previous explainer showed that choosing the right integrator (one that respects the symplectic structure of Hamilton's equations) buys you bounded energy error over arbitrarily long trajectories. That was a symmetry argument in the time direction: the flow preserves the symplectic 2-form, so discretisations that preserve a shadow of it conserve a shadow Hamiltonian. This explainer is the configuration-space cousin. If the system itself carries a point-group symmetry, we can exploit that symmetry at discretisation time and compute forces on a small fraction of the atoms.

The numbers matter. A Hartree–Fock or DFT calculation on benzene evaluates a four-index electron-repulsion integral that scales as O(N4) in the number of basis functions. Benzene has point group D6h, a group of order 24. If every term in the four-index sum has a definite transformation law under D6h, then only one in twenty-four of those terms is independent; the rest are fixed by the group action. Real quantum-chemistry codes realise this factor routinely. For a C60 fullerene with point group Ih of order 120, it is a hundred-and-twenty-fold win.

Asymmetric unit: the fundamental domain of G acting on atoms

Let G be a finite group of orthogonal transformations fixing the origin, a point group. Let G act on ℝ3 by rotations and reflections. A molecule is a finite set A ⊂ ℝ3 of atom positions, and we say G is a symmetry of the molecule when g·A = A for every g ∈ G. The group acts on A, and the orbit of an atom x ∈ A is

The atom set A partitions into disjoint orbits. For benzene, C6H6, the six carbons form one orbit and the six hydrogens form another; D6h has twelve rotational elements and twelve elements with a reflection, and acts transitively on each orbit. An asymmetric unit is a choice of one representative atom from each orbit. Benzene has two orbits, so its asymmetric unit has two atoms: one C and one H, totalling 2 = 12 / 6 atoms, which is |atoms|/|C6| since the stabiliser of each carbon under the rotational subgroup is trivial for a generic position. In full D6h language, 12 / 24 = ½ atom of each kind, but we always round up to one: the fundamental domain includes part of each symmetry-axis atom.

The algorithmic use is the force-evaluation covariance:

If you computed the force F(x) at one representative atom x, then the force at g·x is obtained by applying the same group element to the vector. You do not need to re-evaluate the potential, read the electron density, or touch the neighbour list. For a homogeneous pair potential this is a sanity check; for a DFT computation with a million-basis-function Fock matrix build, it is a twenty-four-fold saving.

Covariance of forces: the computation you don't repeat

Why is this legal? Because the energy functional that produces the forces is invariant under G. If V(x1, …, xN) is the total potential energy, G-symmetry says V(g·x1, …, g·xN) = V(x1, …, xN) for every g ∈ G. Differentiate both sides by xi, use the chain rule, and you get the covariance above: F(g·x) = g · F(x). The cost of the gradient at the image atom is zero, given the cost at the representative.

For pair-potential MD the saving is modest (the neighbour search still costs something); for DFT it is a factor of |G| on the most expensive step in the code.

The catch. Symmetry reduction only works if the simulation preserves the symmetry throughout its evolution. A molecule in a symmetric ground state can develop vibrations that break G down to a subgroup H; at that moment the asymmetric unit has to expand by |G|/|H| atoms. Ab-initio MD codes therefore monitor the current-configuration symmetry and adapt: full G during equilibration, reduced symmetry when a mode breaks it. A Jahn–Teller-unstable molecule like cyclobutadiene (formally D4h) spends its actual life at D2h because the high-symmetry geometry is a saddle point. The symmetry group of the Hamiltonian is D4h; the symmetry group of the equilibrium configuration is smaller.

Block-diagonalising the Hessian

The force-covariance trick is the first-order statement. The second-order statement is representation theory. A vibrational normal-mode analysis diagonalises the 3N × 3N mass-weighted Hessian matrix

whose eigenvalues λ = ω2 are the squared vibrational frequencies and whose eigenvectors are the normal modes. For benzene, 3N = 36 (twelve atoms in three dimensions), which is a 36×36 eigenproblem in the naive formulation, and there are 30 non-trivial vibrational frequencies (after subtracting six for rigid-body translations and rotations).

Point-group symmetry block-diagonalises this matrix. The 3N-dimensional displacement vector space carries a representation of G, and that representation decomposes into irreducible representations (irreps) of G. Applying Pα to any column of the Hessian gives the component of that column lying in the irrep-α sector. Do this for every irrep and the Hessian separates into its blocks.

In practice no quantum-chemistry code constructs the full 3N × 3N Hessian and then block-diagonalises it; that would defeat the purpose. The code builds the representation of G on the basis functions up front, constructs symmetry-adapted linear combinations (SALCs) of basis functions, and evaluates matrix elements directly between SALCs. Integrals between SALCs in different irreps are guaranteed to vanish and are never computed. For the four-index electron-repulsion integral , symmetry-adapted basis functions force many of the integrals to zero by a selection-rule argument alone, so the code skips them in the innermost loop.

The four-index saving is the big one

For Hartree–Fock, the computational bottleneck is the evaluation of the two-electron integrals

of which there are naively N4 in the basis-function count N. With point-group symmetry of order |G|, the number of symmetry-distinct integrals is asymptotically N4/|G|, and all others are obtained by applying G to the computed ones. For benzene |G| = 24 this is an order-of-magnitude win. For SF6 (Oh, |G| = 48) almost a factor of fifty. For C60 (Ih, |G| = 120) it is the difference between "calculation finishes overnight" and "calculation finishes next week". Every serious electronic-structure code in existence (GAMESS, Gaussian, NWChem, ORCA, Psi4, TURBOMOLE) exploits this.

The saving is not free. Detecting the current-configuration symmetry requires geometry analysis; building symmetry-adapted orbitals requires running the character table against the orbital basis; monitoring symmetry-breaking during SCF iterations requires extra bookkeeping. These are all one-time costs that get amortised over the cubic or quartic inner loop. The net win is the ratio of the reduced loop cost to the full loop cost, approaching exactly |G| as N → ∞.

The pattern: symmetry of the problem, exploited at discretisation time

The shape of this argument is the same as the symplectic-integrator argument in the previous explainer: there is a symmetry of the continuous problem that we want the discrete approximation to honour. In the symplectic case it is the time-translation symmetry of the Hamiltonian flow, which forces the integrator to preserve the 2-form. Here it is the point-group symmetry of the molecular Hamiltonian, which forces the discretisation (the basis-function expansion) to align with the irreps of the group.

Act III will push this further: on a lattice, the point group of the lattice becomes the relevant symmetry, and its irreps determine which discrete velocity sets a lattice Boltzmann method can use, which rank-4 tensors are available for a lattice gas to recover isotropic Navier–Stokes, and which cellular automaton rules commute with the grid symmetries. The mathematics is identical; only the group changes.

The subgroup poset of reductions

The catch in the Jahn–Teller footnote above (reduce by G when the configuration is fully symmetric; fall back to a subgroup H ⊂ G when a mode breaks it) is really a choice, not a retreat. Every subgroup of the full point group is a candidate reduction. The poset of those subgroups makes the design space visible: each node is a group you could quotient by, labelled by the dimension of the reduced (asymmetric-unit) configuration space, and the vertical axis is how many degrees of freedom you have collapsed.

Reading it top to bottom, the cascade is a cost-versus-fidelity ledger. Quotient by the full D6h and you pay the fewest force evaluations (a half-atom asymmetric unit, one or two genuine degrees of freedom) but any out-of-irrep motion breaks the reduction and you have to re-expand. Quotient by a small subgroup like C2 and the asymmetric unit is large and the saving is modest, but most physically interesting distortions (E2g stretches, out-of-plane buckles) still live inside it. The poset shows which intermediate groups buy the most dimension-drop for the least loss of generality; real AIMD codes walk this lattice adaptively as the simulation evolves.

Takeaways

  • Asymmetric unit. If a molecule has point group G, only |atoms|/|G| atoms are independent under the group action. Compute forces on that set; propagate the rest.
  • Force covariance. F(g·x) = g·F(x). A free factor of |G| in any force or gradient evaluation, once per time step.
  • Block-diagonal Hessian. The normal-mode eigenproblem splits into one block per irrep of G. For benzene the biggest block is 4×4 instead of 36×36.
  • Character tables do the work. Projectors built from characters separate the displacement space into irrep sectors. Symmetry-adapted basis functions make many matrix elements zero by inspection.
  • Four-index ERI saving. Electronic-structure codes reduce O(N4) integral evaluation to O(N4/|G|). For Td fullerenes that is 24×, for Ih fullerenes 120×.
  • Symmetry of the Hamiltonian vs. the configuration. Jahn–Teller and other symmetry-breaking distortions force the code to track the instantaneous symmetry and fall back to a subgroup when the full one no longer applies.
