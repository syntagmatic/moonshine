Symplectic Integrators

Part 2 of 6. A time step has no reason to conserve energy. One that keeps the symplectic structure of Hamilton's equations keeps its energy error bounded and some momenta exact, though not every symmetry.

Part 1 discretised space; this part discretises time. The exact flow of a Hamiltonian system preserves phase-space area, runs backwards, and conserves the momentum of each symmetry of the Hamiltonian. The figures below measure which of these four common integrators keep.

Hamilton's flow preserves area

For a Hamiltonian H(q, p) the equations of motion are

and for H = p2/2 + V(q) they are Newton's law. The Jacobian M of the flow over any time satisfies

In one degree of freedom this is det M = 1: the flow preserves area in the (q, p) plane, which is Liouville's theorem. With more degrees of freedom the condition is stronger than det = 1, since it preserves the oriented area of every two-dimensional projection, not just total volume. A map that satisfies it is called symplectic.

Three integrators on the pendulum

Take the pendulum, H = p2/2 − cos q, and three ways to advance it by a step h:

Forward Euler, order 1
q ← q + h · p
p ← p − h · sin(q_old)
RK4, order 4
k1 = f(y), k2 = f(y + h·k1/2)
k3 = f(y + h·k2/2), k4 = f(y + h·k3)
y ← y + h/6·(k1+2k2+2k3+k4)
Velocity Verlet, order 2
p ← p − (h/2) · sin(q)
q ← q + h · p
p ← p − (h/2) · sin(q)

RK4 costs four force evaluations per step, Verlet one (its closing half-kick reuses the next step's force).

Euler and RK4 have no such function: neither preserves area, and no Hamiltonian flow changes it.

Can the step be run backwards?

The pendulum's flow is reversible: flip the sign of p, run for the same time again, and you are back at the start with p reversed. Verlet's step is symmetric (its inverse is itself with h → −h), so it retraces exactly. Symplectic Euler, a full kick and then a drift, preserves area but is not symmetric. RK4 is neither.

1000

Figure 5. From (1.3, 0) with h = 0.2: run N steps, flip p, run N more, and measure the distance from (1.3, 0). Log scale; the dashed line is double-precision rounding, 2.2 × 10−16. Verlet misses by accumulated rounding only. Symplectic Euler comes back near the right orbit but at the wrong phase, so its miss is on the scale of the orbit itself. RK4 and Euler miss by amounts that grow with N.

Hamiltonian Monte Carlo relies on this: its acceptance rule is correct only if the integrator preserves volume and is reversible, which is why it uses Verlet under the name leapfrog (Neal 2011).

Rotation symmetry and angular momentum

Each continuous symmetry of H comes with a conserved momentum. Verlet's kick is the exact flow of V alone and its drift the exact flow of p2/2 alone. If both are rotation invariant, each substep conserves angular momentum L exactly, and so does the step. Symplectic Euler is built from the same two flows. RK4 treats a rotated orbit exactly as it treats the original, yet loses L anyway. The planar Kepler problem, H = |p|2/2 − 1/|q|, has the rotation symmetry to test this.

On any run this figure can show, RK4 has the smaller energy error, while losing angular momentum. The bounded band wins only on runs of the length the readout reports, which is the scale of long molecular-dynamics and solar-system integrations. Higher-order symplectic methods, such as Yoshida's (1990) compositions of Verlet steps, keep the band and the exact momenta with a smaller band width.

Which symmetry does Verlet lose?

Kepler's problem has a hidden symmetry. The Runge–Lenz vector A = p × L − q/|q| points at perihelion and is conserved, which is why the orbits close. Verlet's shadow Hamiltonian is still rotation invariant, so L survives, but its potential is no longer exactly 1/r, so A does not.

Verlet and symplectic Euler precess at nearly the same rate, and halving h divides it by about 4. Symplectic Euler's line starts offset because its orbit is shifted at first order in h. RK4 precesses too, but at a rate that falls as h4 and is flat at this scale.
