Symplectic Integrators

Part 2 of 6. A Hamiltonian flow preserves a 2-form on phase space. An integrator that preserves it too keeps its energy error bounded; one that doesn't lets the error grow. Velocity Verlet does it in three lines, because its Jacobian is a product of shears.

A molecular-dynamics run simulates Newton's laws for millions of particles over billions of time steps. If the integrator ignores the geometric structure of the equations, a run that long drifts: energy creeps up or down, orbits unwind, and the simulated world heats or cools for no physical reason.

The fix comes from geometric integration. Hamilton's equations describe a flow that preserves a particular antisymmetric 2-form on phase space. Any numerical scheme that preserves a discrete analogue of that form nearly conserves a slightly shifted energy, closely enough that its energy error stays bounded over exponentially long times. A generic scheme that does not preserve it lets energy error accumulate step after step.

It is the cleanest case in this series of a general principle: a discretisation that keeps the structure keeps the conservation laws too, in slightly deformed form.

Hamilton's equations, in one line

Fix a smooth function H(q, p) on phase space ℝ2n, with coordinates q (position) and p (momentum). Hamilton's equations of motion are

For a particle of mass m in a potential V(q), take H = p2/(2m) + V(q); then dq/dt = p/m and dp/dt = −V′(q), which is Newton's F = ma. The Hamiltonian form is equivalent, but it exposes a structure the Newtonian form hides: the flow on phase space preserves an antisymmetric bilinear form, the symplectic 2-form

Preserving ω means: for any two infinitesimal displacements δ1 and δ2 in phase space, the oriented area ω(δ1, δ2) = δ1q · δ2p − δ1p · δ2q is the same before and after the flow. Equivalently, the Jacobian M of the flow satisfies MT J M = J, where J is the standard symplectic matrix with +I and −I off the diagonal. In one degree of freedom ω is just signed area in the (q, p) plane, and preservation is Liouville's theorem: phase-space volume is conserved.

Any symplectic map has Jacobian determinant 1 (because det MTJM = (det M)2 det J). In one degree of freedom the converse also holds: a 2×2 matrix is symplectic exactly when its determinant is 1, so area preservation is the whole story. With two or more degrees of freedom, determinant 1 is strictly weaker. Volume preservation in ℝ4 allows maps that squeeze the (q1, p1) plane while stretching (q2, p2); symplecticity forbids that, because it preserves the oriented area of every 2-dimensional projection.

Three integrators on one pendulum

Take the pendulum, with H(q, p) = p2/2 − cos q. Its level sets are small oscillations near (0, 0), separatrices at energy 1, and rotations for energy > 1. The true flow keeps each trajectory on its level set exactly, because H is conserved. Here are three integrators, each with time step h.

Forward Euler is the textbook first try: two lines, first-order accurate, with Jacobian

whose determinant is 1 + h2 cos q. At a stable equilibrium (small q, cos q ≈ 1) the determinant is strictly greater than 1, so phase-space volume inflates on every step. The violation of Liouville's theorem compounds geometrically, and the orbits spiral outward.

RK4 is the workhorse of scientific computing. Its per-step error is O(h5) and its global error O(h4). Its Jacobian is not symplectic either. Fourth-order accuracy makes the energy drift very small, but the drift is still there, and on long runs it accumulates.

Velocity Verlet is a half-kick to momentum, a drift to position using the new momentum, and another half-kick. It is only second-order accurate, so over short runs it is worse than RK4. But its Jacobian is a product of three shears:

Each factor is a shear matrix, a linear map of the form I + nilpotent, whose determinant is exactly 1 and which preserves ω exactly. A product of symplectic maps is symplectic, so Verlet's update map is exactly symplectic for every step size h, with no error term.

Verlet also has no free parameters, is explicit and time-reversible, and needs one force evaluation per step, since the end-of-step force is reused at the start of the next. RK4 needs four and is more accurate per step. On a long enough run its accumulating energy error still overtakes Verlet's bounded one; how long depends on the step size and the problem, and the Kepler figure below measures it.

Why Euler inflates, why Verlet does not

For small h and short runs, RK4 is more accurate than Verlet, so the difference is structural rather than a matter of per-step accuracy.

Forward Euler's one-step map q, p ↦ q + hp, p − h sin q has Jacobian

with det = 1 + h2 cos q > 1 near the stable equilibrium. A small cloud of initial conditions of area A0 grows to A0(1 + h2)n ≈ A0 · exp(n h2) after n steps, and the energy, roughly quadratic near equilibrium, grows exponentially with it.

Velocity Verlet's one-step map is the composition of three shears. Writing K(q) = (h/2)(−sin q) for the half-kick and D(p) = hp for the drift, the update is
p ← p + K(q); q ← q + D(p); p ← p + K(q). Each sub-step leaves one coordinate alone and shifts the other, so it has Jacobian determinant 1 and is symplectic, and so is their composition. The full step has det = 1 exactly, not just to leading order in h.

This is Liouville's theorem in miniature. A method that does not preserve volume is no longer simulating a Hamiltonian system. It simulates some other dynamical system that agrees with the Hamiltonian one to order hk for a few steps. That is why molecular dynamics, orbital mechanics, accelerator physics and lattice gauge theory use symplectic integrators.

The shadow Hamiltonian

Verlet cannot conserve H exactly, since it is only a second-order approximation to the true flow. What it conserves is a slightly-shifted Hamiltonian Ĥ, the shadow Hamiltonian, whose series expansion in the time step is

You can check this against the simulation. At h = 0.2 and θ0 = 1.3, the default in Figure 1, H itself wanders over a band of width 6.4 × 10−3 during 20,000 Verlet steps. H + h2H2 wanders over 4.8 × 10−5, more than a hundred times less. Each further term in the series shrinks the band again, until the series itself stops converging.

That last clause is where the theorem is careful. The series for Ĥ generally diverges, so no smooth function is conserved exactly. What Benettin and Giorgilli (1994) and Hairer and Lubich (1997) proved is that truncating it at the optimal order gives a function conserved up to an error exponentially small in 1/h, over a time exponentially long in 1/h. Since Ĥ differs from H by O(h2), the energy error H(qn, pn) − H(q0, p0) oscillates inside a band of width O(h2) for a time far longer than any simulation will run.

Forward Euler has no such function. It expands phase-space area on every step, so no nearby Hamiltonian can describe it, and its energy climbs. RK4 is not symplectic either, so nothing pins it to a level set; its error per step is tiny, but it has no reason to cancel, and it accumulates. Verlet's trajectory sits on a level set of Ĥ, and those level sets are everywhere close to the level sets of H.

Where Noether actually comes in

This is not quite a discrete Noether theorem for time translation, since only a nearby Ĥ is conserved, and only approximately. The exact discrete statement concerns the other symmetries. Noether's theorem ties each continuous symmetry of the Hamiltonian to a conserved momentum, and an integrator keeps that momentum exactly when its update map is symplectic and also commutes with the symmetry. Commuting alone is not enough: RK4 treats a rotated orbit exactly as it treats the original, and Figure 3 shows it still losing angular momentum.

Verlet meets both conditions, for any symmetry that the kinetic and potential parts share. Each of its sub-steps is the exact flow of one piece of the Hamiltonian: the kick is the flow of V(q) alone, the drift is the flow of p2/2 alone. If both pieces are invariant under rotations, each sub-step conserves angular momentum exactly, and so does their composition. Symplectic Euler, which is also built from these two exact flows, inherits the same property. The general statement is the discrete Noether theorem for variational integrators, the family Verlet belongs to: a discrete action with a symmetry yields a discrete momentum conserved to machine precision.

The planar Kepler problem, H = |p|2/2 − 1/|q|, has the rotation symmetry to test this. Figure 3 adds symplectic Euler, the first-order member of the family.

Why not just use RK4 with a smaller step?

If RK4's error is O(h4) and Verlet's is O(h2), a small enough RK4 step should make the drift negligible over any run we care about. Figure 3 shows how that plays out. RK4's error grows linearly, as N h5 after N steps, while Verlet's stays in a band of fixed width O(h2). At h = 0.02 on the Kepler orbit, 50,000 RK4 steps leave a relative energy error of about 3 × 10−8, thousands of times smaller than Verlet's band. On that run RK4 is simply better, and for an integration of a few orbits it is the right choice.

The two lines cross eventually, and the runs where that matters are the very long ones: molecular dynamics, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and solar-system integrations take 109 steps or more. There, a linearly growing error eventually dominates any fixed band, and shrinking h to postpone the crossing multiplies the cost. Energy is also only one of the quantities at stake. RK4 loses angular momentum at a rate comparable to its energy drift, while Verlet keeps it to rounding error on every run. And for Hamiltonian Monte Carlo the property that matters most is different again: the sampler's acceptance rule is only correct if the integrator is volume-preserving and time-reversible, which Verlet is and RK4 is not.

There are higher-order symplectic integrators too: Forest and Ruth's fourth-order scheme, Yoshida's explicit families of arbitrary even order, McLachlan's optimised fourth-order schemes. They are compositions of Verlet steps with carefully chosen coefficients, and they inherit the shadow-Hamiltonian theorem and the exact momentum conservation. Second-order Verlet is the simplest representative and the one that runs in most production codes.

The same move recurs in space instead of time. The square lattice fails that test and the hexagonal one passes.
