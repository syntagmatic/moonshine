Ising & the Z₂ Break

Part 9 of 15. Every spin can flip. The Hamiltonian is unchanged if all of them do: a global ℤ₂ symmetry. Above the critical temperature the magnetisation averages to zero and ℤ₂ is intact; below it, the system picks one of two broken-symmetry states and stays there.

The previous two explainers treated the lattice as a stage for a deterministic fluid rule. Now we put thermal noise on the stage. A spin at each site, two allowed values, an energy that prefers neighbours to agree: that is the Ising model, written down by Wilhelm Lenz in 1920 and solved in one dimension by his student Ernst Ising in 1924 (who wrongly concluded that the model had no phase transition in any dimension). Onsager's 1944 exact solution in two dimensions made it the canonical lattice model of a continuous phase transition, and its universality class is shared by real magnets, binary alloys, the liquid–gas transition, and any other system whose order parameter is a scalar with ℤ₂ symmetry.

The point of this explainer is not to rederive Onsager. It is to watch the symmetry break. Below the critical temperature, the infinite system develops a non-zero magnetisation: every spin leans the same way. Which way it leans is not determined by the Hamiltonian (the Hamiltonian is perfectly symmetric under the global flip σ → −σ). It is determined by whichever direction the system happened to slip into as it cooled. That is what spontaneous symmetry breaking means, in its starkest lattice form.

The ℤ₂ symmetry

Flip every spin at once: σi → −σi for every i. Each product σiσj picks up (−1)(−1) = +1. So when h = 0, the Hamiltonian is unchanged:

The symmetry group generated by this single flip is the cyclic group of order two, ℤ₂ = {id, flip}. The magnetic field h breaks it explicitly: flipping every spin sends H to H + 2h Σ σi, not back to H. We will mostly work at h = 0 and come back to the infinitesimal-field trick at the end.

The partition function Z = Σ{σ} exp(−H/kT) and the free energy F = −kT log Z are automatically ℤ₂-symmetric when h = 0. So is the ensemble-averaged magnetisation ⟨M⟩, which has to satisfy ⟨M⟩ = ⟨−M⟩ = 0 by a term-by-term pairing of configurations. In finite volume at finite temperature this is always true. The interesting thing is what happens in the infinite-volume limit: ⟨M⟩ jumps discontinuously from zero to ±M0(T) as the limit is taken, for T below a critical value.

Monte Carlo Metropolis

We cannot enumerate 2N configurations of a 64×64 grid (that is 24096, roughly 101233). Instead we generate a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the Boltzmann distribution π(σ) ∝ exp(−H(σ)/kT). The 1953 Metropolis algorithm does exactly this. Pick a site i at random, propose flipping σi, compute the energy change

where si = Σj∈nbrs(i) σj is the sum of the four nearest-neighbour spins. Accept the flip with probability min(1, exp(−ΔE/kT)). If ΔE ≤ 0, always accept; if ΔE > 0, accept with exponentially suppressed probability.

This satisfies detailed balance: π(σ) P(σ → σ′) = π(σ′) P(σ′ → σ) for every pair of configurations differing by one spin flip. Detailed balance plus ergodicity guarantees the chain converges to π. One sweep is N proposed flips, enough that each site is visited once on average. Thermodynamic observables are time-averages along the chain after it has equilibrated.

Why this is cheap. A single-spin flip changes only four bond energies, and the change depends only on σi and its four neighbours. ΔE ∈ {−8J, −4J, 0, +4J, +8J}, five possible values. Precompute exp(−ΔE/kT) once per temperature and the inner loop is a table lookup and one PRNG call. On a modern machine this runs at ten million spin-flips per second in a browser, comfortable for interactive sweeping.

Magnetisation trajectory

The companion plot shows M(t) for the last several hundred sweeps. Three regimes look qualitatively different. At T = 4 the trace bounces around zero with amplitude 1/√N; the ℤ₂ symmetry is intact and both signs are equally likely. At T = 1.5 the trace is trapped near +1 or −1; the barrier between the two ordered phases is of order J × (domain wall area), and at these low temperatures it is crossed only on exponentially long timescales. At T = 2.269 (exactly Tc) the trace shows huge fluctuations across all timescales (critical slowing down) and frequent excursions between positive and negative values. This last behaviour is finite-size; in the true thermodynamic limit the magnetisation just below Tc would pick one sign and stay there forever.

Spontaneous symmetry breaking

Here is the subtle part. Every finite-N Ising system at h = 0 has ⟨M⟩ = 0 identically, because the partition sum pairs each configuration σ with its flipped partner −σ. So in what sense is there a broken phase?

The cleanest answer uses the thermodynamic limit. Turn on an infinitesimal field h = 0+, take the magnetisation ⟨M⟩h,N, then send N → ∞ first and h → 0 afterwards. Below Tc, the two limits do not commute. Taking N → ∞ first makes the barrier between the +M0 and −M0 basins infinitely high; the h = 0+ field then selects the + basin and ⟨M⟩ = +M0(T). Send h → 0+ instead and the infinite-N limit gives a non-zero result:

Equivalently, the free energy per site f(T, h) is analytic in h for T > Tc but has a kink at h = 0 for T < Tc; the magnetisation −∂f/∂h jumps across that kink. The ℤ₂ symmetry is still there in the Hamiltonian; the state fails to preserve it. The simulation demonstrates this by making the basin-crossing time explode as the lattice grows. At T = 1.5 on a 10×10 grid, sign flips of M happen every few hundred sweeps; on an 80×80 grid you can run for hours without seeing one.

Universality

The exponents β = 1/8, γ = 7/4, ν = 1 are not properties of this particular Hamiltonian. They are properties of a universality class: the set of physical systems that flow to the same fixed point under the renormalisation-group transformation (successive coarse-graining of the lattice). Any system with

  • a scalar order parameter,
  • a ℤ₂ symmetry acting by negation,
  • short-range interactions,
  • on a two-dimensional lattice (or any lattice that coarse-grains to a 2D fluctuating surface),

sits in the 2D Ising class and has the same critical exponents. Binary alloys Cu–Zn, vapour–liquid transitions in fluids confined to 2D films, the onset of ferromagnetism in atomically thin materials: all share the 1/8, 7/4, 1 triple. Change to an n-component order parameter with O(n) symmetry and the exponents shift: the XY model (n = 2) and Heisenberg model (n = 3) are different classes. Change from 2D to 3D and the exponents shift again to β ≈ 0.326, γ ≈ 1.237, ν ≈ 0.630 (still Ising universality, but a different fixed point). Change to 1D and the phase transition disappears entirely: Peierls' argument says a single domain wall in 1D costs finite energy but gains extensive entropy, so at any non-zero temperature domain walls proliferate and long-range order is destroyed.

Why lattice details mostly do not matter. Swap the square lattice for a triangular one and the critical temperature changes (Tctri = 4/log 3 ≈ 3.64) but the exponents do not. Add weak next-nearest-neighbour couplings and the exponents do not. Put the spins on a disordered graph with the same average coordination, and provided the disorder is not too strong, the exponents still do not change. The exponents are controlled by the symmetry and the dimensionality of space, with lattice geometry irrelevant in the RG sense. This is the most important fact in critical phenomena.

What the simulation is actually doing

The Metropolis update is a single-flip chain: it explores state space by proposing local moves. Near Tc this is terrible. A correlated domain of size ξ has to be flipped one spin at a time, and the autocorrelation time of M grows as ξz with z ≈ 2.165 for 2D Ising Metropolis. On an 80×80 grid at T = 2.27 you can watch individual domains wobble for thousands of sweeps and barely make progress. This is critical slowing down, and it is why equilibrium measurements near Tc need patience or cleverer algorithms.

The Wolff algorithm (1989) fixes this. Instead of proposing one spin flip, it builds a cluster of like-aligned neighbours (starting from a random seed and adding each same-sign neighbour with probability 1 − exp(−2J/kT)) and flips the whole cluster at once. The cluster has the right distribution to satisfy detailed balance for the Ising measure. Near Tc the cluster is a typical domain, and one cluster flip achieves what hundreds of Metropolis sweeps cannot. The toggle in Figure 1 switches between the two updates; run both at T = 2.27 and watch how much faster Wolff equilibrates.

Back to the theme

This is Act III's last word on what the lattice costs and what it buys. A lattice plus a local symmetric rule gives you a finite-site Hamiltonian with a discrete global symmetry. The symmetry is preserved by the dynamics (Metropolis, Glauber, Wolff all respect ℤ₂). The state in the infinite-volume limit, below a critical temperature, does not. Spontaneous symmetry breaking is an emergent phenomenon of the infinite-site limit, invisible to any finite simulation except as an exponentially slow basin crossover; and its universality class, via the exponents β, γ, ν, is controlled by the symmetry of the order parameter and the dimensionality, not by the lattice itself.

The remaining explainers move away from regular lattices toward graphs (Act IV) and toward lattices as tools rather than as stages (Act V). Symmetry and breaking come back in different clothing: a graph's automorphism group in place of the lattice's point group. The algebraic skeleton is the same.

Takeaways

  • ℤ₂ global flip symmetry. The Ising Hamiltonian at h = 0 is invariant under σ → −σ applied simultaneously to every site. The finite-volume Gibbs measure is also ℤ₂-symmetric.
  • Metropolis samples the Boltzmann distribution. Propose one spin flip, accept with min(1, exp(−ΔE/kT)). Detailed balance makes the chain's stationary distribution exactly π ∝ exp(−H/kT).
  • Onsager's Tc = 2 / log(1 + √2). The 2D square-lattice Ising model has an exactly soluble second-order transition at this temperature (in J = k = 1 units). Below it the magnetisation is non-zero; above, zero.
  • Exponents β = 1/8, γ = 7/4, ν = 1. The order parameter, susceptibility, and correlation length diverge with these exponents on approach to Tc. They are shared by every system in the 2D Ising universality class.
  • Spontaneous symmetry breaking is an infinite-volume phenomenon. Finite lattices have ⟨M⟩ = 0 at h = 0 identically; the ordered phase emerges in the limit N → ∞, h → 0+ taken in that order, where the two limits do not commute.
  • Universality hides lattice details. Change the lattice geometry or add short-range perturbations and Tc moves but the exponents do not. Change the dimensionality or the order-parameter symmetry and they do.
