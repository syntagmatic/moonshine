Bravais Lattices

Part 1 of 15. A lattice is a free abelian group of rank n sitting inside ℝn, all integer combinations of a basis. In two dimensions there are exactly five Bravais types, distinguished by the point group that fixes them. Pick them up by dragging the basis vectors.

Before a simulation runs, it has to decide what space looks like. A cellular automaton places a cell on every point of the integer lattice ℤ². A molecular dynamics code wraps particles into a parallelepiped and repeats. A lattice gauge theory puts gluon link variables on the edges of ℤ⁴. All of these, and every other simulation of a physical system that lives in space, are choosing a lattice: the discrete object that replaces the continuum.

A lattice L is by definition a discrete subgroup of ℝn that spans the whole space as a vector space. Equivalently, it is the set of all integer combinations of a basis:

The basis vectors a1, …, an are the primitive vectors. They are not unique; any integer unimodular change of basis gives another valid set. The parallelogram (or parallelepiped in higher dimension) spanned by one choice of primitive vectors is called a primitive unit cell, and the volume of that cell is an invariant of the lattice, independent of the choice of basis.

In two dimensions the continuum of possible basis shapes (two lengths |a1|, |a2| and one angle γ between them) looks, at first glance, like three continuous parameters. What makes Bravais's classification non-trivial is that we are sorting lattices not by the basis we chose but by the symmetry group of the lattice itself. That symmetry group only takes five values in 2D, no matter what the basis looks like.

The point group of a lattice

The point group of a lattice is the set of all orthogonal transformations (rotations and reflections fixing the origin) that map the lattice to itself. Translation is already in the lattice's translation group; the point group captures the additional symmetries at a site.

Two immediate restrictions. First, the point group must send lattice vectors to lattice vectors, so it is a subgroup of O(2) that also preserves the integer structure. Second, the crystallographic restriction: any rotation in the point group of a 2D or 3D lattice must have order 1, 2, 3, 4, or 6. No 5-fold or 7-fold rotational symmetry is possible. The argument is short: a rotation of order k must permute the finite set of shortest non-zero lattice vectors, and the only sums-to-integer-trace conditions allow k ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 6}.

Why five types, not more. The possible 2D point groups, restricted to those with no forbidden rotations, are C1, C2, C3, C4, C6 (cyclic) and D1, D2, D3, D4, D6 (dihedral, with reflections). Not every one of these is the point group of a lattice: a lattice must contain both directions of every rotation axis and both directions of every mirror, which forces pairs of axes into specific geometric relationships. After the dust settles, exactly five distinct "symmetry classes" of 2D lattice remain, with point groups C2, D2, D2 (centred), D4, D6. These are the five 2D Bravais lattices.

The five 2D Bravais types

Figure 1. The five Bravais types in two dimensions. Oblique (C2) has only a 180° rotation at each site. Rectangular (D2) adds mirrors along the axes. Centred rectangular (D2, centred) is a rectangular lattice with an extra site at the centre of each cell; equivalently, a rhombic lattice with basis vectors of equal length but angle ≠ 60°, 90°, 120°. Square (D4) adds 90° rotations. Hexagonal (D6) is the most symmetric: two equal-length vectors at 60°.

The point-group poset

The five types are not a flat list. They sit in a partial order by point-group inclusion: every point group on the list is contained in, or contains, some of the others. Going up the order means adding a symmetry generator; going down means specialising a continuous parameter back into a generic position. Two types that sit at the same rank with no edge between them are incomparable: no amount of generator-pushing turns one into the other without first passing through something smaller.

Square (D4) and Hexagonal (D6) are the two incomparable maxima. D4 contains a 4-fold rotation; D6 contains a 6-fold rotation; neither group sits inside the other because 4 and 6 are not multiples of one another in the crystallographic sense. This is why the D4-versus-D6 choice reappears throughout the series: the two maxima of the Bravais poset become two rival starting points for any lattice-based physics, and the split will become quantitative in Act III, where the lattice Boltzmann method works on a D6 lattice and fails on a D4 one.

What changes when the Bravais type changes

Every simulation built on a 2D lattice inherits the lattice's point group as a symmetry of its update rule (as long as the rule itself is isotropic in the sense of using only nearest-neighbour relationships). A square-lattice cellular automaton with a Moore neighbourhood has at least D4 symmetry. A hexagonal-lattice one has D6. An oblique-lattice one has only C2, and an anisotropic rule on that oblique lattice will produce macroscopically anisotropic behaviour, because there is no point-group machinery to smooth directional differences.

This becomes quantitative in Act III, when we look at lattice Boltzmann methods. Recovering the Navier–Stokes equations from a discrete lattice fluid requires a rank-4 isotropic tensor: a tensor that is invariant under every rotation. On a square lattice, the largest rank-4 isotropic tensor you can build has one parameter less than you need, and the missing term shows up as artificial anisotropic drag. On a hexagonal lattice, the point group D6 is exactly big enough: its generic rank-4 tensor has the right number of parameters, and the simulation becomes isotropic at the macroscopic scale. The difference between "works" and "does not work" is a point-group count.

From 2D to 3D

In three dimensions the analogous classification gives 14 Bravais lattices grouped into 7 crystal systems, and when we extend from translation groups to full space groups (translations and point-group operations and glides and screw axes) we land at 230 in 3D, 17 in 2D. The 17 is the wallpaper-group count we pick up in the next explainer; the 230 is what crystallographers use to catalogue real materials. Every crystal on earth is one of these 230, and every lattice-based simulation of a 3D material inherits one of them as its symmetry.

Takeaways

  • A lattice is a discrete subgroup. A free abelian group of rank n generated by a basis of ℝn, sitting inside the continuum as a regular grid.
  • Point group = symmetries at a site. Orthogonal transformations that map the lattice to itself. For any lattice in 2D or 3D, rotations must have order 1, 2, 3, 4, or 6: the crystallographic restriction.
  • Five Bravais types in 2D. Oblique (C2), rectangular (D2), centred rectangular (D2′), square (D4), hexagonal (D6). Every 2D periodic lattice is one of these.
  • Fourteen in 3D. The three-dimensional analogue, Bravais's 1848 count, grouped into seven crystal systems.
  • The point group travels with the lattice. Anything simulated on top of the lattice (a CA rule, an MD force field, a gauge theory) inherits the point group as part of its symmetry. Isotropy requires the lattice's point group to be big enough to build the isotropic tensors the physics needs.
