Periodic Boundaries and the Torus

Part 3 of 15. A finite simulation box wants to imitate an infinite continuum. Identify opposite edges and it does: topologically, the box becomes a torus 𝕋² = ℝ²/ℤ², a flat manifold without boundary. Geodesics lift to straight lines on the universal cover; irrational slopes are dense.

Every molecular dynamics code, every lattice Boltzmann solver, every Monte Carlo sampler of a periodic spin system runs on a box of finite side L. The physics it is trying to approximate lives in an unbounded space. Without further ceremony, particles reaching the right wall of the box would bounce, or vanish, or pile up against a reflecting barrier, any of which introduces an artefact the continuum physics does not have.

The standard remedy is topological surgery: glue the right edge of the box to the left edge and the top edge to the bottom. A particle that leaves through one face re-enters through the opposite face with its velocity unchanged. The simulation no longer has boundaries. What it now has, in their place, is a new global shape: a torus.

The quotient construction

Two points in the plane are declared equivalent if they differ by an integer translation:

The set of equivalence classes is the quotient ℝ²/ℤ². Concretely: every point of the plane has a unique representative inside the unit square [0, 1)², and the quotient just is that square with the understanding that (1, y) ≡ (0, y) and (x, 1) ≡ (x, 0). Topologically this is the standard two-dimensional torus 𝕋².

Metrically the torus is flat. Small neighbourhoods of any point look exactly like small neighbourhoods of the plane; the quotient map is a local isometry. The torus has no intrinsic curvature; its global shape comes entirely from the identifications, not from any bending. The round doughnut surface embedded in ℝ³ is a common cartoon, but that embedded surface has non-zero Gaussian curvature and is not the flat torus. The simulation box is the flat torus.

In any dimension n, the same construction gives ℝn/ℤn = 𝕋n, the n-torus. A 3D molecular dynamics box with periodic boundaries is, topologically, 𝕋³.

Straight lines unfold

Take a particle inside the box moving with constant velocity v. At some time it hits an edge and wraps. Inside the box the trajectory looks broken, a piecewise straight path with jumps from one edge to the opposite one. But there is a second, cleaner picture: lift the trajectory to ℝ², where no identifications have been made. On ℝ² the trajectory is a single straight line, and the torus trajectory is its image under the quotient map.

ℝ² is the universal cover of the torus. The covering map is the quotient ℝ² → ℝ²/ℤ². Every point on the torus has infinitely many pre-images in the plane (one for each lattice vector), and a straight line downstairs lifts to a whole family of parallel lines upstairs. The simulation box, tiled across the plane, is just the fundamental domain of this covering action.

Weyl's equidistribution theorem (1916). If α is irrational, the sequence {nα mod 1} is equidistributed in [0, 1). The 2D analogue: a straight-line flow on 𝕋² with velocity (1, α) has every orbit equidistributed in the square whenever α is irrational. Rational α gives closed orbits that cover a finite union of parallel line segments. This is the single cleanest example of the distinction between periodic and quasi-periodic motion, and it is exactly what you watch happen above when you slide the slope off a rational value.

Why the torus, not the sphere

A common student question: why not put the simulation on a sphere instead? Both are compact, both are boundaryless. The answer is that the sphere has intrinsic curvature, and curvature breaks translation invariance. A particle drifting in a uniform direction on a sphere has no "uniform direction": parallel transport around a loop rotates the velocity vector by the holonomy. Gauss–Bonnet forces the total curvature to be 4π, so you cannot flatten it away.

The torus is special precisely because it is the only compact 2D manifold that is both boundaryless and flat. (The Klein bottle is also flat, but non-orientable, which is worse. The Euler characteristic of a compact flat 2-manifold is zero; that singles out the torus and the Klein bottle from the menagerie.) On the torus the Christoffel symbols vanish globally, the notion of a "straight line" is unambiguous, momentum is conserved, and the discrete translation subgroup ℤ² acts freely. Everything a physicist wants from a periodic box.

Minimum-image convention

On the torus, the distance between two points is not the Euclidean distance between any chosen representatives in ℝ². It is the minimum over all pairs of representatives:

For a unit box, this reduces to the componentwise rule: compute Δx = x2 − x1, then shift by whichever of {−1, 0, +1} brings the result closest to zero. Every molecular dynamics code in the world has this rule, usually as a one-line macro. It is what makes the force between two particles near opposite walls act through the periodic wrap, as if the walls weren't there.

The construction only makes sense when the interaction range is at most L/2. A Lennard-Jones cutoff of 2.5σ requires a box side of at least 5σ; anything smaller and a particle would "see" multiple images of itself, double-counting forces. This is the first of several finite-size artefacts the torus introduces, artefacts the explainer series will keep returning to.

Finite-size effects

Periodic boundaries eliminate one problem and introduce another. They remove reflected particles and surface layers; they install a maximum length scale equal to L/2. Any physical correlation longer than that becomes a simulation artefact: a fluctuation near one wall sees its own images at distance L, so correlation functions at separations approaching L/2 turn anomalous. Near a critical point, where the correlation length diverges, the torus shape dominates; this is the entire subject of finite-size scaling.

The scaling works in reverse, too: by running the same simulation at several box sizes and extrapolating, one recovers the infinite-volume limit. The torus is not an obstacle but a controllable distortion.

Twisted boundary conditions

A more exotic option, ubiquitous in lattice gauge theory: when wrapping a field across the box, multiply it by a phase. A particle passing through the right wall picks up a factor eiθ. Physically this corresponds to threading a magnetic flux through the handle of the torus: the torus has two non-contractible loops, one horizontal and one vertical, and each can carry an independent flux. Varying the phases lets simulations explore different flux sectors without physically modifying the gauge field. Berry phase calculations, superfluid stiffness measurements, and anyonic braiding simulations all live here.

Lattice inside a torus vs lattice inside ℝⁿ

The previous two explainers put a Bravais lattice inside ℝn. Here we did something different: we quotiented ℝn by the lattice. These are dual pictures.

Inside ℝn, the lattice is an infinite discrete set and translation by a lattice vector is a symmetry of the ambient space. Inside 𝕋n, the lattice has collapsed to a single point (every lattice vector is identified with zero), and the only translations are the continuous ones on the quotient. Periodicity is no longer a symmetry to be enforced; it is automatic, built into the topology.

For a simulation, this duality is a choice about where the bookkeeping lives. A lattice Boltzmann code stores fields on ℤn/(NLℤn), a finite discrete torus, and every update rule is implicitly translation-invariant because the index arithmetic is modular. A particle MD code stores continuous coordinates in [0, L)n and applies the minimum-image rule explicitly. Both are simulating the same underlying manifold; they differ only in whether the quotient is built into the data structure or applied at every force evaluation.

Takeaways

  • Glue the edges. Identify (x, 0) with (x, L) and (0, y) with (L, y). The box becomes the flat torus 𝕋² = ℝ²/ℤ². No boundaries, globally flat metric.
  • Quotient construction. Points of ℝn are identified if they differ by an integer translation. The quotient is 𝕋n. The original simulation box is a fundamental domain of the covering ℝn → 𝕋n.
  • Straight lines unfold. A geodesic on the torus lifts to a straight line on ℝn. Rational slope → closed orbit. Irrational slope → dense orbit (Weyl equidistribution).
  • Minimum-image convention. The torus distance between two points is the minimum Euclidean distance over all lattice-translated images. Valid only when interaction range < L/2.
  • Finite-size artefacts. Correlations longer than L/2 become spurious; finite-size scaling is the trade you make for not having walls. Twisted boundary conditions thread flux through the torus handles, used in lattice gauge theory.
