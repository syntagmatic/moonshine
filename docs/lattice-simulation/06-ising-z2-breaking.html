Ising & the Z₂ Break

Part 6 of 6. The Ising Hamiltonian is unchanged when every spin flips, and so is the Monte Carlo rule that samples it. Below the critical temperature the state picks a sign anyway.

Parts 4 and 5 ran deterministic rules on the grid; this part adds thermal noise. Put a spin σ = ±1 on each site and reward neighbours for agreeing, and you have the Ising model (Lenz 1920, Ising 1924). The earlier parts asked which rotations a lattice keeps. The symmetry here is internal, flipping every spin, and the lattice keeps it exactly. The question is how a state can lose a symmetry its rules never break.

Does the update rule keep the symmetry?

A symmetric Hamiltonian does not guarantee a symmetric simulation; an update rule could favour one sign through how it spends random numbers. The test: run a second copy that starts as the exact flip of the first, and feed both the same random numbers. At h = 0 flipping σi costs the same in both, since σi and si both change sign, so every decision matches and the overlap

stays at exactly −1. A field adds 2σih to ΔE, with opposite signs in the two copies.

Finding Tc without Onsager

Most lattice models have no exact solution, and a rounded curve does not say where the transition is. The Binder cumulant does:

It tends to 2/3 in the ordered phase and to 0 in the disordered one. At the critical point the shape of the distribution of M stops depending on size, so curves for different L cross there.

Figure 5. U for L = 8, 16, 32 at eleven temperatures, each from 2,000 Wolff sweeps. The readout interpolates each pair's crossing; the dashed green line is Onsager's Tc. Crossings move by a few hundredths between seeds, most for the smallest pair.

That number is what the lattice sets. The triangular lattice has Tc = 4/ln 3 ≈ 3.64 but the same exponent 1/8, because exponents belong to a universality class fixed by the ℤ₂ symmetry, short-range couplings and two dimensions. Layered magnets such as K2CoF4, whose planes barely interact and whose spins prefer one axis, measure β close to 1/8. Binary alloys like β-brass and the liquid-gas critical point are Ising too, but in the three-dimensional class, with β ≈ 0.326. Weak bond disorder is marginal in 2D by the Harris criterion: the exponents survive with logarithmic corrections.

Why single flips stall near Tc

Metropolis turns a domain over one spin at a time, and near Tc domains fill the box. The cost shows in the autocorrelation ρ(t) of |M| and its integrated time τ = ½ + Σt≥1 ρ(t), roughly the sweeps per independent sample. The Wolff update (1989) grows a cluster by adding each agreeing neighbour with probability 1 − e−2J/kT, the value that makes it satisfy detailed balance, and flips it whole.

Figure 6. ρ(t) of |M| at Tc: Metropolis runs of 20,000, 30,000 and 80,000 sweeps at L = 8, 16, 32, and 4,000 Wolff sweeps at L = 32 (a Wolff sweep is enough clusters to flip N spins). τ is summed until the lag passes 6τ. Equal steps on the log axis mean a power law, τ ∝ Lz; published estimates give z ≈ 2.17 for Metropolis.

Each doubling of L multiplies the Metropolis time by four or five, while Wolff stays under one sweep. That is why Figures 4 and 5 use Wolff.
