Lattice Boltzmann & the D2Q9 Stencil

Part 5 of 6. Replace boolean particle occupation by a continuous distribution fi(x, t) on each of nine lattice velocities. The weights of the D2Q9 stencil are chosen so the square lattice carries an isotropic rank-4 tensor, and Chapman–Enskog then recovers Navier–Stokes, enough to grow a Kármán vortex street in the browser.

Part 4 ended with HPP failing because the four axis velocities of the square lattice cannot build an isotropic rank-4 tensor. That does not rule out square lattices for fluids. McNamara & Zanetti (1988), refined by Qian, d'Humières & Lallemand (1992), showed that the square lattice works once you enlarge the velocity set and drop boolean occupation. The result is lattice Boltzmann.

The method keeps the lattice structure of HPP and FHP (discrete positions, discrete velocities, unit timestep) and changes two things. Particle counts become densities: a continuous fi(x, t) in each velocity channel, the expected number of particles per cell moving along ci. And the exclusion-principle collision rules, which forced HPP and FHP to use booleans, become a linearised relaxation toward local equilibrium. The algorithm that remains is two nested loops over nine floating-point channels with one line of collision arithmetic.

The D2Q9 velocity set

The square lattice has a natural set of nine velocities: the zero vector (a rest population), four axis neighbours at speed 1, and four diagonal neighbours at speed √2, indexed i ∈ {0, 1, …, 8}. In "D2Q9", D is the dimension and Q the number of velocities.

The three speed classes (rest, one vector; axis, four vectors; diagonal, four vectors) are orbits under the point group D4 of the square lattice, which is why they have to share weights inside each class. Three free weights meet the Gauss–Hermite quadrature conditions, and the solution is unique.

On the square lattice, nine is the smallest velocity set whose weighted sums over wi ci are isotropic through rank 4, meaning invariant under the full SO(2) and not only under D4. With fewer vectors the rank-4 moment fails and the viscosity turns anisotropic, as in HPP. Larger sets (D2Q17, D2Q21 and other higher-order models) match more Hermite moments and can simulate compressible or thermal flow. Isothermal incompressible Navier–Stokes needs only nine.

Streaming and collision

Each step first collides at every site, pulling each channel toward a local equilibrium, then streams each channel's population one cell in its own direction.

This is the BGK (Bhatnagar–Gross–Krook) single-relaxation-time collision. fieq is the value each channel would take in local thermodynamic equilibrium at the present density and velocity. τ is a dimensionless relaxation time: near 0.5 relaxation is fast and viscosity low, and larger values give slower relaxation and higher viscosity.

From microscopic to Navier–Stokes

A Chapman–Enskog expansion (take ε to be the Knudsen number, the ratio of the relaxation length to the scale on which the flow varies, expand fi = fieq + ε fi(1) + ε2 fi(2) + …, take moments) recovers the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations. The anisotropy that sank HPP is gone because the D2Q9 weights were chosen to remove it.

Boundary conditions and bounce-back

Walls and obstacles are boolean flags on lattice sites. At a solid site, populations arriving from outside are reflected: the channel pointing into the wall is swapped with the channel pointing out. Each reflection flips ci to −ci, which at O(u) gives zero mean velocity at the wall, so this bounce-back rule approximates a no-slip boundary.

Bounce-back makes awkward geometries easy. A circular cylinder on a Cartesian grid becomes a staircase, and bounce-back applied site by site gives a no-slip surface that is circular to within one lattice spacing. At the moderate Reynolds numbers of a Kármán street, that is enough.

The Kármán street

Flow past a cylinder has one of the best-known regime diagrams in fluid mechanics. Below Re ≈ 5 the flow is attached and laminar. Above it a pair of steady recirculation bubbles forms behind the cylinder. Past Re ≈ 47 the pair becomes unstable to an antisymmetric perturbation, the bubbles detach alternately, and a von Kármán vortex street of alternating clockwise and counter-clockwise vortices sheds downstream. The shedding frequency is set by the Strouhal number St = f D / U, which sits near 0.2 for a wide range of Reynolds numbers.

All of this comes out of the D2Q9 BGK update below, about forty lines of inner-loop arithmetic with no special treatment of vortices and no adaptive mesh.

Reading the simulation

Below Re ≈ 40 the flow settles into a steady symmetric wake, two mirror-image recirculation bubbles with no oscillation. Past 50 the steady wake gives way to an oscillation. This is a Hopf bifurcation: a pair of complex eigenvalues of the linearised flow crosses into the unstable half-plane, and a periodic state replaces the fixed point. The flow's Z2 symmetry, reflection through the centreline, does not simply vanish. No snapshot of the street is mirror-symmetric, but the street is unchanged by reflection combined with a shift of half a shedding period, so the symmetry survives as a space-time symmetry. Lattice-scale numerical noise decides which way the first vortex sheds.

A square obstacle has sharper separation points and sheds at a slightly different frequency in the same Strouhal regime. A wing at zero angle of attack barely separates. A tandem pair produces the wake interference familiar from bridge aerodynamics. The speed render shows what vorticity hides: the velocity deficit behind the obstacle and the faster bypass flow on its flanks.

Everything specific to D2Q9 is in the weights (4/9, 1/9, 1/36). Change them and the rank-4 moment stops being isotropic, giving anisotropic viscosity of the HPP kind. Keep them, and a local update produces Kármán streets, boundary layers and much of low-Mach incompressible flow. The moment conditions on the D4 orbits of the velocity set fix the weights uniquely.

What this method is and is not

LBM is at its best in low-Mach, nearly incompressible flow through complicated geometry, where finite-volume Navier–Stokes solvers struggle with mesh generation. It runs fast on GPUs because each site collides independently and streaming is a shift. It extends to multiphase flow, porous media, microfluidics and non-Newtonian rheology. Commercial codes such as PowerFLOW and XFlow, and research frameworks such as waLBerla, are built on it.

In the D2Q9 form it cannot handle high-Mach compressible flow, which needs a larger velocity set (D2Q17, D2Q37) or a different closure. It also violates Galilean invariance at finite u: the equilibrium is Taylor-exact only to O(u²), and the truncation appears as a small Mach-dependent anisotropy that vanishes as u → 0. Below Ma ≈ 0.3, where most engineering flows sit, this is invisible.
