Life & Symmetry Attractors

Part 6 of 15. Conway's update rule is isotropic on the Moore neighbourhood, so the point group D4 of the square lattice commutes with time evolution. A stabiliser subgroup can only grow, never shrink, so symmetry is an attractor of the Life dynamics.

A cellular automaton is a lattice simulation in its starkest form: a grid of cells, a discrete clock, and a local update rule. Conway's Game of Life, published in Scientific American in 1970, lives on the integer lattice ℤ². Each cell has a state in {0, 1} (dead or alive), and the whole configuration advances one tick by applying the same arithmetic rule to every cell in parallel, looking only at the cell itself and its eight Moore neighbours.

The rule is famously short. A dead cell with exactly three live neighbours is born; a live cell with two or three live neighbours survives; everything else dies. The reason Life has stayed interesting for fifty-plus years is that this trivial-looking rule is Turing-complete: any computation a laptop can do, a large enough Life board can do as well.

What interests us here is not Turing completeness but the relationship between the rule and the lattice it lives on. The Moore neighbourhood is invariant under every element of the square lattice's point group D4: the eight symmetries of a square, generated by a 90° rotation and a mirror. And Life's rule only looks at the count of live neighbours, not at their positions. Counts are invariant under relabelling, so the rule itself is D4-invariant. That invariance has a consequence the simulator will make visible: symmetry is an evolutionary attractor in the state space of Life.

Equivariance under the square's point group

The point group of ℤ² is D4, the dihedral group of order 8: identity, three non-trivial rotations by 90°, 180°, 270°, and four mirrors (horizontal, vertical, and the two diagonals). Any element g ∈ D4 permutes the lattice sites and, in doing so, permutes the eight Moore-neighbours of any cell among themselves. The count Ni,j is therefore unchanged when we replace the whole configuration x by its transformed version g·x.

In words: rotating or reflecting the grid and then applying the rule gives the same result as applying the rule first and then rotating or reflecting. The rule commutes with the symmetry. A function with this property is called equivariant under the group. (If the group acts trivially on the output, the weaker property f(g·x) = f(x) is called invariance. Life's rule is the stronger, equivariant kind.)

Why this is the right form of the law. The lattice imposes a finite symmetry group on everything that lives on it. If the rule we write down ignores that symmetry (say, by treating the east neighbour differently from the west) the simulation will develop macroscopic anisotropy: particles will drift, waves will refract, statistics will differ along different axes. Equivariance is the minimum discipline that keeps the lattice's symmetry honest. Conway's Life has it for free, because the rule is a function of a count.

Stabilisers can only grow

The stabiliser of a configuration x is the subgroup of D4 that fixes it: Gx = {g ∈ D4 : g·x = x}. A configuration with a vertical mirror line has a D1 stabiliser (identity plus that one mirror). A pulsar, which repeats with period 3 and has a central 4-fold rotation, has stabiliser C4 at phases where no mirror is present and D4 at the symmetric phase.

Equivariance forces the stabiliser of f(x) to contain the stabiliser of x. If g·x = x, apply f to both sides and use equivariance on the left: g·f(x) = f(g·x) = f(x). So every element that stabilised x also stabilises f(x). Unrolling in time:

Symmetry can only be gained, never spontaneously lost. The rule can discover a mirror that the previous tick did not have (a C1 configuration can evolve into a D2 one, a symmetry that emerges by accident as the population shrinks), but once a mirror is there, it stays for every subsequent generation unless an external perturbation destroys it. In the language of dynamical systems, the set of configurations with stabiliser at least H is a forward-invariant subset for every subgroup H ⊆ D4.

And the orbit-stabiliser theorem is not just counting: because Life's rule is equivariant, the eight panels g·x all evolve in lockstep; applying one Life step to g·x gives g·f(x). An attractor census

If we sample a thousand random seeds at density 0.375 and run each for 300 generations, most have settled into a "debris field" of still-lifes (blocks, beehives, loaves, boats, ships) and oscillators (blinkers, beacons, toads, pulsars). The stabiliser distribution of the endpoint, aggregated over all the surviving live cells, is heavily weighted toward small groups; almost all debris is built out of asymmetric still-lifes (C1) and period-2 oscillators whose symmetric phase lifts them to D2. A random seed almost never evolves into a pulsar; the pulsar's basin of attraction in state space is vanishingly small. But once a pulsar is planted by hand, the equivariance argument guarantees that no subsequent Life step can destroy its C4 rotation; only an outside intervention can.

This is the qualitative shape of every lattice-based simulation. The lattice fixes a finite point group. The rule, if it respects the lattice, is equivariant under that group. Equivariance then makes the stabiliser of a configuration a monotone quantity in the absence of perturbations. Coarse-graining over long times, the typical (random) initial condition ends up with a small stabiliser (symmetry is rare), but the dynamics preserve whatever symmetry happens to be present. Hence the phrase "symmetry attractor": not "trajectories rush toward symmetry" (they do not, in a strict-measure sense), but "the subset of symmetric configurations is forward-invariant".

The attractor zoo, by stabilizer

Every Life attractor has a stabilizer subgroup of D4, and D4 has exactly ten subgroups. Cataloguing the zoo by stabilizer replaces the usual still-life / oscillator / spaceship breakdown with a group-theoretic one: the subgroup lattice below is the shelf on which each pattern files itself.

Time-averaged stabilizers matter too. A blinker viewed frame-by-frame is a line that rotates 90° every tick, so its still-frame stabilizer is only D1; but its period-2 orbit is fixed by both axial mirrors, so over time it carries D2. The chiral attractors (glider, R-pentomino) have trivial stabilizer and so come in orbits of 8 under D4.

What breaks this picture

Three kinds of thing can make the stabiliser drop. First, the board is finite, so a pattern that drifts (glider, lightweight spaceship) can have its bounding box touch the edge of the universe and be chopped; finite-board effects break equivariance trivially. We use periodic boundary conditions to avoid this, which makes the stabiliser analysis exact. Second, explicit external perturbation (the glider-hit button) adds mass to the configuration that was not there before, and the new combined pattern has whatever (usually trivial) stabiliser its new shape has. Third, randomised rules (noisy Life, stochastic cellular automata) give a probability distribution over next configurations, and the equivariance argument still holds in distribution, but individual realisations can drift.

The takeaway for the rest of this series: a lattice rule that is faithful to the lattice's point group protects every non-trivial configurational symmetry against spontaneous breaking. HPP and FHP in the next explainer will make the same structural observation for a lattice gas rather than a lattice automaton; the failure mode when the rule's symmetries do not match the continuum physics is exactly what the square-lattice HPP model demonstrates.

Takeaways

  • Life's rule is D4-equivariant. Rotating or reflecting the board and then updating is identical to updating and then rotating or reflecting. The rule depends only on the live-neighbour count, which is manifestly rotation- and reflection-invariant.
  • Stabilisers are monotone non-decreasing. If Gx is the subgroup fixing configuration x, then Gx ⊆ Gf(x). Symmetry can be gained by accident but cannot be lost without an external perturbation.
  • Attractor census. Random seeds at density ≈ 0.375 almost all land in low-symmetry debris (C1 still-lifes and D2-phase period-2 oscillators). The pulsar's C4 basin is measure-zero in practice, but once you are in it you stay.
  • Equivariance is the right discipline for discretisation. A rule that breaks lattice symmetry injects anisotropy into the emergent dynamics. Conway's rule has equivariance for free because it is a function of a count. Most physically motivated rules need to earn it.
  • The symmetry detector is a D4 scan. Eight bitmap comparisons per frame, bounding-box normalised so translating shapes are detected by their intrinsic symmetry rather than their position.
