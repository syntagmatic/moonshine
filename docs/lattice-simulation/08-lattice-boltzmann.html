Lattice Boltzmann & the D2Q9 Stencil

Part 8 of 15. Replace boolean particle occupation by a continuous distribution fi(x, t) on each of nine lattice velocities. Stream, collide, repeat. The weights of the D2Q9 stencil are chosen so the point group of the square lattice carries a rank-4 isotropic tensor, and Chapman–Enskog reconstructs Navier–Stokes. The payoff is a Kármán vortex street forming live in the browser.

The previous explainer ended with a diagnosis: HPP fails because the square lattice's point group D4 cannot build an isotropic rank-4 tensor from the axis-neighbour set alone. FHP succeeds on a hexagonal lattice because D6 can. One possible reading is "square lattices are broken for fluids". A better reading, due to McNamara & Zanetti in 1988 and refined by Qian, d'Humières & Lallemand in 1992, is that the square lattice is fine once you enlarge the velocity set and relax the boolean-occupation rule. Enter lattice Boltzmann.

The method keeps the lattice structure of HPP and FHP (discrete positions, discrete velocities, unit timestep) but throws out two things. First, particle counts are replaced by densities: a continuous fi(x, t) in each velocity channel, interpretable as the expected number of particles per cell moving along ci. Second, the exclusion-principle collision rules (which were what forced HPP/FHP to use booleans) are replaced by a linearised relaxation toward local equilibrium. What is left is a remarkably compact algorithm: two nested loops, nine floating-point streams, one line of collision arithmetic.

The D2Q9 velocity set

On a square lattice in two dimensions there is a natural choice of nine discrete velocities: the zero vector (a rest population that does not move), four axis neighbours at speed 1, and four diagonal neighbours at speed √2. Label them with an index i ∈ {0, 1, …, 8}. The "D2Q9" name is standard: D stands for dimension (2) and Q for the number of velocities (9).

The three speed classes (rest, one vector; axis, four vectors; diagonal, four vectors) are orbits under the point group D4 of the square lattice, which is why they have to share weights inside each class. There are exactly three free parameters and exactly three Gauss–Hermite quadrature conditions to match, so the weights are unique up to normalisation.

Why exactly nine. The smallest 2D velocity set that makes every rank-k ≤ 4 sum over wi ci isotropic (invariant under the full SO(2), not just the point group D4) is nine. Fewer vectors and the rank-4 moment fails; isotropy dies and you get HPP-style anisotropic viscosity. More vectors (D2Q17, D2Q21, higher-order models) recover higher-order Hermite moments and let you simulate compressible or thermal flow, but for isothermal incompressible Navier–Stokes, nine is enough.

Streaming and collision

The evolution of fi is a split step. First collide at every site, pulling each channel toward a local equilibrium. Then stream: each channel's population hops one cell in its own direction.

The collision operator above is the BGK (Bhatnagar–Gross–Krook) single-relaxation-time approximation. fieq is the local equilibrium distribution: the value each channel would take if the fluid were in local thermodynamic equilibrium at the present density and velocity. τ is a dimensionless relaxation time. Closer to 0.5 means fast relaxation and low viscosity. Larger means slow relaxation and high viscosity.

From microscopic to Navier–Stokes

A Chapman–Enskog expansion (treat ε = 1/τ, expand fi = fieq + ε fi(1) + ε2 fi(2) + …, take moments) recovers the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations. The kinematic viscosity falls out of the first-order correction:

and the pressure is determined by the equation of state p = cs2 ρ. This is the payoff. A local, nearest-neighbour update on a regular lattice with a handful of floating-point multiplies per cell is equivalent, at macroscopic scales, to an incompressible Navier–Stokes solver. The anisotropy that killed HPP is gone because the D2Q9 weights were chosen to kill it.

Boundary conditions and bounce-back

Walls and obstacles are encoded as boolean flags on lattice sites. At any site flagged as solid, the populations that would have streamed in from outside are instead reflected: the channel pointing into the wall gets swapped with the channel pointing out. This is the bounce-back rule, and it approximates a no-slip boundary. Each wall reflection flips ci to −ci, which at O(u) gives zero mean velocity at the wall: the no-slip condition.

Bounce-back makes LBM comfortable with awkward geometries. A circular cylinder on a Cartesian grid is a staircase approximation; bounce-back applied site-by-site gives a no-slip surface that is approximately circular, accurate to one lattice spacing. For the Kármán-street regime at moderate Reynolds number, this is already enough.

The Kármán street

Flow past a cylinder exhibits one of the most famous regime diagrams in fluid mechanics. At very low Reynolds number (Re ≲ 5) the flow is attached and laminar. Past Re ≈ 5 a pair of steady recirculation bubbles appears behind the cylinder. Past Re ≈ 47 that pair becomes unstable to an antisymmetric perturbation: the two bubbles start alternately detaching, and a von Kármán vortex street (alternating clockwise and counter-clockwise vortices) sheds downstream. The shedding frequency is set by the Strouhal number St = f D / U, which sits near 0.2 for a wide range of Reynolds numbers.

All of this falls out of the D2Q9 BGK update below. No special treatment of vortices. No adaptive mesh. The simulator is about forty lines of inner-loop arithmetic.

Reading the simulation

Three things to try.

Sweep the Reynolds number. Below Re ≈ 40 the flow settles into a steady symmetric wake: two mirror-image recirculation bubbles and no oscillation. Bump Re past 50 and the wake loses its reflection symmetry. The simulation goes through a pitchfork bifurcation: the Z2 symmetry of the flow (reflection through the centreline) spontaneously breaks. Which way the first vortex sheds is set by lattice-scale numerical noise.

Change the obstacle. A square obstacle has sharper separation points and sheds at a slightly different frequency but the same Strouhal regime. A wing-shaped obstacle at zero angle of attack barely separates at all. A tandem pair creates wake-interference patterns familiar from bridge aerodynamics.

Toggle the render mode. Vorticity (curl of velocity) highlights the vortex cores as sharp alternating signs; speed magnitude highlights the velocity deficit behind the obstacle and the accelerated bypass flow on the flanks. They tell you different things about the same field.

What the stencil buys you. The entire non-trivial content of the D2Q9 method is in the weights (4/9, 1/9, 1/36). Change them and the rank-4 moment stops being isotropic; you get anisotropic viscosity of exactly the HPP flavour. Keep them, and a trivial-looking local update produces Kármán streets, boundary layers, drag crises, and the whole zoo of low-Mach incompressible flow. Representation theory of D4 on the orbit structure of the velocity set picks the weights uniquely; physics does the rest.

What this method is and is not

LBM is at its best in low-Mach, weakly compressible, incompressible-like regimes with complicated geometry, exactly where finite-volume Navier–Stokes solvers struggle with mesh generation. It runs fast on GPUs because every site's update is independent of every other site's during the collision step, and the streaming step is a shift. It extends cleanly to multiphase flow, porous media, microfluidics, and non-Newtonian rheology. Modern industrial CFD tools (PowerFLOW, XFlow, waLBerla) are all lattice-Boltzmann.

It is not a high-Mach compressible solver, at least not in the D2Q9 form. For that you need a larger velocity set (D2Q17, D2Q37) that captures more Hermite moments, or a different closure entirely. It is also subject to Galilean-invariance violations at finite u; the equilibrium is only Taylor-exact to O(u²), and the truncation shows up as a small Mach-dependent anisotropy that vanishes as u → 0. For most engineering flows below Ma ≈ 0.3 this is invisible.

Takeaways

  • D2Q9 = 9 velocities on the square lattice. Rest, four axis neighbours (speed 1), four diagonal neighbours (speed √2). Weights 4/9, 1/9, 1/36 chosen so every rank-k ≤ 4 moment over wi ci is isotropic under SO(2).
  • Stream then collide. Stream: fi(x + ci, t + 1) = fi*(x, t). Collide (BGK): fi* = fi − (1/τ)(fi − fieq). Two nested loops. Embarrassingly parallel.
  • Equilibrium is Taylor-Maxwell. fieq = wi ρ [1 + 3(ci·u) + (9/2)(ci·u)2 − (3/2) |u|2]. Second-order Taylor expansion of Maxwell–Boltzmann.
  • Viscosity comes from τ. ν = cs2 (τ − 1/2) with cs2 = 1/3. The Chapman–Enskog expansion recovers incompressible Navier–Stokes with pressure p = cs2 ρ.
  • Bounce-back for walls. Swap opposite channels at solid sites. No-slip to leading order; works on staircase approximations of curved boundaries.
  • Kármán streets for free. Above Re ≈ 47 a Z2 symmetry spontaneously breaks and the wake oscillates at St ≈ 0.2. LBM reproduces this with no special treatment.
  • Point-group symmetry picks the weights. The orbit decomposition of the velocity set under D4 forces equal weights inside each orbit; Gauss–Hermite quadrature fixes the three free numbers. Group theory in, fluid dynamics out.
