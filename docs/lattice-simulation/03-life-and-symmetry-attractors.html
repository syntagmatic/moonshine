Life & Symmetry Attractors

Part 3 of 6. Conway's rule counts live neighbours without caring which is which, so it commutes with the eight symmetries of the square. Whatever symmetry a Life board has, it keeps: the stabiliser of a configuration can grow as the board evolves, but it cannot shrink.

A cellular automaton is the barest lattice simulation: a grid of cells, a discrete clock and a local update rule. Conway's Game of Life, published in Scientific American in 1970, lives on the integer lattice ℤ². Each cell is dead or alive, and every cell updates in parallel from its own state and those of its eight Moore neighbours. A dead cell with exactly three live neighbours is born, a live cell with two or three survives, and everything else dies. The rule is Turing-complete: a large enough Life board can do any computation a laptop can.

This article is about how that rule relates to its lattice. The Moore neighbourhood is carried to itself by every element of the square lattice's point group D4, the eight symmetries of a square, generated by a 90° rotation and a mirror (Part 1 derived it). Life's rule only looks at the count of live neighbours, and a count does not change when the neighbours are relabelled, so the rule is D4-invariant. The simulator below makes one consequence visible: a symmetric board stays symmetric, and a board can pick up symmetry it did not start with.

Equivariance under the square's point group

The point group of ℤ² is D4, the dihedral group of order 8: identity, three non-trivial rotations by 90°, 180°, 270°, and four mirrors (horizontal, vertical, and the two diagonals). Any element g ∈ D4 permutes the lattice sites and, in doing so, permutes the eight Moore-neighbours of any cell among themselves. The count Ni,j is therefore unchanged when we replace the whole configuration x by its transformed version g·x.

Rotating or reflecting the grid and then applying the rule gives the same result as applying the rule and then rotating or reflecting. The rule commutes with the symmetry. A function with this property is called equivariant under the group. (If the group acts trivially on the output, the weaker property f(g·x) = f(x) is called invariance. Life's rule is the stronger, equivariant kind.)

A rule that treats the east neighbour differently from the west breaks the lattice's symmetry, and the simulation picks up a preferred direction at large scales. Equivariance is the least a rule needs to keep the lattice's symmetry. Life has it for free, because its rule is a function of a count.

Stabilisers can only grow

The stabiliser of a configuration x is the subgroup of D4 that fixes it: Gx = {g ∈ D4 : g·x = x}. A configuration with a vertical mirror line has a D1 stabiliser (identity plus that one mirror). A block, a 2×2 square, has all of D4. So does every phase of the pulsar, a period-3 oscillator; the argument below shows it could not be otherwise.

Equivariance forces the stabiliser of f(x) to contain the stabiliser of x. If g·x = x, apply f to both sides and use equivariance on the left: g·f(x) = f(g·x) = f(x). So every element that stabilised x also stabilises f(x). Unrolling in time:

Symmetry can be gained but never lost on its own. A C1 configuration can evolve into a D2 one by accident as the population shrinks, but once a mirror is there it stays unless something outside the rule destroys it. In the language of dynamical systems, the set of configurations with stabiliser at least H is a forward-invariant subset for every subgroup H ⊆ D4. The pulsar's first phase has all eight symmetries, so its second and third phases must have them too.

On a real board a pattern's mirror line rarely passes through the centre of the board, so the detector below asks a looser question: is g·x equal to x up to a translation? The same argument goes through, because Life also commutes with translations. If g·x = τ(x) for a shift τ, then g·f(x) = f(τ(x)) = τ(f(x)), so this stabiliser can only grow as well.

Symmetry of an oscillator

A blinker is a row of three cells that turns into a column of three and back. Each frame has two mirrors and a half turn, the axis-aligned D2, a subgroup of order 4, so its orbit in Figure 2 has 8 / 4 = 2 distinct panels: the row and the column. The quarter turn is missing from each frame, but it carries the row to the column, which is the next frame, so r·x = f(x). The blinker's two-frame cycle, taken as a set, is fixed by all of D4 even though neither frame is. The pulsar is the other extreme: it has all of D4 in each of its three phases, and the Pulsar preset collapses Figure 2 to a single panel at every step.

A census of the debris

A random soup starts with no symmetry, so the theorem says nothing about where it ends up. The census below measures that. It runs many random 32×32 soups on the same torus with the same rule for 600 generations, splits each final board into connected objects (live cells touching, diagonals included), and runs the same D4 test as the badge on every object and on the whole board.

Figure 3. Stabilisers of the objects left after 600 generations of random soups at density 0.38, as a share of all objects, with the most common shapes in each row and how often they occurred. Conjugate subgroups are merged (a horizontal mirror and a vertical one differ only by a quarter turn); the two D2's, with mirrors on the axes or on the diagonals, are not conjugate and are kept apart. An object is whatever is 8-connected at the moment the run stops, so two objects that happen to touch count as one, and a glider still flying counts as C1. Each run draws fresh soups, so the numbers move a little between runs.

The objects are mostly symmetric. In runs of 120 soups, about three objects in four have a stabiliser of order 4 or 8, most of them blocks (D4), blinkers and beehives (both the axis-aligned D2). Boats and loaves supply most of the single diagonal mirrors, and only about one object in eight has no symmetry at all. The board as a whole is another matter: in almost every soup the final configuration, taken up to translation, is fixed by the identity alone, because a few dozen symmetric objects scattered at random positions do not line up about a common centre. None of this contradicts the theorem, which is about one board's history. A soup that starts with trivial stabiliser has nothing to lose, and the census shows it almost never gains anything either.

What breaks this picture

Three things can make the stabiliser drop. The first is a finite board: with hard walls, a glider or spaceship eventually hits the edge and is chopped, and the edge breaks equivariance. We use periodic boundary conditions, where every element of D4 about the centre of the square torus maps the board to itself, so the argument is exact; the detector itself can under-report when live cells occupy every row or column of the torus, because its bounding box then has nowhere to start. The second is outside interference: the Drop glider button adds cells the rule did not put there, and the new pattern has whatever stabiliser its shape has, usually the trivial one. The third is a randomised rule, such as noisy Life, which gives a probability distribution over next configurations. The equivariance argument still holds for that distribution, but individual realisations can lose symmetry.

The same equivariance applies to any lattice rule that treats neighbours symmetrically, including lattice gases, where the state is a set of particles moving between sites. There, a second question matters as well: whether the lattice's point group is large enough for the gas to behave like an isotropic fluid once it is coarse-grained. Part 4 shows that the square-lattice HPP gas is not, and the hexagonal FHP gas is.
