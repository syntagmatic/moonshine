HPP vs FHP: Why Hexagons

Part 7 of 15. Hardy, Pomeau and de Pazzis put a lattice gas on a square grid in 1973 and got a fluid that drags along its own axes. Thirteen years later Frisch, Hasslacher and Pomeau moved to a hexagonal grid and got Navier–Stokes. The gap between them is a single tensor identity.

A lattice gas is almost the simplest possible model of a fluid. Particles sit on the sites of a regular lattice. Each particle carries one of a small set of discrete velocities — one for every edge leaving the site. Every time step has two phases. Streaming: each particle hops along its velocity to a neighbouring site. Collision: at each site, the incoming particles scatter off each other according to a local rule that conserves mass and momentum. That is all. No forces, no floats, no stencils; just a Boolean table that says which links are occupied.

The obvious first question is whether this toy recovers real fluid dynamics in the limit where you average over many sites and many steps. A Chapman–Enskog expansion takes the Boolean microdynamics, defines coarse-grained density and momentum fields, and asks what partial differential equation those fields obey. For the lattice gas to recover the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations, the convective term has to come out right. And the convective term, we shall see, pivots on a single rank-4 tensor built from the lattice's velocity set. On a square lattice that tensor is anisotropic. On a hexagonal lattice it is isotropic. That is the whole story — told here in simulators, diagrams, and one group-theoretic line.

The two lattices and their rules

HPP uses a square lattice with four velocity directions (±x, ±y) — the nearest-neighbour edges of ℤ². FHP uses a triangular (hexagonal-symmetric) lattice with six velocity directions at 60° apart. A Boolean occupation variable says whether each directed link at each site is occupied; the Pauli-style exclusion principle forbids two particles with the same velocity at the same site.

HPP collisionssquare, 4 velocities
Head-on pair along x becomes head-on pair along y (and vice versa). All other configurations stream through unchanged. Mass (4) and momentum (0) conserved; kinetic energy also conserved because all velocities have the same speed.
FHP collisionshex, 6 velocities
Head-on pair rotates by +60° or −60° (chosen randomly); three particles symmetric under 120° rotation reverse all three. Mass and momentum conserved; the extra collision channels make the macroscopic viscosity finite and tunable.

Both rules are easy to implement as a lookup table on the set of possible incoming configurations at a site. Both conserve exactly what they should conserve. The difference is not in the rules but in the lattice geometry they live on.

The tensor on each lattice

Sum ca,ica,jca,kca,l over the four HPP velocities (±1, 0), (0, ±1). The sum is zero unless each index pairs up — so the only non-zero components are those of the form Tiijj or Tijij with i = j. It works out to 2δijδjkδkl: the tensor is non-zero only when all four indices are equal. That is the Kronecker "diagonal" term. No combination of δδ pairings reproduces it unless i=j=k=l, so the HPP tensor is not of the isotropic form.

Sum the same quantity over the six FHP velocities ca = (cos πa/3, sin πa/3) for a = 0, …, 5. The cross terms average away by the 6-fold symmetry, leaving exactly the two-parameter isotropic form with A = 3/4 and B = 3/4. No anomalous diagonal term. The sum is fully rotationally invariant.

Figure 1. The rank-4 fourth-moment tensor of each lattice's velocity set, flattened to a 16×16 "supermatrix" of index pairs (ij, kl). Colour intensity is the tensor component's magnitude. HPP carries a diagonal spike in the four entries where i=j=k=l. FHP does not: its pattern depends only on which δ pairings are formed, the hallmark of an isotropic tensor.

The group-theoretic statement. The point group of the square lattice, C4v (or D4), has a two-dimensional invariant subspace of rank-4 symmetric tensors. One basis vector is the "universally isotropic" δδ-combination. The other is the offending all-equal-indices tensor, which is invariant under D4 but not under the full rotation group O(2). The point group of the hexagonal lattice, D6, only has the first of these: the six 60° directions average the extra term away. The thirteen-year gap between HPP (1973) and FHP (1986) closes this one dimension.

Square lattices are not wrong. They just can't do fluid dynamics. A square lattice is a perfectly good setting for the Ising model, Conway's Life, a lattice gauge theory, or almost anything where rotational invariance is not required. HPP works exactly as designed: it conserves mass and momentum, and its long-wavelength limit is some hydrodynamic equation. It just is not the Navier–Stokes equation. The broken symmetry is built into the fourth-moment tensor of the velocity set, not into any explicit coefficient that could be tuned away.

Why the extra term on the square lattice is not removable

One naive fix is to add diagonal velocities to HPP: eight directions instead of four. That is the geometry of lattice Boltzmann's D2Q9 stencil, which the next explainer covers in detail. It works — but not because it enlarges the rotation group (the point group is still D4); it works because the two shells of velocities (length 1 and length √2) provide enough independent weights that you can solve a system of moment constraints and make the fourth-moment tensor isotropic to the required order. You buy isotropy with weights, not with symmetry. FHP gets it for free because its six directions at 60° apart form a single orbit of D6, and the sum over any orbit of a group containing a rotation of order > 4 is automatically isotropic to fourth order.

The cleanest way to see this is through representation theory. The symmetric rank-4 tensors in 2D form a 5-dimensional vector space (the number of ways to put 4 indistinguishable indices into 2 slots). The trivial representation of D4 decomposes that space into a 2-dimensional invariant subspace — there are two independent D4-invariant symmetric rank-4 tensors. The trivial representation of D6 decomposes it into a 1-dimensional invariant subspace — up to scaling, there is only one D6-invariant symmetric rank-4 tensor, and that one is necessarily the isotropic one. The presence of extra invariants under D4 is precisely what produces anisotropy; the absence of them under D6 is what gives isotropy.

The FHP moment. "We finally understood," Frisch, Hasslacher and Pomeau wrote in 1986, "that the difficulty was not that lattice gases cannot describe fluids — it was that the square lattice cannot." The realisation arrived in a conversation at Los Alamos; the first hex-lattice simulation ran within weeks. The six-fold symmetry of the hex lattice does, in a single geometric stroke, what thirteen years of tuning HPP had failed to do.

What the simulator is actually doing

To keep this pedagogical, both simulators above use simplified collision tables and a coarse grid. HPP uses the canonical two-body head-on rule. FHP implements the two-body and three-body rules of the original FHP-I model, with a random ±60° rotation chosen per collision. Streaming is a single-step hop along each occupied link; a reflective boundary on the top and bottom keeps the stream in the box, and particles flow out the far end. Velocity arrows are averaged over non-overlapping bins that cover the whole box; anisotropy is measured as the root-mean-square angular deviation from the injected direction, averaged over cells where the density is above a threshold. The numbers are not quantitative — they are there to make the qualitative story visible. The lesson is stable across much more careful implementations.

Takeaways

  • A lattice gas is a Boolean flow. Occupation bits on directed links, local collision rules that conserve mass and momentum, synchronous streaming. That is the whole model.
  • HPP (1973) is on a square lattice, FHP (1986) is on a hexagonal lattice. Four vs six velocity directions.
  • Chapman–Enskog requires an isotropic rank-4 tensor. The fourth moment of the velocity set appears in the convective term of the macroscopic momentum equation. Navier–Stokes demands that this tensor be of the form Aδijδkl + B(δikδjl + δilδjk).
  • D4 has a 2-dim space of invariants at rank 4; D6 has a 1-dim space. The square lattice admits a second, non-isotropic D4-invariant tensor. The hexagonal lattice does not.
  • The failure is visible. On HPP, a diagonally-injected stream bends toward the lattice axes; on FHP it travels where it was aimed.
  • The fix was not computational. It was the recognition that point-group choice is a precondition on the physics a simulation can represent, not an implementation detail.
