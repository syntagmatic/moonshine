The Cayley Plane

In standard geometry, you can build a projective space of any dimension using reals or complexes. A 3D space, a 100D space, or anything in between. But octonions are different. Because they are non-associative, the concept of dimension for a projective space stops at two. You can build an octonionic line () and an octonionic plane — the Cayley plane (). Why not? To define a 3D projective space, you need matrices that act on 3-vectors. Matrix multiplication requires associativity. Since the octonions lack this, the 16-dimensional Cayley plane is the absolute end of the projective line. Its symmetry group is the 52-dimensional exceptional Lie group .

Octonions in Physics

Superstring theory requires the universe to have 10 spacetime dimensions: 2 longitudinal (time plus the string direction) and 8 transverse. The number 8 is not arbitrary. The mathematics of consistent superstring quantization demands a normed division algebra in the transverse dimensions, and the only 8-dimensional normed division algebra is the octonions. The triality symmetry of — unique to dimension 8 — is what makes the worldsheet theory anomaly-free.

At lower energies, the Standard Model's gauge group SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1) has long been suspected of having octonionic origins. The complex octonions naturally split into structures that mirror quark color (SU(3)) and electroweak symmetry (SU(2)×U(1)). Several research programs — notably those of Furey, Boyle, and Dixon — attempt to derive the full Standard Model spectrum from octonionic geometry.

Whether these connections are coincidences or clues remains an open question. What is certain is that the octonions sit precisely where the deepest structures of physics require an 8-dimensional algebra, and no other algebra can fill that role.
