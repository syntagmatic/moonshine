The Fano Plane

Part 4 of 15. The Octonions have seven imaginary units and you never need to memorize their product table. You read it off a geometric circuit — seven points, seven lines — and every product is a single line of that circuit.

In Part 3 we reached the eighth dimension with the Cayley-Dickson recipe. The result has one real part (e0 = 1) and seven imaginary parts e1, …, e7. That leaves 7 × 7 = 49 products among the imaginaries to pin down. A table would be ugly and hide all the symmetry. The Fano plane — the smallest projective plane, with seven points and seven lines — holds the entire multiplication pattern in a single picture.

Three rules, and that is all.

  1. Find the line. Every two distinct imaginaries ei, ej lie on exactly one line of the plane. (Every line is a triple of units, and every line is a copy of the quaternions.)
  2. Follow the arrow. Each line carries a cyclic orientation. Going with the arrow gives a positive result: ei · ej = +ek. Going against flips the sign: ej · ei = −ek.
  3. Squares. Every imaginary unit squares to minus one: ei · ei = −1. (The plane doesn't show this — it's the same rule as the quaternions.)

Figure 1. The Fano plane. Seven imaginary units sit at the seven points; seven lines (three sides, three altitudes, and one inner circle) carry the multiplication rules. Every line is a cyclic quaternion triple (ei, ej, ek) with ei · ej = ek, ej · ek = ei, and ek · ei = ej. Reversing any of those flips the sign.

The Fano plane isn't just a mnemonic — it is a statement about symmetry. Every one of the seven imaginary directions plays the same role; every one of the seven lines is a copy of the quaternions; every one of the 7 · 6 = 42 ordered imaginary pairs lives on exactly one line, with a sign determined by the cycle. No direction is privileged. In Part 5 we'll multiply three units together and watch this clean story break down — the moment where parentheses finally matter and associativity gives out.
