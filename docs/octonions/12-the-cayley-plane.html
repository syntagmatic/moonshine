The Cayley Plane

Part 12 of 15. Standard projective geometry works in any dimension. But for Octonions, the math of points and lines reaches a final, 16-dimensional peak.

In standard geometry, you can build a "projective space" of any dimension using Reals or Complexes. You can have a 3D space, a 100D space, or anything in between. But Octonions are different. Because they are non-associative, the concept of "dimension" for a projective space stops at two. You can build an Octonionic Line ($S^8$) and an Octonionic Plane—the **Cayley Plane** ($OP^2$). But you cannot build an $OP^3$.

Mission: The 16D Intersection

The Cayley Plane is 16-dimensional. In this world, "lines" are 8-dimensional spheres. Any two of these 8D lines must intersect at exactly one point. Your goal is to "sweep" an 8D line across the plane to find the unique point where it meets another.

The Final Projective World: Why can't we build $OP^3$? Because to define a 3D projective space, you need matrices that act on 3-vectors. Matrix multiplication $(AB)v$ requires associativity. Since the Octonions lack this, the 16-dimensional Cayley Plane is the absolute end of the projective line.

In Part 13, we see how physicists tried to bypass this "associative wall" by reinventing matrix multiplication itself, creating the **Albert Algebra**.
