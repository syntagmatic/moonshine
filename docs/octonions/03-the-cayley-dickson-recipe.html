The Cayley-Dickson Recipe

Part 3 of 15. Every algebra can be doubled to create a new one. But the recipe has a hidden tax: every leap costs a fundamental geometric property.

How do you invent a new kind of number? You don't have to guess. Mathematicians use a recursive "machine" called the Cayley-Dickson construction. It takes an existing algebra and doubles its dimensions.

The logic is simple: if you have two numbers $(a, b)$ from your current system, you can treat them as a single number in a higher system. But to make multiplication work, you must follow a very specific rule.

The Price of Progress

As you level up the machine, you will notice that certain "laws of math" start to fail. This is the Property Loss Checklist. Every doubling adds complexity but subtracts order.

✓ Ordered (Can say a < b)
✓ Commutative (ab = ba)
✓ Associative ((ab)c = a(bc))
✓ Normed (|ab| = |a||b|)
