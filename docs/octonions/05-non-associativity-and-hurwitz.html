Non-Associativity and Hurwitz's Theorem

Part 5 of 8. The associative cliff: in 8 dimensions, (ab)c no longer equals a(bc). And Hurwitz proved this is as far as we can go — beyond 8D, multiplication can't even preserve distance.

In almost every number system we use, parentheses are invisible. Whether you compute $(1+2)+3$ or $1+(2+3)$, the answer is 6. This is associativity, and it holds for Reals, Complexes, and Quaternions. But at the edge of the Octonions, this fundamental law breaks.

This failure is why Octonions are so much more difficult to work with than Quaternions. You cannot represent them as standard matrices of real numbers, because matrix multiplication $(AB)C$ is always equal to $A(BC)$. Octonions refuse to be contained by the tools of linear algebra.

The Associator: Mathematicians measure this failure with the associator: $[a, b, c] = (ab)c - a(bc)$. In 8D, this vector is often non-zero, acting as a "geometric friction" that changes your result depending on how you group your steps.

Mission: The Associator Hunt

Octonions aren't always non-associative. If you pick units from the same Fano line, they behave perfectly. Your goal is to find a triple of imaginary units $(a, b, c)$ that does associate, and then find one that diverges into opposite directions.

The Diassociative Safety Net

Any two Octonions generate a copy of the Quaternions inside the 8D space -- a property called diassociativity. As long as you only use two different units, you can move parentheses however you like. You only fall off the associative cliff when you introduce a third direction that isn't on the same Fano line. Non-associativity is the interaction of three independent worlds.

Where the Doubling Line Stops

The Cayley-Dickson doubling machine built Reals, Complexes, Quaternions, and Octonions. It seems like it could go on forever. But in 1898, Adolf Hurwitz proved that we have already hit the limit. The Octonions are the last algebra where multiplication preserves the norm ($|ab| = |a||b|$).

When you double to 16 dimensions (the Sedenions), the link between multiplication and distance finally snaps. The result is a mathematical minefield.

Mission: The Zero-Divisor Minefield

In a normal number system, if $a \times b = 0$, then either $a$ or $b$ must be zero. But in 16 dimensions, you can find two non-zero vectors that multiply to zero. These are called zero-divisors. Your goal is to find one.

The Geometric Consequence: Because zero-divisors exist in 16D, you can no longer reliably "divide" by a number. Uniqueness is lost. The Sedenions are an algebra, but they are not a "number system" in the way we expect.

What We Take With Us

The associator $[a,b,c] = (ab)c - a(bc)$ measures how badly associativity fails. Zero on a Fano line, nonzero otherwise.

Diassociativity: any two octonions generate a copy of $\mathbb{H}$. Non-associativity only bites with three independent directions.

Hurwitz's theorem (1898): $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{H}$, $\mathbb{O}$ are the only normed division algebras. In 16D, zero divisors appear and $|ab| = |a||b|$ fails.

Each Cayley-Dickson doubling sacrifices one property. The octonions are the last algebra where multiplication respects geometry.
