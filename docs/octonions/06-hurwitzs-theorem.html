Hurwitz’s Theorem

Part 6 of 15. Why the line stops at 8. Adolf Hurwitz proved that there are only four dimensions where multiplication respects distance.

You have seen the doubling machine build Reals, Complexes, Quaternions, and Octonions. It seems like it could go on forever. But in 1898, Adolf Hurwitz proved that we have already hit the limit. The Octonions are the "last" algebra where multiplication preserves the norm ($|ab| = |a||b|$).

When you double to 16 dimensions (the **Sedenions**), the link between multiplication and distance finally snaps. The result is a mathematical minefield.

Mission: The Zero-Divisor Minefield

In a normal number system, if $a \times b = 0$, then either $a$ or $b$ must be zero. But in 16 dimensions, you can find two non-zero vectors that multiply to zero. These are called **zero-divisors**. Your goal is to find one.

The Geometric Consequence: Because zero-divisors exist in 16D, you can no longer reliably "divide" by a number. Uniqueness is lost. The Sedenions are an algebra, but they are not a "number system" in the way we expect.

The Octonions stand at the edge. They are the largest system that still feels like geometry. In Part 7, we explore the unique "bonus" that this 8D structure gives us: a cross product that works in 7 dimensions.
