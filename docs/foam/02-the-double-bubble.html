The Double Bubble

Two bubbles are the smallest foam in three dimensions. The least-area way to enclose two volumes is the familiar double bubble, though proving it took until 2002.

Part 1 settled the plane, where equal cells use the least wall as hexagons and walls meet three at a time at 120 degrees. In three dimensions the smallest cases come first. A single soap bubble is a sphere, because a sphere is the least-area surface that can enclose a fixed volume. This is the isoperimetric property, the one-cell case of the foam problem.

Blow a second bubble against the first and they merge into a double bubble: two rounded chambers sharing a wall, with a single circular seam where three surfaces meet. This part asks which shape encloses two volumes with the least area, and why proving the familiar answer took until 2002.

One bubble: the sphere

Among all regions of a given area in the plane, the circle has the shortest boundary; among all solids of a given volume, the sphere has the smallest surface. The competitors are not close. A cube holding the same volume as a unit-radius sphere has about 23 percent more surface; a regular tetrahedron, nearly 50 percent more.

Figure 1. Three shapes enclosing the same area, with their perimeters. The circle spends the least boundary. In three dimensions the same logic picks the sphere.

The standard double bubble

With two volumes the answer is the standard double bubble: two spherical caps with a third spherical surface between them, all three meeting along one circle at 120 degrees, the junction angle of the plane foam in the last article. When the two volumes are equal, the dividing wall is flat, a plain disk. When one bubble is larger, the wall bows into it: the smaller chamber is at higher pressure, and it pushes the partition toward its bigger, lower-pressure neighbor.

Figure 2. A double bubble in cross-section (rotate it about the horizontal axis to get the real surface). Drag the slider to change the volume ratio. The three films always meet at 120° along the seam; the wall is flat only when the bubbles are equal, and otherwise bulges toward the larger one.

The wall bows because of pressure. Surface tension makes a curved film press on the gas behind it, harder when the film is more sharply curved. This is the Young–Laplace law, pressure proportional to curvature. The two bubbles sit at different pressures, and the wall between them must curve by exactly the difference:

The smaller bubble is more sharply curved (larger ), so the wall curvature comes out positive. The wall is hollow on the small bubble's side and bulges into the large one. Make the bubbles equal and the two curvatures cancel, so the wall goes flat. Every frame of Figure 2 obeys this.

Ruling out other clusters

That the standard double bubble is the answer looks obvious, since it is the shape real bubbles make. As with the honeycomb, the hard part is ruling out every other competitor. The hardest case was a non-standard double bubble in which one region wraps the other as a torus: a doughnut of one gas threaded by a ball of the other. No one expected it to have less area, but no one could rule it out.

Figure 3. A non-standard competitor: one region (torus) wrapped around the other (ball). The proof's real work was showing that some pinch or shift always lowers the area of every shape like this, so none can be the minimizer.

The equal-volume case fell first, in 1995, when Joel Hass, Michael Hutchings, and Roger Schlafly reduced it to a finite computer search that took twenty minutes on a 1995 PC. The full conjecture, for any pair of volumes, was proved in 2002 by Michael Hutchings, Frank Morgan, Manuel Ritoré, and Antonio Ros, with no computer at all: they showed that every conceivable non-standard double bubble is unstable, so the standard double bubble is the only candidate left.

Two volumes needed a 2002 proof. Three bubbles in space were only settled much later, and the general N-bubble problem is open. The difficulty climbs fast with the number of cells, a first hint of how hard an infinite foam will be.

The next step is to stop making clusters and fill space: keep the rule that three films meet at 120 degrees, demand that congruent cells tile all of three-dimensional space, and ask what shape does it best. That is Kelvin's question, and his almost-correct answer is next.
