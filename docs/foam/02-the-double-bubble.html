The Double Bubble

Two bubbles are the smallest foam in three dimensions. The least-area way to enclose two volumes is the familiar double bubble, though a proof came only in 2000.

Part 1 settled the plane, where equal cells use the least wall as hexagons and walls meet three at a time at 120 degrees. In three dimensions the smallest cases come first. A single soap bubble is a sphere, the least-area surface that can enclose a fixed volume. This is the isoperimetric property, the one-cell case of the foam problem.

Blow a second bubble against the first and they merge into a double bubble: two rounded chambers sharing a wall, with one circular seam where three surfaces meet. This part asks which shape encloses two volumes with the least area, and why proving the familiar answer took until 2000.

One bubble: the sphere

Among all regions of a given area in the plane, the circle has the shortest boundary; among all solids of a given volume, the sphere has the smallest surface, which Hermann Schwarz proved in 1884.

Twice as tall as wide, it spends 20.2% extra, and no proportion gets it under 14.5%, reached when height equals diameter (the tick): better than an octahedron, worse than a dodecahedron.

The standard double bubble

With two volumes the answer is the standard double bubble: two spherical caps with a third spherical surface between them, all three meeting along one circle at 120 degrees, the junction angle of the plane foam. When the two volumes are equal, the dividing wall is a flat disk. When one bubble is larger, the wall bows into it.

Figure 3. A double bubble in cross-section (spin it about the horizontal axis for the real surface). The slider moves gas between the chambers at a fixed total volume of 1. The three films meet at 120° along the seam at every setting, and each chamber shows its cap's radius. The bars are the three curvatures, one over each radius: the wall's always makes up the difference between the other two.

The wall bows because of pressure. A curved film presses on the gas behind it, harder the more sharply it curves (the Young–Laplace law), so the smaller bubble is at the higher pressure. A wall between two pressures must curve by exactly their difference:

Equal bubbles cancel, and the wall goes flat. Sharing a wall saves film, though less than one might guess.

The saving peaks at 5.5% for equal volumes and is still 3.6% when one chamber holds a tenth of the gas. Dashed: one sphere holding all of it, the cost with no wall, 20.6% less at equal volumes.

Ruling out other clusters

That the standard double bubble is the answer looks obvious: it is the shape real bubbles make. The hard part, as with the honeycomb, is ruling out every other cluster, and nothing forces a region to stay in one piece. In space, the rivals that survived longest had one gas as a ring around the waist of the other. A slice through such a ring shows two pieces, one on each side, and the plane has a rival of exactly that form. There every film is an arc of a circle, so it can be computed exactly.

Figure 5. Two equal areas in the plane, the purple one in two pieces on either side of the green. Every film is a circular arc, every corner is at 120°, and split evenly the pieces are at the same pressure, so the cluster balances. Yet it costs 15.3% more perimeter than the standard double bubble, more even than two separate circles (dashed). Sliding a piece changes nothing until four films meet; moving gas between the pieces lowers the perimeter either way.

Each control is a proof in miniature. Sliding is the key step of the proof for the plane, found in 1990 by five undergraduates at a Williams College summer program (Foisy, Alfaro, Brock, Hodges and Zimba, published 1993): an end piece can be moved along the film it sits on at no cost until it bumps into something, and the four-way crossing made there can always be shortened, as in Part 1. Moving gas shows the other idea: the balanced rival sits at the top of a hill.

In space the films of a rival are not all pieces of spheres, and the proof came in two steps. The equal-volume case fell in 1995, when Joel Hass, Michael Hutchings, and Roger Schlafly reduced it to a two-parameter family of rivals and a computer search, which took twenty minutes on a 1995 PC. The general case was proved in 2000 (published in the Annals in 2002) by Michael Hutchings, Frank Morgan, Manuel Ritoré, and Antonio Ros, with no computer. They showed that every rival left standing is unstable: some small change lowers its area with both volumes fixed, as moving gas does in Figure 5.

Three bubbles

Three bubbles in the plane follow the same rules: six arcs, four corners at 120 degrees, and every wall curved by the pressure difference across it. Figure 6 finds that cluster from its three areas.

Figure 6. The standard triple bubble for three chosen areas, solved in the page at total area 1. Each bubble shows its pressure, and the three pressures are the rungs of the ladder. Each bar is one wall's curvature, measured from its arc and stood on the lower of its two neighbors' rungs; it ends on the other rung every time. At 4 : 1 : 2 the cluster needs 83.7% of the perimeter of three separate circles.

Wacharin Wichiramala proved in 2002 that this cluster is the least-perimeter way to enclose three areas in the plane. In space, three bubbles waited until 2022, when Emanuel Milman and Joe Neeman proved the triple bubble in every dimension from three up and the quadruple bubble from four up (published 2025); a 2023 preprint of theirs adds five bubbles from dimension five. One step of their proof moves volume between two pieces of a region, the move of Figure 5. Four bubbles in ordinary space were left open, a first hint of how hard an infinite foam will be.

The next step is to stop making clusters and fill space with congruent cells whose films still meet in threes at 120 degrees. Which cell does it best is Kelvin's question, and his almost-correct answer is next.
