The Lattice Recipe

Give every point of a lattice the region nearest to it and you have a foam, in any number of dimensions. The lattice that packs balls best is not always the one that makes the cheapest foam. In four dimensions the recipe returns a regular solid that three dimensions has no version of.

Kelvin's cell in Part 3 came out of a recipe. Start with a lattice of points, there the body-centered cubic one, and give each point the region of space closer to it than to any other point, its Voronoi cell. The cells are identical, they leave no gaps, and their walls are a foam. Nothing in the recipe mentions the number of dimensions, so it can propose a foam in a space nobody can picture.

The same cell answers a second, older question. Put the largest ball that fits inside every cell and you have a packing of equal balls, whose density is the fraction of the cell that the ball fills. So one shape gives each lattice two scores: how much of it a ball fills, and how much wall it needs. Both reward a cell that is nearly round.

In the plane, one winner

In the plane one lattice wins both. Take two steps of equal length at an angle to each other and repeat them to make a lattice. At 90 degrees the cells are squares. At 60 degrees they are the hexagons of Part 1.

The curves score the whole family: the share of the plane the discs cover, and the wall cost of Part 1 (half the perimeter over the square root of the area). One peaks and the other bottoms out at the same angle, 60 degrees.

Both values at 60 degrees, 90.69 percent and 1.861, are the best possible, well beyond this one family. Thue showed that no arrangement of equal discs is denser, and the honeycomb theorem of Part 1 says no division into equal areas has less wall.

In space, two winners

Now take three steps of equal length with the same angle between every pair. At 90 degrees this is the simple cubic lattice. Close the angle to 60 degrees and it becomes the face-centered cubic lattice, the pattern of a pyramid of stacked oranges. Open it to 109.47 degrees, the tetrahedral angle of Part 3, and it is the body-centered cubic lattice.

Walls the ball touches are tinted blue with a dot at the contact; walls it never reaches are orange. The upper curve peaks twice, higher at the face-centered lattice. The lower curve dips twice, deeper at the body-centered one (its axis is zoomed, so the cubic lattice's 6.000 is off the top). The dashed line is the flat-walled Weaire-Phelan foam of Part 4, below every lattice on the curve.

The face-centered lattice wins the packing contest, and Thomas Hales proved (the paper appeared in 2005) that no packing of equal balls beats its 74.05 percent, lattice or not. The body-centered lattice fills only 68.02 percent, but its cell, Kelvin's, has less wall: 5.3147 against 5.3454. The simple cubic lattice between them is the worst at both.

Packing looks only at a cell's nearest walls, the ones the ball touches. Partition pays for all of them. The face-centered cell has twelve walls and the ball touches every one. Kelvin's cell has eight touched walls and six square ones the ball never reaches. The squares do nothing for the packing, but they blunt six corners, and the blunter cell has less wall for its volume.

Neither cell is the best foam. The Weaire-Phelan foam is not a lattice foam, since its cells come in two shapes, and with flat walls it already costs 5.297. The recipe supplies candidates, and a foam from outside it can do better.

The checkerboard, one dimension up

The face-centered cubic lattice has another description: the points with whole-number coordinates that add up to an even number, like the black squares of a checkerboard. That description works in any dimension. In three dimensions its cell is a cube with a low pyramid on each face, which gives it eight cube corners and six pyramid tips. The tips sit farther from the center than the cube corners, 1 against 0.866, so the solid has two kinds of corner.

In four dimensions the checkerboard lattice is called . Its cell is a four-dimensional cube with a pyramid on each of its eight cubic sides: sixteen cube corners and eight tips. The distance from the center of a unit cube to a corner grows with dimension, in three dimensions and in four, where it equals the distance to the tips. All 24 corners are now equally far out, all the edges have one length, and a turn of four-dimensional space carries tips onto cube corners. The cell is regular. It is called the 24-cell, after its 24 walls, each an octahedron.

In three dimensions the checkerboard lattice and the lattice of cube corners plus cube centers are different, face-centered and body-centered, and each wins one contest. In four dimensions the two constructions give the same lattice, turned and scaled.

Two more contests

The cell holds more scores than these two. Blow every ball up until it reaches the cell's farthest corner and the balls cover space: the covering score is the ball volume that takes per cell, and less is better. Or round every point of space to its nearest lattice point and average the squared error per coordinate, on cells of volume 1, which engineers call the quantizer problem.

Which flat foams have to bend?

Kelvin's cell could not be a soap film as drawn. Part 3's two rules ask walls to meet three at a time at 120 degrees, and the lines where they meet to come together four at a time at 109.47 degrees; his flat walls meet at 109.47 and 125.26. The same test can be run on any lattice foam, wherever three or more cells meet.

Figure 6. Every kind of junction in four flat lattice foams, measured from the cells. At the top of each column, walls are seen edge-on where they meet, with the angles between them (dashed: the 120 degrees of the rule). Below, the lines along which walls meet leave the point where they come together, their tips joined to show what they point at; drag to turn them. Rose marks a junction a film can rest in.

The face-centered foam comes close. Its walls meet at exactly 120 degrees, and at eight corners of each cell four lines meet at the right angle. At the six pyramid tips eight lines meet, and Jean Taylor's theorem of Part 3 allows only fours, so a film there would rearrange. The four-dimensional checkerboard foam has the same corner one dimension up: sixteen lines, toward the corners of a four-dimensional cube. Ken Brakke proved in 1991 that this arrangement, which loses in two and three dimensions, is area-minimizing from four up, and with that Annalisa Cesaroni and Matteo Novaga showed in 2023 that no rearrangement of the flat 24-cell foam inside a small enough ball has less wall. Of the seven lattices above the plane in Figure 4, it alone needs no bending.

Is a nearby lattice cheaper?

In three dimensions the lattice contest may be settled. A 2006 conjecture of K. Bezdek says that among convex cells that fill space by sliding copies of themselves, Kelvin's flat cell has the least wall. Three 2026 preprints, none yet in a journal, bear on it. In March, Cesaroni and Novaga proved that every small change of the body-centered lattice raises the wall cost and that some small changes of the face-centered one lower it. In September they, and separately Thomas Hales and Lark Song, announced proofs of the whole conjecture. Nothing like this is proved in four dimensions, so there it can only be tested.

Left to slide downhill, the face-centered lattice ended at the body-centered one and A4 at . Four dimensions has at least two bowls, then, and only their depths, 6.727 against 6.831, pick the winner. This is evidence, not proof. Cesaroni and Novaga suggest the 24-cell may be the best lattice foam in four dimensions; nobody has proved it, and nothing rules out a four-dimensional Weaire-Phelan that is not a lattice and does better. The least-wall partition is known only in the plane.
