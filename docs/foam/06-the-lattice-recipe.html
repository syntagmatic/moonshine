The Lattice Recipe

Give every point of a lattice the region nearest to it and you have a foam, in any number of dimensions. The lattice that packs balls best is not always the one that makes the cheapest foam. In four dimensions the recipe returns a regular solid that three dimensions has no version of.

Kelvin's cell in Part 3 came out of a recipe. Start with a lattice of points, there the body-centered cubic one, and give each point the region of space closer to it than to any other point, its Voronoi cell. The cells are identical, they leave no gaps, and their walls are a foam. Nothing in the recipe mentions the number of dimensions, so it can propose a foam in a space nobody can picture. This part follows it up to four.

The same cell answers a second, older question. Put the largest ball that fits inside every cell and you have a packing of equal balls, whose density is the fraction of the cell that the ball fills. So one shape gives each lattice two scores: how much of it a ball fills, and how much wall it needs. Both reward a cell that is nearly round, and it is tempting to assume one lattice wins both.

In the plane, one winner

In the plane it does. Take two steps of equal length at an angle to each other and repeat them to make a lattice. At 90 degrees the cells are squares. At 60 degrees they are the hexagons of Part 1.

The two curves score the whole family: the share of the plane the discs cover, and the wall cost of Part 1 (half the perimeter over the square root of the area). The first peaks and the second bottoms out at the same angle, 60 degrees.

At 60 degrees the discs cover 90.69 percent of the plane and the wall cost is 1.861, and both numbers are the best possible, well beyond this one family. Thue showed that no arrangement of equal discs is denser, and the honeycomb theorem of Part 1 says no division into equal areas has less wall.

In space, two winners

Now take three steps of equal length with the same angle between every pair. At 90 degrees this is the simple cubic lattice. Close the angle to 60 degrees and it becomes the face-centered cubic lattice, the pattern of a pyramid of stacked oranges. Open it to 109.47 degrees, the tetrahedral angle of Part 3, and it is the body-centered cubic lattice.

The lower curve dips twice, and the body-centered dip is the deeper (its axis is zoomed, so the cubic lattice's 6.000 is off the top). The dashed line is the Weaire-Phelan foam of Part 4 with flat walls, which no lattice on the curve reaches.

The face-centered lattice wins the packing contest. Its balls fill 74.05 percent of space, and Thomas Hales proved (the paper appeared in 2005) that no packing of equal balls does better, lattice or not. Its cell, the rhombic dodecahedron, has wall cost 5.3454. The body-centered lattice fills only 68.02 percent, and its cell, Kelvin's, costs 5.3147. The simple cubic lattice between them is the worst at both.

The figure shows how the scores come apart. Packing looks only at a cell's nearest walls, the ones the ball touches. Partition pays for all of them. The face-centered cell has twelve walls and the ball touches every one. Kelvin's cell has eight touched walls and six square ones the ball never reaches. The squares do nothing for the packing, but they blunt six corners, and the blunter cell has less wall for its volume.

Is any lattice cheaper than the body-centered one? A 2006 conjecture of K. Bezdek says no: among convex cells that fill space by sliding copies of themselves, Kelvin's flat cell has the least wall. In September 2026 two preprints announced proofs, one by Annalisa Cesaroni and Matteo Novaga and one by Thomas Hales and Lark Song; neither has yet appeared in a journal. Even so, that settles only the lattice contest. The Weaire-Phelan foam is not a lattice foam, since its cells come in two shapes, and with flat walls it already costs 5.297. The recipe supplies candidates, and a foam from outside it can do better.

The checkerboard, one dimension up

The face-centered cubic lattice has another description: the points with whole-number coordinates that add up to an even number, like the black squares of a checkerboard. That description works in any dimension. In three dimensions its cell is a cube with a low pyramid on each face, which gives it eight cube corners and six pyramid tips. The tips sit farther from the center than the cube corners, 1 against 0.866, so the solid has two kinds of corner.

In four dimensions the checkerboard lattice is called . Its cell is a four-dimensional cube with a pyramid on each of its eight cubic sides: sixteen cube corners and eight tips. The distance from the center of a unit cube to a corner grows with dimension, in three dimensions and in four, where it equals the distance to the tips. All 24 corners are now equally far out, all the edges have one length, and a turn of four-dimensional space carries tips onto cube corners. The cell is regular. It is called the 24-cell: 24 corners, 96 edges, 96 triangles and 24 walls, each wall an octahedron.

Of the regular solids in four dimensions, the 24-cell is the only one with no direct counterpart in three. It is also its own dual: the 24 nearest neighbors of a point of sit at the corners of another 24-cell, turned and scaled. In three dimensions the checkerboard lattice and the lattice of cube corners plus cube centers are different, face-centered and body-centered, and each wins one contest. In four dimensions the two constructions give the same lattice, turned and scaled.

A four-dimensional cube costs 8. The four-dimensional relative of Kelvin's cell, a solid with 120 corners and 30 walls, costs 6.831. Of the four lattices scored here has the cheapest cell and also the densest packing, filling 61.69 percent of space. It is the densest lattice packing known in four dimensions, and whether any packing at all does better is open.

On the foam side there is one firm result. The 24-cell's walls meet three at a time at exactly 120 degrees with no curving, and in 2023 Cesaroni and Novaga proved that the flat 24-cell honeycomb passes the local test completely: inside any small enough ball, no rearrangement of the walls has less area. Kelvin's flat cell fails that test, which is why his faces had to bend. The authors suggest the 24-cell may be the best lattice foam in four dimensions. Nobody has proved it, and nothing rules out a four-dimensional Weaire-Phelan that is not a lattice and does better. The least-wall partition is known only in the plane.
