The Counterexample

In 1993 two physicists in Dublin relaxed a foam made of two different cells on a computer. It came out 0.3 percent cheaper than Kelvin's. Nobody has proved it is the best.

Part 3 left Kelvin's cell at a wall cost of 5.306, the best anyone had found in 106 years. In 1993 Denis Weaire and Robert Phelan, at Trinity College Dublin, tried a different arrangement in Ken Brakke's Surface Evolver, a program that cuts a film into triangles and moves them downhill in area. Their foam, published in 1994, costs 5.288. This page builds it from eight points, relaxes it beside Kelvin's by the same method, and then cuts a slice through it.

Two cells instead of one

Kelvin assumed every cell had the same shape. Weaire and Phelan's foam uses two shapes of equal volume: a dodecahedron with twelve pentagonal faces, and a fourteen-faced cell with twelve pentagons and two hexagons.

The second formula is Euler's rule for any solid with three edges at each corner. No solid has 5.10-sided faces, so this ideal cell does not exist, but it says what to aim for. Kelvin's cell has 14 faces averaging 5.14 sides, and six of them are squares. Weaire and Phelan's cells average 13.5 faces and 5.11 sides, with nothing but pentagons and hexagons.

Eight points

The arrangement comes from eight points in a cube. Put one at each corner and one at the center, as for Kelvin's foam. Then put two on each face, on a line through the middle of the face, a quarter of the way in from either side, turning that line to a different axis on each pair of faces. Give every point the region of space nearer to it than to any other. The corner and center points get dodecahedra. The face points get fourteen-faced cells, joined hexagon to hexagon in columns that run in three perpendicular directions without crossing.

Figure 2. One repeat of the foam: two dodecahedra and six fourteen-faced cells. "Three columns" shows only fourteen-faced cells, stacked on their hexagons. With the walls slider at 0 every wall sits halfway between its two points and the dodecahedra come out about 3 percent smaller than the others. At 1 the walls between unlike cells have moved by 0.2 percent of the cube's edge and all eight volumes match.

That last adjustment matters. Kelvin's problem asks for equal volumes, and the nearest-point cells are unequal: the dodecahedra take 125/1024 of the cube each and the others 129/1024. Sliding the walls between unlike cells toward the larger ones, without tilting them, evens the volumes. The result still has flat faces and straight edges, and its wall cost is already 5.297, below Kelvin's relaxed 5.306.

Relaxing both foams

Flat faces break Plateau's rules in both foams, so both can still lose a little wall. The method is the Evolver's: cut each wall into triangles, move the triangle corners in whatever direction lowers the total area, and hold every cell's volume fixed. Here it runs with about 4,500 triangles per foam.

Kelvin's foam falls from 5.3147 to about 5.3064 and Weaire and Phelan's from 5.2970 to about 5.2885. The published values are 5.30628 and 5.28834. This page's triangles are coarse, and flat triangles overestimate a curved film slightly; repeating the run on finer meshes and extrapolating gives 5.30628 and 5.28835. The gap is 0.34 percent. In Kelvin's foam the squares stay exactly flat and the hexagons warp. In Weaire and Phelan's it is the reverse: the hexagons stay flat and every pentagon bends.

What is known

The comparison does not rest on numerics alone. In 1996 Rob Kusner and John Sullivan proved that the Weaire-Phelan foam beats every foam whose cells are arranged like Kelvin's. Nothing proves that it beats every foam. The Kelvin problem is open, and the 1993 structure is only the best one known.

It took until 2011 to see it in a real foam. Ruggero Gabbrielli and colleagues got six layers of about 1,500 equal bubbles to order into the structure, in a container whose walls had been moulded to fit it. A flat wall does not suit this foam, which is one reason it had not turned up in a beaker.

A slice through it

The most visible copy is the National Aquatics Center built for the 2008 Beijing Olympics, the Water Cube. Its designers at Arup, PTW and CCDI filled space with Weaire-Phelan cells in a computer, rotated the array, and cut out a box 177 meters square and 31 meters high. Steel tubes, about 22,000 of them, follow the cell edges, and the walls show where the box's faces cut through the cells. Arup's Tristram Carfrae said of the foam that "when it is viewed at an arbitrary angle it appears totally random and organic".

Figure 5. A flat cut through the flat-faced foam, with dodecahedra and fourteen-faced cells shaded by type. Face-on, the pattern repeats once per cube. At most other angles no direction of the lattice lies in the plane, and the pattern never exactly repeats.

The cut is regular in the only sense that matters to a builder: every piece comes from one of two cells. It looks like froth because the plane meets each copy of a cell at a different height.

The eight points were not new in 1993. Chemists and metallurgists had been drawing them for decades, for reasons that had nothing to do with soap. Part 5 follows them.
