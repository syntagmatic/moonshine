Why It Recurs

The eight points behind the Weaire-Phelan foam are also a metal, a cage of ice around a gas molecule, and a liquid crystal. The same misfit of 7.36 degrees between tetrahedra runs through all of them.

Part 4 built the best known foam from eight points in a cube. Weaire and Phelan did not invent those points, and said so: their 1994 abstract calls the foam "a structure analogous to that of some clathrate compounds and also related to the β-tungsten structure". A clathrate is a crystal of cages, and β-tungsten is a metal. This part asks why ice, metal and soap should agree.

Tetrahedra almost fill space

Four equal spheres can all touch one another in only one way, at the corners of a regular tetrahedron. If regular tetrahedra could fill space, atoms of one size would have an obvious way to crystallize. They cannot, because the tetrahedra do not fit around an edge. The angle between two faces of a regular tetrahedron is , about 70.53 degrees. Five of them leave a gap, and six overlap.

Twelve neighbors, or a few more

In 1958 F. C. Frank and J. S. Kasper described a family of alloys that share the misfit out in one particular way. In these crystals, they wrote, "tetrahedral groupings of atoms occur everywhere in the structure", and they singled out four shells of neighbors that an atom can have, with 12, 14, 15 and 16 atoms.

The eight points of Part 4, which crystallographers call A15, belong to this family. Figure 2 takes one of the eight, builds its cell with the code from Part 4, and puts an atom at every point whose cell shares a wall with it. The cell and the shell are one object read two ways. Each wall of the cell is crossed by one bond. Each corner of the cell, where three walls meet, is a triangle of three neighbors, and that triangle makes a tetrahedron with the atom in the middle. A wall with five sides means five tetrahedra ring that bond. A hexagon means six.

Why twelve and fourteen

No bond can have 5.104 tetrahedra around it, but an average can come close. One repeat of A15 has 48 pentagonal walls and 6 hexagonal ones, so its bonds average 5.111. Euler's rule from Part 4 turns sides per wall into walls per cell, and 5.104 sides goes with 13.4 faces. Cells should therefore have 12 faces, 14, and now and then more, mixed to average a little over 13. A15 uses one quarter 12s and three quarters 14s.

So the count says which cells turn up, not which foam wins. In C15 the 16-faced cell starts out 23 percent larger than the 12-faced one, and forcing the volumes equal costs wall. The two cells of A15 start within 3 percent of each other.

Atoms at the centers, water at the corners

Put an atom at each of the eight points and the result is a metal. In chromium silicide, Cr3Si, silicon sits at the cube's corners and center and chromium at the six face points. β-tungsten is tungsten in the same arrangement. So is the superconductor Nb3Sn, though nothing about foam explains its superconductivity.

Now put a water molecule at every corner of the cells instead. One cube has 46 corners, and four edges meet at each. In 1952 Linus Pauling and Richard Marsh published this structure for chlorine hydrate, a crystal of ice cages around chlorine molecules: 46 water molecules per cube, forming "two pentagonal dodecahedra and six tetrakaidecahedra", with the chlorine in the six larger cages. They had not been looking at ice. They were "studying the structures of intermetallic compounds", they wrote, and "in the course of this work a structure was found which seemed to be ideally suited to the hydrates of smaller gas molecules". Methane hydrate has the same framework, with methane in the cages.

Water gets there for its own reason. A water molecule in ice makes four hydrogen bonds, and one of Pauling and Marsh's objections to an earlier proposal was that its bond angles "differ greatly from the expected tetrahedral angle". That angle is 109.47 degrees, the one at which Plateau's rule brings four foam edges together.

Foam, metal and ice share a network and differ in their measurements. The foam bends its walls until they meet at Plateau's angles. The ice moves its corners until every bond has the same length. Ken Brakke, who wrote the Surface Evolver, notes the irony that a picture with this topology was in Pauling's 1960 chemistry book.

Soft spheres

The newest case is the closest to foam. In 1997 Balagurusamy, Ungar, Percec and Johansson reported tree-shaped molecules that gather, between two and twelve at a time, into soft balls, and the balls into a cubic liquid crystal: balls at the corners and center of a cube, and on the bisectors of its faces at one quarter and three quarters. Those are the eight points again. In 2000 Primoz Ziherl and Randall Kamien proposed a reason. Each ball has a hard core and a soft, brush-like coat, and pressing two coats together costs entropy in proportion to the area of the wall between their cells. The coats, they wrote, "favor area-minimizing structures, precisely the type of structures that dry foams might make". On that account the molecules are working on Kelvin's problem.

Block copolymers, chains of unlike polymers joined end to end, came later. In 2010 Lee, Bluemle and Bates found a Frank-Kasper phase with 30 spheres per repeat in melts of them, and in 2019 Morgan Bates and colleagues reported A15 itself.

The argument has a limit. Ziherl and Kamien start from the fact that hard spheres with no soft coat should crystallize as face-centered cubic, and the foam of that lattice costs 5.345, worse than Kelvin's. With a soft coat the packing problem becomes a partition problem, and the two have different winners. Part 6 takes up that difference.
