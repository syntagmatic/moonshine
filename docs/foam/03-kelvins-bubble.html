Kelvin's Bubble

In 1887 William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, asked the honeycomb question in three dimensions: which cells of equal volume fill space with the least wall? No flat-faced cell obeys the soap-film rules. Thomson bent one just enough to obey them and conjectured it was optimal. A better foam was found in 1993.

The truncated octahedron

Kelvin's cell is the truncated octahedron: take an octahedron and slice off its six points, each cut a third of the way along the edges. What remains has fourteen faces (six squares where the points were and eight hexagons from the triangular faces), 36 edges and 24 corners, and copies of it fill space with no gaps.

Plateau's laws in three dimensions

A real foam obeys the three-dimensional version of Plateau's laws, which Jean Taylor proved in 1976 are the only stable possibilities. Along every edge, exactly three films meet at 120 degrees. At every corner, exactly four edges come together, at the tetrahedral angle, the angle between two lines from the center of a regular tetrahedron to two of its corners:

Figure 2. Plateau's two rules. Along an edge, three films meet at 120° (first panel). Where edges meet, four come together at the tetrahedral angle 109.47° (second panel). The flat truncated octahedron breaks both: its faces meet at 109.47° and 125.26°, never 120°, and its edges at 90° and 120°.

Why four edges and no more

Thomson began with a simpler cell. The rhombic dodecahedron fills space with twelve flat faces that meet at exactly 120 degrees along every edge, at a cost of 5.345. But at six of its corners eight edges and twelve films come together, like the twelve triangles that join the edges of a cube to its center. Plateau had dipped a cubic wire frame in soap solution and got something else: the film opened a small square in the middle.

Figure 3. A soap film on a cubic wire frame, found by moving 1,408 triangles downhill in area with only the frame held. Shut, twelve flat films meet at a point with area 4.2426; every size up to 0.28 has less, the film at rest 0.07 percent less. Thomson blew on his square until it shut, and a new one opened facing another way.

Thomson let every such corner of the dodecahedron foam open the same way, then squeezed the foam until it pulled equally in all directions. Out came a cell with six equal squares and eight hexagons: the truncated octahedron, already bent.

How the faces bend

By symmetry each square stays flat while its edges bow outward. Each hexagon keeps its six corners and three long diagonals in one plane, and the six sectors between the diagonals rise and fall in turn.

Wall cost

The bending is a trade. Each square grows by 9 percent, each hexagon shrinks by 3 percent, and the total falls from 5.315 to about 5.306, a gain of 0.16 percent. For over a century nobody did better.

What a real foam does

In 1946 E. B. Matzke reported building a foam one equal bubble at a time with a syringe and examining 600 bubbles from its interior under a microscope. None was a Kelvin cell. They averaged 13.70 faces, and two faces in three were pentagons, of which Kelvin's cell has none.

In 1993 Denis Weaire and Robert Phelan found a foam of two kinds of cell, a dodecahedron and a fourteen-faced cell, whose faces are pentagons but for a few hexagons. Its is about 5.288, some 0.3 percent less wall than Kelvin's. Nobody has proved it optimal either, and the three-dimensional problem is still open.
