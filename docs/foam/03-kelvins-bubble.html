Kelvin's Bubble

In 1887 William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, proposed a single fourteen-faced cell as the least-wall way to fill space with equal volumes. It stood for 106 years, and the problem is still open.

In 1887 William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, asked the three-dimensional version of the honeycomb question: fill space with cells of equal volume, and find the shape that spends the least wall. There is no perfect cell here, since no single polyhedron both tiles space and meets the soap-film rules on its own. Kelvin found the shape that comes closest, curved it just enough to obey the rules, and conjectured it was optimal. A better foam was found in 1993.

The truncated octahedron

Kelvin's cell is the truncated octahedron: take an octahedron and slice off its six points. What remains has fourteen faces (six squares where the points were and eight hexagons from the original triangular faces), 36 edges and 24 vertices. Copies fill space with no gaps, the way cubes do but with blunter corners.

It is the Voronoi cell of the body-centered cubic lattice: the territory of one point when points sit at every cube corner and cube center.

Plateau's laws in three dimensions

A real foam obeys the three-dimensional version of Plateau's laws, which Jean Taylor proved in 1976 are the only stable possibilities. Two rules matter here. Along every edge, exactly three films meet at 120 degrees. At every point where edges meet, exactly four of them come together, at the tetrahedral angle, the angle between two lines from the center of a regular tetrahedron to two of its corners:

Figure 2. Plateau's two rules. Along an edge, three films meet at 120° (first panel). Where edges meet, four come together at the tetrahedral angle 109.47° (second panel). The flat truncated octahedron breaks both: its faces meet at 109.47° and 125.26°, never 120°. So the faces must bend.

Kelvin's fix was to let the faces relax. The square faces stay flat while their edges curve, the hexagons warp into gentle non-planar saddles, and every junction settles to 120 degrees. The cell still tiles space and holds the same volume, and the curved faces save a little wall.

The cell was so natural a candidate that for 106 years it was assumed to be optimal. Unlike the honeycomb and the double bubble, it was not. In 1993 Denis Weaire and Robert Phelan, relaxing foams numerically in Ken Brakke's Surface Evolver, found a structure built from two kinds of cell: a pentagonal dodecahedron and a fourteen-faced cell with twelve pentagons and two hexagons, six of the second for every two of the first. Its is about 5.288, some 0.3 percent less wall than Kelvin's. Regular pentagons cannot tile the plane, but pentagonal faces work well in space once they are allowed to curve. Nobody has proved that Weaire and Phelan's foam is optimal either. The three-dimensional problem is still open.
