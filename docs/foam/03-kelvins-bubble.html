Kelvin's Bubble

In 1887 Lord Kelvin proposed a single elegant cell as the best way to fill space with equal-volume foam. It was believed for 106 years.

The plane gave up its answer cleanly: hexagons, proven optimal. Lord Kelvin asked the obvious next question in 1887. Fill all of three-dimensional space with cells of equal volume; which shape spends the least wall? Unlike the hexagon, there is no perfect cell here — no single polyhedron both tiles space and meets the soap-film rules on its own. Kelvin found the shape that comes closest, curved it just enough to obey the rules, and conjectured it was the winner. Proving him wrong took until 1993.

One cell, fourteen faces

Kelvin's cell is the truncated octahedron: take an octahedron and slice off its six points. What remains has fourteen faces — six squares where the points were, and eight hexagons from the original triangular faces — with 36 edges and 24 vertices. Stack copies and they fill space with no gaps, the way cubes do but with gentler corners. It is the single most symmetric space-filling cell there is.

It is the Voronoi cell of the body-centered cubic lattice — the territory of one point when points sit at every cube corner and cube center.

The rules a foam must obey

Why curve the faces at all? Because a real foam obeys the three-dimensional version of Plateau's laws, proved to be the only stable possibilities by Jean Taylor in 1976. Two rules matter here. Along every edge, exactly three films meet at 120 degrees. At every point where edges meet, exactly four of them come together, at the tetrahedral angle — the angle between two lines from the center of a regular tetrahedron to two of its corners:

Figure 2. Plateau's two rules. Along an edge, three films meet at 120° (left). Where edges meet, four come together at the tetrahedral angle 109.47° (right). The flat truncated octahedron breaks both: its faces meet at 109.47° and 125.26°, never 120°. So the faces must bend.

The flat polyhedron cannot satisfy these. Its hexagons meet each other at 109.47 degrees and meet the squares at 125.26 degrees — neither is the 120 the films demand. Kelvin's fix was to let the faces relax: the square faces stay nearly flat, but the hexagons warp into gentle, non-planar saddles, and every junction settles to 120 degrees. The cell still tiles space and still holds the same volume; it just trades flat faces for curved ones and saves a little wall.

Kelvin published this in 1887 and no one improved on it. The cell is so symmetric, so natural, so obviously right, that for 106 years it was simply assumed to be optimal. It is the same trap as the honeycomb and the double bubble: a beautiful candidate that looks unbeatable. This time the candidate lost. In 1993 Denis Weaire and Robert Phelan, relaxing foams numerically in Ken Brakke's Surface Evolver, found a structure built from two kinds of cell: a pentagonal dodecahedron and a fourteen-faced cell with twelve pentagons and two hexagons, six of the second for every two of the first. Its is about 5.288, some 0.3 percent less wall than Kelvin's. The pentagons that could not tile the plane turn out to do well in space, once the faces are allowed to curve. Nobody has proved that Weaire and Phelan's foam is optimal either. The three-dimensional problem is still open.
