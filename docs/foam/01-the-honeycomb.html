The Honeycomb

Flattened soap froth settles into hexagons. Among all ways to divide the plane into cells of equal area, the hexagonal grid uses the least wall, as Hales proved in 1999.

Squeeze soap froth between two sheets of glass and you get a foam one bubble thick. Left to settle, its walls shorten, every junction becomes a three-way fork, and the cells drift toward six sides. Bees build the same pattern, and around 36 BC the Roman writer Varro was already reporting what geometers said about it.

The problem: divide the plane into regions of equal area with as little wall as possible. A soap film is wall under tension, so a foam relaxes toward the layout with the least film.

Plateau's rule

Start with the local rule, named for the nineteenth-century physicist Joseph Plateau. In a dry foam, whose films are thin and drained of liquid, exactly three films meet at every junction, at 120 degrees. Four never cross and two never kink.

Four pins show why. The obvious way to join the corners of a square with the least film is an X, and it is wrong. Pull the crossing apart into two forks joined by a short bridge and the total drops, until each fork has opened to 120 degrees. That is where three equal pulls cancel: every film tugs on its junction with the same tension, and three equal tugs balance only when spread evenly, apart.

Three regular tilings

Why a hexagon and not a square or a triangle? Of the three regular shapes that tile the plane on their own, the hexagon spends the least wall per unit of area. A cell's cost is half its perimeter (so that each shared wall is counted once) over the square root of its area:

"Best of three" is a weak claim. Pappus of Alexandria compared only those three when he wrote that bees, "by a certain geometrical forethought," build the cells that hold the most honey for the least wax. Mixed shapes or wavy walls might still do better.

Why mixing shapes does not help

Mixed shapes can be ruled out in a few lines if the walls are straight. The regular polygon has the least perimeter for its area and number of sides, so its cost is a floor for every cell with that many sides. More sides means a lower floor: the regular heptagon costs 1.836, less than the hexagon. But Euler's formula caps the average at six, so every cell with more than six sides is paid for by cells with fewer, and the floor rises faster on that side than it falls on the other.

Figure 4. The curve is the wall cost of the regular polygon with each number of sides. The twelve cells always average six sides, and the diamond is the floor for the whole mix, the average of its dots. It opens as six pentagons and six heptagons at 1.871, against 1.861 for twelve hexagons: the curve bends upward, so the midpoint of two of its points lies above it. The dashed line is what Hales's proof uses in place of the curve; the heptagon clears it by 0.00007.

László Fejes Tóth proved the honeycomb conjecture for convex cells in 1943. Real films are not that obliging: a film between bubbles at different pressures is an arc of a circle, and a cell whose walls bulge outward holds its area inside less perimeter than any polygon can. A proof without convexity, he wrote in 1964, would "involve considerable difficulties." Thomas Hales found one in 1999, published in 2001 as "The Honeycomb Conjecture": of all ways to divide the plane into regions of equal area, with walls of any shape, periodic or not, none has less perimeter per cell than the hexagonal grid.

An experiment

A theorem that strong can be tested. Figure 5 holds 64 bubbles on a box that wraps around. Films pull with a fixed tension and gas pushes back with whatever pressure keeps every bubble at the same area: the foam model of Part 7 with the gas sealed in. Nothing in it mentions hexagons or 120 degrees. Swap some walls of a honeycomb, so that neighbors change, and let go. With 8 walls swapped it healed completely in all 7 runs. With more, cells get stuck with the wrong number of sides, each adding about 0.085 percent to the cost. Foams relaxed from a random start cost 2.1 to 4.6 percent more than the honeycomb.

Ninety-six foams that fail to beat the honeycomb prove nothing, any more than Pappus's three tilings did. A relaxing foam only finds the nearest valley.

Pentagons

The regular tilings leave out the pentagon. Its corners are 108 degrees, 12 short of Plateau's angle, and three of them around a point come to 324.

Figure 7. Three regular pentagons around a vertex leave a 36° gap. The plane has no five-fold honeycomb. In three dimensions the cells a foam forms are made mostly of pentagons, and they never fit exactly.

The plane has a perfect answer and a foam can reach it. Space does not: no polyhedron tiles space and satisfies the foam's junction rules on its own. The cells that come closest are built largely from pentagons, and the search for their best arrangement has run for more than a century and is not finished.
