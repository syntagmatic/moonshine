The Honeycomb

In the plane, the least-area way to divide space into equal cells is settled. It is the hexagon, and the proof took until 1999.

Squeeze soap froth between two sheets of glass and you get a foam one bubble thick: a flat patchwork of cells pushing against each other. Left to settle, it is never a random mess. The walls straighten, the junctions tidy themselves into a recurring three-way fork, and the cells drift toward six-sided shapes. The froth is solving an optimization problem, and so is a beehive, and so was a Greek geometer named Pappus around the year 340.

The problem is to divide the plane into regions of equal area while spending as little wall as possible. A soap film is wall under tension, and tension is energy per unit length, so a real foam relaxes toward whatever layout has the least total film. In two dimensions the answer is known and proven. The rest of the series is about how this tidy ending falls apart in three.

Three films, one angle

Start with the local rule. Take a dry foam, one where the films are thin and the liquid has drained away, and look at any point where walls meet. You will always find exactly three films meeting there, at 120 degrees. Four films never cross, and two films never kink.

The reason is a force balance. Each film pulls on the junction with the same tension, directed along the film. The only way three equal pulls can sum to zero is if they are spread evenly around the circle, and evenly around a circle for three directions means between each pair. A four-way crossing is possible for an instant but unstable: nudge it and it resolves into two three-way junctions joined by a short new wall. This is Plateau's rule, named for the blind nineteenth-century physicist Joseph Plateau, who mapped the behavior of soap films by having assistants describe them to him.

Watch the junctions converge on three-way forks near 120°, the cells settle to a mean of six sides, and the wall cost fall toward the hexagon's value of 1.861. The number counts wall length per cell, each wall split between the two cells it separates, divided by the square root of the cell's area so it does not depend on scale.

Figure 1 runs the geometric shadow of a soap-film simulation. Each cell is the territory of a site point (every spot closer to that site than to any other), and each step moves the site to the middle of its territory. Iterated, that one move shrinks the walls and evens out the cells until the froth is a honeycomb, give or take the ragged boundary.

The honeycomb wins

Why a hexagon and not a square or a triangle? Among the three regular shapes that tile the plane on their own, the hexagon spends the least wall per unit of area. The dimensionless cost of a single cell is its perimeter (counting each shared wall once, so half of it) divided by the square root of its area:

For a square this number is exactly 2; for an equilateral triangle it is about 2.280; for the regular hexagon it is 1.861. The hexagon wins because its interior angle is 120 degrees, the angle three films want to make, so a hexagonal grid satisfies Plateau's rule at every junction. Squares meet four-to-a-corner at 90 degrees, which a foam will not tolerate; triangles crowd six-to-a-corner.

Figure 2. The three regular tilings, ranked by wall cost. Hover a tiling to highlight its bar. The hexagon's 1.861 is the lowest a regular tiling can reach, and its corners already sit at the 120° a foam demands.

"Best among the three regular tilings" is a weak claim. A clever irregular partition, with cells of wavy or mixed shapes, might beat the hexagon on average. Pappus assumed no such thing existed; he wrote that bees, "by a certain geometrical forethought," build hexagonal cells because hexagons hold the most honey for the least wax. For sixteen centuries that was a guess, because ruling out every alternative, including the non-periodic ones, is hard.

The hexagonal grid is also the densest way to pack equal circles in the plane. The two facts are cousins but not the same problem, and in three dimensions the best packing and the best foam part ways.

It was finally proved in 1999 by Thomas Hales, who published it as "The Honeycomb Conjecture" in Discrete & Computational Geometry in 2001. The theorem is unconditional: among all ways of partitioning the plane into regions of equal area, the regular hexagonal grid minimizes total perimeter, periodic or not. The two-dimensional foam problem is closed.

Why not pentagons

One shape is conspicuously missing from the regular tilings: the pentagon. Its interior angle is 108 degrees, and three of them around a point come to 324 degrees, leaving a 36-degree wedge of empty space. Regular pentagons cannot tile the plane at all. The number 120 is special because 360 divides by it into a whole number of films, and the hexagon is the regular polygon whose own corners are 120 degrees. The pentagon misses by twelve.

Figure 3. Three regular pentagons around a vertex leave a 36° gap. The plane has no five-fold honeycomb. Hold onto this frustration: in three dimensions the cells a foam wants are stitched mostly out of pentagons, and they never quite fit.

The two-dimensional story is clean because the plane has a perfect answer and the foam can reach it. Space does not. No polyhedron tiles space and satisfies the foam's junction rules on its own, the way the hexagon does in the plane. The cells that come closest are built largely from pentagons, the shape that cannot tile, and the search for the least-wall arrangement of them has run for more than a century and is still not finished.
