Foam

Dividing space into equal cells with the least wall. Three short essays: the plane, which is solved; two bubbles, which are solved; and all of space, which is not.

A soap film pulls itself as small as it can. Put many cells of gas together and the films settle into a foam, and the foam is a physical answer to a geometric question: how do you split a region into pieces of equal size while spending the least boundary? The films obey two local rules, three walls meeting at 120 degrees and, in space, four edges meeting at the tetrahedral angle. Those rules are easy to state. Whether they pin down the best global arrangement is the part that took centuries.

The essays are short and each makes one argument. Along the way you can relax a froth toward hexagons, resize a double bubble whose walls are built from the 120-degree condition, and turn Kelvin's cell over to see which of its flat faces break the rules.

The strip above is a real Voronoi diagram. Its seed points slide from random on the left to a triangular lattice on the right, and the cells go from an irregular froth to the honeycomb.
