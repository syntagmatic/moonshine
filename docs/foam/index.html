Foam

Dividing space into equal cells with the least wall. Seven short essays: the plane, which is solved; two bubbles, which are solved; all of space, which is not; and what the best known answer has in common with ice, metal and a foam that will not hold still.

A soap film pulls itself as small as it can. Put many cells of gas together and the films settle into a foam, and the foam is a physical answer to a geometric question: how do you split a region into pieces of equal size while spending the least boundary? The films obey two local rules, three walls meeting at 120 degrees and, in space, four edges meeting at the tetrahedral angle. Those rules are easy to state. Whether they pin down the best global arrangement is the part that took centuries.

The first three essays reach Kelvin's 1887 guess for space: you can relax a froth toward hexagons, resize a double bubble whose walls are built from the 120-degree condition, and turn Kelvin's cell over to see which of its flat faces break the rules. The fourth builds the foam that beat it in 1993 and relaxes both in the page. The last three follow that foam outward: into crystals and ice, up to four dimensions, and into a foam that changes with time.
