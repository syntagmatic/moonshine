Foams in Motion

A foam left alone coarsens: gas leaks from bubble to bubble, small bubbles vanish and large ones grow. In two dimensions, how fast a bubble changes depends only on how many sides it has.

The foams in the earlier parts were drawn at rest: the honeycomb of Part 1, Kelvin's cell in Part 3, the Weaire-Phelan foam in Part 4. A real foam does not rest. Its films are slightly permeable, and gas seeps through them from one bubble into the next. Small bubbles empty into their neighbors and disappear, the survivors grow, and the foam gets coarser. No film has to burst for this to happen.

This part follows that process in a dry foam one bubble thick, like the froth between glass plates in Part 1. There it obeys an unusually simple rule: how fast a bubble gains or loses area depends on its number of sides and on nothing else.

Pressure, curvature and a leak

A film between two bubbles is flat only if the pressures on its two sides are equal. Otherwise it bows into the bubble with the lower pressure, as the wall of the double bubble did in Part 2, and settles on a circular arc whose curvature is proportional to the pressure difference. Gas then leaks through the film from the high side to the low side, at a rate proportional to the pressure difference and to the length of film available. So the area a bubble loses through one wall, per unit time, is proportional to that wall's curvature times its length. For a circular arc that product is the angle the arc turns through.

Measuring it

None of this was put into the foam of Figure 1. Its films are chains of short straight segments. Each segment pulls on its two ends with the same tension, each bubble pushes on its walls with its pressure, and gas crosses each film in proportion to the pressure difference. The 120-degree corners are not imposed either: a junction is a point pulled by three films, and it comes to rest where three equal pulls balance. Why it never settles

Six is also the average. In any foam with three-way junctions, Euler's formula fixes the mean number of sides at exactly six, as it did for the froth of Part 1. So bubbles above six are always balanced by bubbles below, and the only foam at rest is one in which every bubble has six sides.

The law gives a bubble's rate, not its fate, because side counts change, in two ways. When a film shrinks to nothing, the two bubbles that shared it are parted and the two at its ends become neighbors. That is a T1, a neighbor swap: one pair loses a side each, the other pair gains one, and nothing disappears. When a bubble shrinks to nothing, that is a T2. In this simulation it gets there as a triangle, having shed its other sides in T1s on the way down, and its three neighbors each lose a side. A growing seven-sided bubble can lose two neighbors this way and start to shrink.

Less film, larger bubbles

Through all of this one quantity moves in a single direction. Gas flows from high pressure to low, and every such transfer shortens the total length of film. In Parts 1 to 4 least film was a property of a finished pattern. Here it is a direction of travel. The pace is set by the law as well: is an area per unit time and the foam has no length of its own to compare it with, so once the starting pattern is forgotten the mean bubble area can only grow in proportion to the time elapsed.

Grains, and the third dimension

John von Neumann gave the argument in 1952, as a written discussion of a paper on the shapes of metal grains by Cyril Stanley Smith. It takes up pages 108 to 110 of the volume Metal Interfaces. Four years later William Mullins showed that the same law holds for the grains themselves. A grain boundary in a metal has no gas to leak, but it moves toward its center of curvature at a speed proportional to the curvature, and the count of turning goes through unchanged.

Coarsening is not a search for the foams of the earlier parts. Those problems fix the number of cells, make them equal, and ask for the least wall. A coarsening foam keeps neither condition. It sheds film by making its bubbles unequal and removing the small ones, and with unequal bubbles it can undercut the honeycomb: the readout under Figure 4 compares its film with a honeycomb of as many equal cells in the same box, and the foam runs several percent lower. Hales's theorem is about equal areas, so nothing is contradicted. Figure 1 halts at 30 bubbles only because its box is small. The rules name no stopping point short of a foam in which every bubble has six sides, and nothing in them steers the foam toward one.
