Foams in Motion

A foam left alone coarsens: gas leaks from bubble to bubble, small bubbles vanish and large ones grow. In two dimensions, how fast a bubble changes depends only on how many sides it has.

The foams of the earlier parts were drawn at rest. A real foam does not rest. Its films are slightly permeable, and gas seeps through them from one bubble into the next. Small bubbles empty into their neighbors and disappear, the survivors grow, and the foam gets coarser. No film has to burst for this to happen.

This part follows that process in a dry foam one bubble thick. There it obeys an unusually simple rule: how fast a bubble gains or loses area depends on its number of sides and on nothing else.

Pressure, curvature and a leak

A film between two bubbles is flat only if the pressures on its two sides are equal. Otherwise it bows into the bubble at lower pressure, as the wall of the double bubble did in Part 2, on a circular arc whose curvature is proportional to the pressure difference. Gas leaks through it from the high side to the low side, at a rate proportional to the pressure difference and to the length of film. So the area a bubble loses through one wall, per unit time, is proportional to that wall's curvature times its length, and for a circular arc that product is the angle the arc turns through.

Measuring it

None of this was put into the foam of Figure 1. Its films are chains of short straight segments. Each segment pulls on its two ends with the same tension, each bubble pushes on its walls with its pressure, and gas crosses each film in proportion to the pressure difference. When the side count changes

The law fixes a bubble's rate only while it keeps its sides, and side counts change in two ways. When a film shrinks to nothing, the two bubbles that shared it are parted and the two at its ends become neighbors: a T1, or neighbor swap. One pair loses a side each, the other pair gains one, and nothing disappears. When a bubble shrinks to nothing, that is a T2. In this simulation it gets there as a triangle, having shed its other sides in T1s, and its three neighbors each lose a side.

A honeycomb, disturbed

Six is also the average. In any foam with three-way junctions, Euler's formula fixes the mean number of sides at exactly six, as in Part 1. So bubbles above six are always balanced by bubbles below, and the only foam at rest is one in which every bubble has six sides. Figure 5 starts from that foam, a honeycomb, and makes one change to it. Give one bubble twice the gas of the others and it stays six-sided, so nothing moves: size is not in the law. Remove one film instead, the one event on this page that is not diffusion, and the two bubbles beside it merge into one with eight sides.

Figure 5. A simulated honeycomb of 320 bubbles under the rules of Figure 1, with one film removed. The merged bubble starts with eight sides and a five-sided bubble at each end. By time 5.5 it has 28 sides and 60 times the area of a hexagon, 50 bubbles have vanished, and 246 of the 269 left are still six-sided. With one bubble doubled instead, it keeps six sides and no film moves.

As the merged bubble grows its perimeter lengthens and touches more neighbors, so its growth speeds up. The honeycomb around it does not become disordered first. It stays a honeycomb until it is eaten.

Less film, larger bubbles

Through all of this one quantity moves in a single direction. Gas flows from high pressure to low, and every such transfer shortens the total length of film. The law sets the pace as well: is an area per unit time and the foam has no length of its own to compare it with, so once the starting pattern is forgotten the mean bubble area can only grow in proportion to the time elapsed.

Grains, and the third dimension

John von Neumann gave the argument in 1952, discussing a paper on the shapes of metal grains by Cyril Stanley Smith. Four years later William Mullins showed that it holds for the grains themselves, whose boundaries have no gas to leak but move at a speed proportional to their curvature.

So the best known foam for equal cells stops having equal cells as soon as gas can cross its walls: its dodecahedra are at higher pressure and empty into their neighbors. Resizing them does not help. From half the volume of a fourteen-faced cell to nearly twice, the dodecahedron shrinks. Kelvin's foam rests while its cells are exactly equal: make one of its two sets larger and that set grows at the other's expense, though both have fourteen faces. In the plane the doubled bubble of Figure 5 held still because it kept six sides. In space no number of faces plays the part of six.

Coarsening is not a search for the foams of the earlier parts. Those problems fix the number of cells, make them equal, and ask for the least wall. A coarsening foam keeps neither condition: it sheds film by making its bubbles unequal and removing the small ones. So it can undercut the honeycomb. Under Figure 6, its film runs a few percent below that of a honeycomb of as many equal cells in the same box, which does not contradict Hales's theorem, a statement about equal areas. The rules name no stopping point short of a foam in which every bubble has six sides, and nothing in them steers the foam toward one.
