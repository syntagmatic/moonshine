Random by Design?

The fly's mushroom body was the textbook case of a brain wired at random. A full reconstruction says almost, and how much structure is left over depends on how much of the calyx's geometry the null is allowed to keep.

Every odour a fruit fly smells is first sorted into about fifty channels. Each olfactory receptor neuron expresses, with few exceptions, one kind of odorant receptor and sends its axon to one glomerulus of the antennal lobe, and projection neurons carry each glomerulus's signal on to the mushroom body, the fly's centre for learned smells. There they meet about 2,200 Kenyon cells on each side of the brain. A Kenyon cell's dendrites end in a handful of claws, each wrapped around a single bouton of a single projection neuron, so each Kenyon cell listens to a few glomeruli out of fifty. Which few?

In 2013 Caron and colleagues traced the inputs of 200 Kenyon cells, one per fly, by dye-filling the boutons inside each cell's claws. Each cell had 2 to 11 claws, 7 on average, and the combinations of glomeruli looked random: they showed no organisation by odour tuning, anatomy or developmental origin, and no pair of glomeruli converged more often than in a shuffle that kept each cell's number of inputs and each glomerulus's share of them. Random wiring fitted the theory, too. A layer that mixes a few random inputs and keeps only its most active cells makes odours easier to tell apart while similar odours still get similar codes, and Dasgupta, Stevens and Navlakha later described that step as a hashing algorithm. The mushroom body became the standard example of a brain area wired at random on purpose.

Testing that claim is a null-model question of exactly the kind in part 3: random compared with what, and keeping what fixed? This article reruns the test on the electron-microscopy reconstruction of Zheng and colleagues, who traced 1,356 Kenyon cells in one fly brain, and then asks what the random wiring, or the almost-random wiring the fly actually has, does for odours.

Which inputs share a Kenyon cell

Zheng and colleagues picked Kenyon cells at random where their axons bundle together, traced each one back to all its claws, and identified the projection neuron bouton inside every claw: about 62% of all the claws on the right side of the brain. Their released table lists 6,468 inputs from projection neurons of 54 glomeruli onto 1,354 of the cells, 4.8 per cell. (The other 9% or so of the claws, with input from other kinds of neuron or too few synapses to identify, are not in it.)

A count means little alone: glomeruli with many boutons appear on many cells. So each Cij becomes a z-score against shuffled wirings. The first shuffle swaps glomerulus labels among all claws, so every Kenyon cell keeps its number of claws and every glomerulus its number of claws, the bipartite version of the degree-preserving null of part 3. It is the shuffle Caron and colleagues used, and Zheng's "random claw" model. The second does the same swaps but only among claws in the same patch of the calyx: the figure cuts the 6,468 claws into spatial cells by k-means on their positions, and a label can only move to another claw of its own cell.

Against the whole-calyx shuffle the wiring is not random. A block of glomeruli converges: the ten that Zheng and colleagues called the core community, DM1, DM2, DM3, DM4, DL2v, DP1m, VA2, VA4, VM2 and VM3, have a mean pairwise z of about 3, and every one of them carries food odours by their classification. Across all 2,862 ordered pairs the z-scores spread with a standard deviation of 1.46 rather than 1 (Zheng's figure gives 1.47). With a cruder null that ignores which boutons the claws actually grab, drawing each claw's glomerulus in proportion to its number of boutons, the core block reaches a z above 6. Caron's 200 cells, with about three inputs identified per cell, were too few to see it, and when Zheng and colleagues thinned their own data to that size the community disappeared too.

Some of the excess is the mixture of cell types. Kenyon cells come in three classes, γ, α′β′ and αβ, and the αβ and α′β′ cells take 36 to 40% of their claws from the core glomeruli against 19% for γ cells. Pool them and the core glomeruli turn up together simply because they all favour the same cells. Within any single class the core block's mean z drops to between 1.2 and 1.6. Li and colleagues found the same dependence on cell class in the second fly connectome, the hemibrain.

Now restrict the shuffle in space. With ten cells, whose claws lie a median 8 micrometres from the cell's centre, the core block falls to about 1.8; with fifty, about 1.0; with a hundred, about 0.8. Geometry absorbs most of the excess. But the slider has the same catch as the distance bins of part 3. A shuffle within small cells keeps more of the real wiring by construction, because neighbouring claws often grip the same bouton. The whole-calyx shuffle leaves 2.4% of claws on their own glomerulus, and only by chance; the 50-cell shuffle leaves 16%, the 100-cell one 25%, and with 400 cells 72% of the labels never move. Zheng's own local null, in which each claw picks one of the five boutons nearest to it, works at that fine scale. Rebuilt here from the synapse positions it leaves the core block near 1 and a quarter of the claws on their own glomerulus. Li and colleagues, shuffling the hemibrain's claws within a radius, point out the limit: at radius zero the model is the data.

So the honest reading is narrower than "geometry explains it". Within a few micrometres, which bouton a claw grabs looks like a random draw from the boutons around it. The structure lies in where the boutons and the claws are: food glomeruli's axons cluster in parts of the calyx, and the dendrites of αβ and α′β′ cells grow into those parts. Zheng and colleagues found the Kenyon cells with the most core input grouped into four clusters, consistent with the four neuroblasts that make the mushroom body. That is a developmental programme for territories, not a wiring diagram for individual cells, and a null that keeps the territories cannot say why they are where they are.

What random wiring is good for

Dasgupta and colleagues read the circuit as a hash function. Fifty channels project to some 2,000 Kenyon cells, each summing about six of them; a single inhibitory neuron, APL, feeds back onto all the Kenyon cells and silences all but the most active 5%, which become the odour's tag. Two odours that excite similar channels excite similar Kenyon cells, so their tags overlap, and something learned about one carries over to the other. Computer scientists call a hash with that property locality-sensitive, and their random projections do the same job.

The real responses exist to test it with. In 2006 Hallem and Carlson recorded how neurons expressing each of 24 odorant receptors responded to 110 odours; 23 of those receptors map to one glomerulus each in Zheng's data. Scaling an odour's responses up or down leaves its tag unchanged, so the normalisation the antennal lobe applies on the way doesn't matter here; the 31 glomeruli Hallem and Carlson didn't record contribute nothing.

The tags do what the hashing view predicts. Odour pairs whose receptor responses correlate above 0.8 share about two thirds of their tag; pairs below 0.2 share 12%, against 5% for two random sets of 68 cells. Across all 5,995 pairs the rank correlation between response similarity and tag overlap is 0.88. Ethyl acetate and methyl acetate, whose responses correlate at 0.76, share 62% of their tags; methanol and ethanol, at 0.94, share 77%.

Swap the wiring and almost nothing changes. Shuffled claws give a rank correlation of 0.88 again, the uniform random wiring of the textbook 0.87, and the similar pairs overlap just as much. The difference is at the dissimilar end, and it is small: unrelated odours share 12% of their tags with the real wiring, 10% with shuffled claws and 9% with uniform wiring, so the real circuit tells dissimilar odours apart slightly less well than a random one. Zheng and colleagues found the same in a simulated discrimination task: worse than random unless the signal ran through the overconvergent glomeruli. The cost is not spread evenly. Pairs of odours that both put most of their response into core-community glomeruli overlap 4.5 points more than with shuffled wiring, pairs that barely use them about 2 points more; ethyl acetate and acetic acid share 40% of their tags in the real wiring against 26% in shuffled ones.

Li and colleagues reached a similar verdict on the hemibrain with a different measure: the spatial structure lowers the dimension of the Kenyon cell representation only modestly, though they left open that it might matter for particular kinds of odour. This model has its own limits. It passes receptor neuron firing straight to the projection neurons, ignores how many synapses each claw makes and how the cells' thresholds differ, and sees only the 23 recorded glomeruli and the 62% of claws that were traced; 154 of the 1,354 Kenyon cells have no claw on a recorded glomerulus and never fire. Within those limits, the tags of the real wiring behave like those of a random one, with a little extra overlap among odours that the food glomeruli carry. Whether a fly uses that overlap, to generalise between fruity smells, say, is a question for behaviour experiments, not for the wiring diagram.
