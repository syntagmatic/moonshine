Which Way Signals Flow

A ranking and a simulated spread both turn a recurrent wiring diagram into layers running from the senses to the outputs. The layers hold up; where one ends and the next begins is a setting of the method.

The textbook nervous system runs one way. Sensory neurons pass what they detect to interneurons, interneurons pass it to motor neurons, and motor neurons move the body. A connectome doesn't look like that. In the worm's chemical synapse network, 233 pairs of neurons synapse onto each other in both directions, about four times as many as their numbers of connections would produce at random (part 3 of this series), and there are longer loops beyond the reciprocal pairs. A wiring diagram is recurrent, and the layers of the textbook have to be read out of it by some method.

This article tries two. The first ranks every neuron of the worm so that connections run downhill as often as possible, solving one linear system. The second follows a probabilistic signal from each sense through the 3,016 neurons of a larval fruit fly's brain and records how many steps it takes to arrive. Both find a direction from senses to outputs, well beyond what chance gives. Both also have a dial, and turning it moves neurons from one layer to the next.

A spring on every synapse

SpringRank, introduced by De Bacco, Larremore and Moore in 2018, gives each neuron i a real number si, its height in the hierarchy. Each synapse from i to j is a spring that wants i exactly one unit above j, and the heights are the ones that leave the springs, all together, with the least energy. With Aij synapses from i to j,

The second term is an optional extra spring of stiffness α pulling every neuron toward zero. Setting the gradient to zero gives a linear system whose matrix is a graph Laplacian, the same kind of matrix that gave the spectral order in part 5, plus α on the diagonal:

where Dout and Din hold each neuron's output and input synapse counts. The right side is each neuron's surplus of outputs over inputs. The matrix is sparse, with one off-diagonal entry per connected pair, so the solve takes a few milliseconds for the worm and seconds for networks with millions of edges. With α = 0 only differences in height are fixed, and the solution is chosen with mean zero.

A ranking says nothing on its own: any graph can be ranked. De Bacco and colleagues test it against a null that keeps the number of edges between every pair of nodes and gives each edge a random direction. If the real directions line up with a hierarchy, the real network's springs relax much further than the shuffled ones.

The worm has a hierarchy. At the default setting 89% of its chemical synapses run from a higher neuron to a lower one, against 58% when every synapse's direction is shuffled, and the springs' energy per synapse is 0.20 against 0.48 for the shuffled networks, which never come close. The columns show what the hierarchy is made of. Sensory neurons sit high and motor neurons low, and the boundary between interneurons and motor neurons is sharp: only 3 of the 105 motor neurons rank above the middle interneuron, and a randomly chosen interneuron outranks a randomly chosen motor neuron 94% of the time. The boundary between sensory neurons and interneurons is not. A random sensory neuron outranks a random interneuron only 71% of the time, and 12 of the 73 interneurons sit above the middle sensory neuron, AIN and AIM near the very top because they receive almost no chemical synapses at all.

Pick a neuron and the hierarchy's details come out. The command interneurons AVA, AVB and AVE rank between 171st and 187th of 279, down among the motor neurons they synapse onto. The head motor neurons RMD, SIA and SIB sit lower still, with the motor neurons of the ventral cord. That is the opposite of the spectral order in part 5, which grouped them with the head's sensory neurons by where they connect; SpringRank, which reads direction, groups them by what they do. At the bottom are the VD and DD motor neurons of the ventral cord, which get 84% of their input synapses from the other cord motor neurons and send most of their own output to muscle, in neuromuscular junctions that WormAtlas lists separately and that this neuron-to-neuron graph leaves out. The 11% of synapses that run up the hierarchy include some heavy connections. The heaviest are AVA's synapses onto PVC, another command interneuron, and the head motor neurons SMD and RMD synapsing back onto the interneuron RIA, which sends them 174 synapses and gets 46 in return.

Now raise α. At zero, a neuron attached by a single synapse can float wherever that one spring puts it, and PLML, a touch receptor neuron with one chemical synapse in this reconstruction and no inputs, ranks first. An α of 1 moves it to 21st and 3 to 85th, while the well-connected neurons barely move: regularisation exists for weakly attached nodes, and De Bacco and colleagues note that it matters less as a network gets denser. Push it further and the order changes character. As α grows the heights shrink toward zero in proportion to each neuron's own surplus of outputs over inputs, the right side of the equation, so the ranking forgets the rest of the graph. That local ranking still sends 77% of synapses down, but the full solve sends 89%, and the two orders have a rank correlation of only about 0.6. Most of the hierarchy is in how neurons are arranged relative to each other, not in any one neuron's balance of inputs and outputs.

Gap junctions, the worm's electrical synapses, conduct both ways, so a pair joined by one gets a spring in each direction, which together pull the two neurons level. Including them squeezes the range of heights from 4.2 units to 3.1 and leaves the share running down at 88%. The neurons that move are the ones with many gap junctions and few chemical synapses. DVB falls from 71st to 138th, and PLML, whose three gap junctions outnumber its one chemical synapse, from first to 80th.

Hops from each sense

The worm's 279 neurons make a small ranking problem. The brain of a first-instar Drosophila larva is ten times larger: Winding and colleagues reconstructed 3,016 neurons and about 548,000 synapses from electron microscopy in 2023, 480 of them input neurons and 2,536 brain neurons. Their released matrix holds 2,952 of the neurons, and it splits every synapse by compartment. Connections from an axon onto a dendrite, the classic direction of transmission, make up most of the network; this article uses them alone, 63,545 connections carrying 234,958 synapses, as Winding and colleagues did for their cascades.

Instead of ranking, they let a signal spread. Start with every neuron of one sensory modality active. At each step every active neuron gets one chance to activate each neuron it synapses onto, and each synapse transmits independently with probability p, so a connection of w synapses is crossed with probability

Each neuron can be active only once in a run, so the signal can't circle, and the brain's output neurons (descending neurons to the nerve cord and to the subesophageal zone, and neurons onto the ring gland) receive the signal but don't pass it on. Winding and colleagues chose p = 0.05, ran each cascade 1,000 times for up to eight steps, and counted a neuron as receiving the signal when it was reached in most runs. A neuron's layer for a modality is then the number of hops it took to get there. The figure runs the same model in a background thread.

At the published settings the senses separate by depth. Smell and taste arrive first: olfactory and external gustatory cascades each reach more than 450 brain neurons within two hops, and the neurons they reach take 3.3 to 3.4 hops on average. The proprioceptive and class II/III mechanosensory signals that ascend from the nerve cord, and the respiratory signal, arrive last, at about 6 hops. The descending neurons to the nerve cord, through which the brain reaches the body's motor circuits, follow the same pattern: half of those reached from smell or taste are reached within 4 hops, and from proprioception or respiration within 6. Winding and colleagues likewise found smell and taste to have the shortest paths to the outputs and the ascending somatosensory signals the longest, and that signals reached these descending neurons in 3 to 6 hops and rarely more than 8.

They also found that most brain neurons are multimodal, reached by signals from more than one sense, and that very few, 12% with eight-hop cascades, are reached from only one. The page gets 11.7% at their settings. Now move p. At 0.025 nearly a quarter of the reached neurons are single-modality, and at 0.1 one in twenty. The threshold does the same in the other direction: requiring a neuron to be hit in three quarters of runs rather than half makes 17% of them single-modality, and a quarter of runs makes 6%. Across p from 0.025 to 0.1 and thresholds from a quarter to three quarters, the share runs from 3% to 24%, and the mean hop of the olfactory cascade from 2.8 to 3.9. The order of the senses does not move: smell and the two kinds of taste are the three shallowest at every one of those settings, and the respiratory and the proprioceptive and class II/III mechanosensory signals are always among the four deepest.

The cascade model is one of several ways to assign layers, and Winding and colleagues also used another. Giving each neuron the order of its shortest path from any input neuron, they put 21% of brain neurons at second order, 56% at third, 15% at fourth and fewer than 1% at fifth, with no brain neuron more than four hops from an input. At the published settings the cascades reach some of the same neurons only at the seventh or eighth hop. Both describe the same wiring under different definitions of a step. The paper is explicit about what hasn't been tested: a five-hop pathway, from class IV multidendritic sensory neurons to dopaminergic neurons of the mushroom body, has been shown to work in the living larva, and no six-, seven- or eight-hop pathway has.
