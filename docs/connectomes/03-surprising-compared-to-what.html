Surprising Compared to What?

Every claim that a brain is small-world, reciprocal or full of motifs is a comparison with a random graph. Which random graph you choose decides much of the answer.

In 1998 Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz set the nervous system of the nematode C. elegans beside a network of film actors and the power grid of the western United States, and found the same pattern in all three. Neurons that share a partner tend to be partners themselves: the worm's clustering coefficient was 0.28, against 0.05 for a random graph of the same size and density. Yet a signal could get from any neuron to any other in about as few steps as in the random graph, 2.65 against 2.25. Clustered like a lattice, with paths as short as a random graph's: they called it a small world, and the label has been applied to almost every connectome since.

"Clustered" there means "more clustered than a random graph", and the random graph does a lot of the work. It is a null model: a graph that keeps some features of the real one and scrambles the rest, so that whatever the real graph does beyond it can be credited to what was scrambled. A random graph with the right number of nodes and edges keeps almost nothing. It has no geometry, so it doesn't know that neurons close together are more likely to connect, and its nodes all have about the same number of partners, so it has no hubs. If either of those makes clustering by itself, the comparison credits the worm with structure it got for free.

Below, two classic results, the small world and network motifs, are tested against a sequence of nulls computed in the page, each keeping more of the real graph than the last, to see how much of each result is left.

The worm as a graph

The wiring used here is the reconstruction by Varshney, Chen, Paniagua, Hall and Chklovskii (2011), built on the electron micrographs of White and colleagues from the 1970s and 80s. It has 279 neurons: the hermaphrodite's 302, less the 20 pharyngeal neurons that form a separate network around the feeding organ and three that make no synapses. Between them are 6,394 chemical synapses on 2,194 ordered pairs of neurons, and gap junctions on another 514 pairs. For the small-world question we follow Watts and Strogatz and ignore direction and weight: two neurons are connected if either makes a chemical synapse onto the other. That gives 1,961 connected pairs, 14 partners per neuron on average.

A ladder of nulls

The figure compares the worm with four kinds of random graph. Each dot is one of them, generated and measured in your browser.

Random graphs have 279 nodes and 1,961 edges placed uniformly among all pairs, the null of Watts and Strogatz. Distance graphs know where the neurons are. All 38,781 pairs of neurons are sorted by the distance between their cell bodies and cut into bins holding equal numbers of pairs; each bin gets as many edges as the worm has in it, placed at random within the bin. Near neighbours connect as often as in the worm, but who connects to whom is otherwise chance. Degree graphs are the worm's own edges, rewired by repeatedly taking two edges A–B and C–D and swapping them to A–D and C–B (the procedure of Maslov and Sneppen), so every neuron keeps its number of partners: AVAR keeps its 85, a typical neuron its 14. Degree and distance graphs make the same swaps but keep one only if both new edges fall in the same distance bins as the old ones, so each neuron's degree and the number of edges at each distance are both preserved; Betzel and Bassett used a null of this kind on interareal connectomes in 2018.

Read the clustering column first. The random graphs have C near 0.05, which is just the fraction of all pairs that are connected. Telling the null where the neurons are raises that only to 0.06: nearby neurons are more likely to connect, but not in a way that closes many more triangles. Telling it each neuron's degree raises it to about 0.13. Adding distance on top of degree moves it a little further. The index follows: about 6 against random graphs, 5 against distance, 2.3 against degree and 2.2 against both, all at the default 20 bins.

Degree matters this much because the worm's partners are concentrated on a few neurons. The command interneurons that drive forward and backward crawling (AVA, AVB, AVD, AVE and PVC, each a left and right pair) have 42 to 85 partners each, and many of the same neurons connect to several of them. In a random graph with the worm's degrees, two partners of a typical neuron are often both hubs, and hubs are often joined to each other. Newman worked out how much closure that alone produces. His estimate is for transitivity, a global cousin of C: the fraction of connected triples (a neuron and two of its partners) whose ends are also connected.

For the worm's degrees it gives 0.12; the degree-preserving rewired graphs have 0.11, and the worm itself 0.20. The estimate is rough here, since it lets hubs join each other more than once, which rewiring can't, but both say that about half of the worm's triangle closure is already implied by its degree sequence.

Now move the distance-bin slider. With one bin the distance null is the random graph. With more bins it knows distance more finely, and the distance row barely moves: wherever you set it, distance alone leaves the index near 5. The degree-and-distance row does move, down to about 1.7 at 200 bins. The readout says why. With bins that narrow, a swap is kept only when both new edges land in the same thin band of distances, so few swaps are possible, and the rewired graphs still contain about 40% of the worm's actual connections. (The swaps stop after ten successes per edge or 2,000 attempts per edge; at 200 bins the attempts run out first. Running four times as many brings the share kept down only to about 36%, with the index still below 2.) A null that can change only a little of the graph reproduces most of it, and can push any index toward 1.

One caution about distance. Worm neurons don't connect at their cell bodies: synapses are made along their processes, mostly in the nerve ring and the nerve cords, often far from either cell body. The distance between cell bodies is a crude stand-in for what a connection costs; the next article in the series takes up wire cost directly. Within this crude version, geometry explains little of the worm's clustering, and the degree sequence explains much more. Counting gap junctions as connections lowers each index by a tenth or less and changes none of that.

Motifs and the reciprocity trap

In 2002 Milo and colleagues proposed that small patterns of connection occurring far more often than chance, which they called network motifs, are the building blocks of a network. Three neurons can be wired in 16 different ways when direction counts. Counting how many triples of neurons fall into each class is the triad census, and a class counts as a motif when its count stands well above a null's, measured in units of the null's spread:

Here the null matters at once, because of reciprocity. Of the worm's 2,194 directed connections, 466 come in pairs, A to B and B to A: 233 reciprocal pairs. Keep each neuron's numbers of inputs and outputs and rewire at random, and about 60 reciprocal pairs remain, so the worm has nearly four times as many as its degrees predict. Song and colleagues found a similar excess in 2005 among layer 5 neurons in rat visual cortex, where pairs connected both ways were about four times as common as independent one-way connections would produce. Any count that includes reciprocal pairs inherits that excess.

The figure computes z for each of the 13 classes in which all three neurons are connected, against two nulls. The first keeps each neuron's in- and out-degree. The second also keeps the reciprocal pairs: one-way connections are swapped only with one-way connections and stay one-way, and reciprocal pairs only with reciprocal pairs. That second one is the null Milo and colleagues used.

Against the degrees-only null, most classes that contain a reciprocal pair look enriched. Hold the reciprocal pairs fixed and three of them, 111D, 111U and 201, flip to strongly depleted. Their apparent excess was the reciprocity itself, counted again in every triple that happens to contain a reciprocal pair. The feedforward loop (030T, one neuron driving two others, one of which also drives the other) goes the other way. Against degrees alone it is unremarkable, z about 2; against the null that keeps reciprocal pairs it is one of the strongest motifs in the graph, z above 15. The degrees-only null breaks most of the worm's reciprocal pairs into one-way connections, and those spare one-way connections form feedforward loops by chance, so it expects too many of them.

The depletions have a simple source as well. All three classes that flip are open triads, with one pair of the three unconnected, and once reciprocity is accounted for every open class (021D, 021U, 021C, 111D, 111U and 201) is depleted, while five of the seven closed ones are enriched; of the other two, 120C sits at chance and the cycle 030C is rare against both nulls. This is the worm's high clustering again, seen with direction: partners of a neuron tend to be connected, so triples that leave a pair unconnected are scarce.

Raise the threshold and the graph thins to its stronger connections. At three synapses or more, 745 connections remain, with 29 reciprocal pairs, still more than three times what the degrees predict. The same three classes again go from about zero against degrees to depleted against reciprocal pairs, and the feedforward loop is now enriched against both, with z near 7 and 10. Milo and colleagues reported a z of 3.7 for the feedforward loop in the worm, on an earlier version of the wiring with 252 nodes, counting only connections of five or more synapses. Set the slider to 5 and this graph gives a similar value. That doesn't contradict the 15 here: a z-score belongs to the graph, the null and the threshold together, and it doesn't carry over when any of them changes.

The rich club of Towlson and colleagues (2013) was measured against a degree-preserving null from the start. The worm's highest-degree neurons, the eleven with 44 or more partners when gap junctions count (AVA, AVB, AVD, AVE and PVC on both sides, and DVA), are more connected among themselves than their degrees predict. Of the 55 possible pairs among them, 46 are joined by a synapse or a gap junction; degree-preserving random graphs give about 34. Against a plain random graph, which would expect about 3, the same 46 would look fifteen times larger and say almost nothing about the hubs beyond the fact that they are hubs.
