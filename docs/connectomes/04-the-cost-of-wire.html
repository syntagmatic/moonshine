The Cost of Wire

A worm's neurons sit roughly where the cheapest wiring would put them, and the ones that don't are the interesting ones.

Axons and dendrites take up volume, cost energy to build and run, and slow signals down as they get longer. Santiago Ramón y Cajal argued over a century ago that nervous systems are laid out to save wire. That is a hypothesis about geometry, and a connectome makes it testable. If we know which neurons connect and how strongly, we can work out where a wire-saving animal would put each cell body and compare that with where the cell bodies actually are.

Chen, Hall and Chklovskii did that for C. elegans in 2006. This article rebuilds their calculation on the worm's 2011 wiring, in the page, and then asks the same question at a much larger scale, about the connections between areas of the macaque cortex. In both cases distance accounts for a lot. The useful part is what it leaves out.

Where the neurons would sit

The worm is more than ten times longer than it is wide, so the model treats it as a line. Each neuron gets one number xi, its position between the nose (0) and the tail (1). Three kinds of connection need wire: between neurons that share synapses, from sensory neurons to the sensory endings they serve, and from motor neurons to the muscles they drive. Aij counts the synapses between neurons i and j in either direction, chemical synapses and gap junctions alike. Sik counts a neuron's endings at sensory organ k, and Mil its neuromuscular junctions on muscle l. The constant α is the number of synapses that share one wire. Most worm neurons are unbranched, with one or two processes that make dozens of synapses in passing, so charging every synapse a whole wire would overcount. Chen and colleagues divided by α = 29.3, half the average of 58.6 synapses and neuromuscular junctions per neuron. A sensory ending has a neurite to itself and is not divided.

That holds for all 279 neurons at once, which makes it a system of linear equations with a single solution, and the page solves it directly. Physically it is the resting shape of a network of rubber bands pinned at the nose, the muscles and the tail. The pins matter. Without them every neuron could slide to the same point at zero cost, and part 5 recovers a useful answer from the same sum of squares only by adding constraints, which gives the Fiedler vector. For exponents other than 2 the cost is still convex, and the page minimises it by repeatedly solving a reweighted version of the same system.

The model gets most neurons roughly right. Their predicted positions are on average 9.7% of the body length from the real cell bodies, and half are within 5.2%. A uniformly random layout misses by about 35%. Chen and colleagues report 9.71% and 5.10% with the 2006 version of the wiring. The errors are not spread evenly: neurons tied to a sensory ending or a muscle land on average 7.7% from their cell bodies, while the 80 tied to neither, 56 of them interneurons, land 14.7% away.

The settings move this less than you might expect. Linear cost, ζ near 1, pulls each neuron toward a weighted median of its partners rather than their mean, and higher powers punish the longest wires hardest. The prediction is best near ζ = 2 and worsens slowly on either side, to about 10.5% at 1.5 and at 3. At other exponents α needs retuning, because positions below 1 shrink when raised to a higher power; Chen and colleagues searched both and found the best pair near α = 27 and ζ = 2. Small α lets the synapses dominate and the layout bunches up: at α = 1 the predicted positions spread less than half as widely as the real ones. Large α lets the anchors dominate, and neurons drift toward their endings and muscles with less regard for their partners.

The real worm is not at the optimum. Its layout costs 4.2 times the minimum, and random positions cost about 16 times. Nearly all of the excess is in the wiring between neurons, at 5.8 times its optimal cost, while the wiring to sensory endings and muscles costs only 1.4 times. Chen and colleagues, on the older wiring, put the ratio of optimal to real to random at 1 : 4 : 16, with 6.24 and 0.93 for the two parts. Either way, cell bodies sit close to the endings and muscles they serve, and the extra cost is in how neurons connect to each other.

The neurons that sit somewhere else

Pick AVG in the figure. Its cell body is in the head, at 0.22, but its partners are spread along the ventral nerve cord, and the model puts it at 0.71. AVG is a pioneer neuron: during development it sends the first process down the right side of the ventral cord, and later axons follow it. Its synapses sit along that process, far from its cell body. The model charges every connection as if it were made at the cell body, so it cannot know this. PVP and PVQ are pioneers in the other direction, with cell bodies in the tail and processes that grow forward, and they are among the worst predictions in the worm. Chen and colleagues found every known pioneer of the ventral cord among the outliers.

The costliest neurons are not the pioneers but the command interneurons that drive locomotion: AVA, about 17.5% of the optimum for each of the left and right cells, PVC about 17%, and DVA 13%, followed by DVC, PVQ, PVP and AVG. They are among the most connected neurons in the worm, so every bit of displacement costs more. Chen and colleagues noticed that the command interneurons have mostly input synapses near their cell bodies, which hints at a rule the cost function lacks: inputs and outputs need not be equally cheap to place far from the cell body.

Now press Pin the ten costliest. Ten neurons, under 4% of the worm, take the cost from the optimum to 2.5 times it, nearly half of the way to the real worm's 4.2. Meanwhile the predictions for the other 269 neurons get worse rather than better: their average error rises from 8.4% to 9.3% of the body length. Pinning AVA in the head drags its partners in the cord toward the head, because in the model a connection pulls on cell bodies. In the worm, AVA's synapses are out along its process, next to the cord's motor neurons. The model is wrong about these neurons because it puts their synapses at their cell bodies, not because the worm wastes wire on them. Chen and colleagues went on to place each neuron's synapses along a wire of its own and, with separate rules for pioneers and command interneurons, brought the error down to 9.4%.

Distance and the weight of a pathway

In a mammal the same question applies at a coarser scale. The cortex is divided into areas a few millimetres to a few centimetres across, joined by axons that run through the white matter. Markov and colleagues injected a retrograde tracer into 29 of the 91 areas in their map of the macaque cortex and counted the labelled neurons in every other area. The fraction of extrinsic labelled neurons (FLNe) of a source area is its share of all the labelled neurons outside the injected area. It is a weight for the pathway from source to target, it sums to 1 over the sources of each injection, and it spans about five orders of magnitude. For 11 of the injected areas they also published the distance from every source, measured through the white matter between the centres of the two areas in a three-dimensional atlas brain: 628 pathways with both a weight and a length.

If long wire is expensive, strong pathways should be short. An exponential decay is the simplest form of that, and it is a straight line on a log scale:

The line captures the trend. Pooled over all 628 pathways the best decay constant is 0.16 per millimetre: weight falls by a factor of e every 6.3 mm and tenfold every 14 mm. Ercsey-Ravasz and colleagues, fitting the distribution of labelled neurons over distance rather than one weight per pathway, found 0.188 per millimetre and called the result the exponential distance rule. The two estimates measure slightly different things and land close together.

But distance explains only 26% of the variance in log weight. At any distance the pathways scatter about a decade either side of the line: the residual standard deviation is 1.07 decades, against 1.24 for the weights themselves. Giving each target area its own height raises the share explained to 32%, and its own slope as well, to 37%. Drag your line away from the best fit and the share falls off slowly, which is another way of saying the data don't pin the slope tightly.

The slopes differ between targets. Inputs to MT, V2 and TEO, visual areas, fall off fastest, at 0.25 to 0.30 per millimetre. Inputs to the frontal area 8l and the parietal area 7A fall off at about 0.08, a decay length of 12 mm, so these areas take a much larger share of their input from far away. Distance sets the overall scale of cortical wiring, and how far an area reaches beyond that scale is a property of the area.

The two models make the same argument at very different scales: connections cost wire, so connected things should be close. In the worm that places most cell bodies within a few percent of the body length, and in the macaque it sets the scale over which pathway weights fall. Where they fail they point at something specific: neurons that make their synapses far from their cell bodies, and cortical areas that reach farther than their neighbours.
