Reading the Matrix

An adjacency matrix looks like whatever order its rows are in, and a statistic computed from one can turn out to be degree under another name.

Most pictures of a connectome are matrices. Each neuron gets a row and a column, and the cell where row i meets column j is filled when i makes a synapse onto j. The picture is a faithful record of the graph, but it carries one extra choice that the graph doesn't contain: the order of the rows. Put the same neurons in a different order, moving each column with its row, and the graph is unchanged while the picture can go from noise to a clean diagonal band.

That makes a matrix figure an argument as much as a record. A block that looks like a module may be there because the rows were sorted by a label that already groups the neurons, and a diagonal band may mean the neurons were sorted by something their wiring follows. This article tries six orders on one graph and asks which one the wiring itself prefers. Then it turns to a statistic computed from the same matrix, average controllability, and checks it against the simplest thing a node has, its number of connections.

Six orders of one graph

The graph is the worm's chemical synapse network from the previous articles: 279 neurons of C. elegans and 2,194 ordered pairs joined by at least one synapse (Varshney and colleagues, 2011). To compare orders we need a number for how compact a picture is. A random order puts the two ends of a connection at independent random positions, and two random positions among n are on average (n + 1)/3 apart: 93 rows for the worm. An order that keeps connected neurons close does better.

Five of the orders come from labels: names in alphabetical order, neurons with the most partners first, sensory then inter then motor neurons, the position of each cell body along the body from head to tail, and a random shuffle. Alphabetical order already beats random, at about 71, because worm neuron names encode their class and side: AVAL sits next to AVAR, and the ventral cord motor classes VA, VB and VD sort near each other. Sorting by degree does little better than chance, about 80. Cell type does better, about 65, and body position better still, about 62: neurons of the same type, and neurons whose cell bodies are near each other, do tend to connect. The spectral order halves that, to 33, and its picture is a band hugging the diagonal with the long-range connections of a few hubs crossing it.

The order the wiring prefers looks a lot like the body. Watch the position strip as the rows move into spectral order: the dots drift from scatter into a rough diagonal, and the readout gives a rank correlation with body position of about 0.78. One end of the spectral order is the head's sensory and motor neurons (SIBDL, OLQVL, IL1VL, URAVL), the other the motor neurons of the posterior ventral cord (VB09, VD10, VD09). It is not a sensory-to-motor axis. Sensory neurons do sit earlier on average and motor neurons later, but mostly because sensory neurons are in the head and motor neurons in the cord: the head's own motor neurons (RME, RMD, SIA and SIB) sit near the head end, ahead of almost all the interneurons.

Where the two orders disagree is informative too. The neurons the spectral order moves farthest from their cell bodies are the ones with long processes. ALN and PLN have cell bodies in the tail, but every one of their chemical synapses is with a neuron whose cell body is in the head; the spectral order puts them among head neurons, where their partners are. The command interneurons AVA, whose cell bodies are in the head, go the other way, toward the ventral cord motor neurons that receive about four fifths of their synapses. A cell body is where a neuron keeps its nucleus, not where it connects, and the order computed from the connections finds where each neuron's wiring sits.

Spectral order is a computed answer with its own settings, not a neutral one. Weight the Laplacian by synapse count and the Fiedler vector changes character: it concentrates on a few neurons attached to the rest by only a handful of synapses, because cutting those off is the cheapest way to split a weighted graph, and the order it gives no longer follows the body (its rank correlation with body position falls to about 0). Normalising the Laplacian by degree, as is usual for weighted graphs, brings the body axis back. Even the order the graph prefers depends on how you ask it.

A statistic that is mostly degree

Matrices also feed statistics, and a new statistic deserves the same suspicion as a new picture. In 2015 Gu and colleagues brought a measure from control theory to brain networks. where ei is the input at node i and the matrix has been scaled so its largest eigenvalue is below 1 and activity dies away. The average controllability of node i is the trace of the controllability Gramian, which for this system is the total squared activity that a unit pulse at i produces over the time horizon T:

Nodes with high average controllability are said to be able to push the system into many easily reached states. The expansion shows what that number contains. The first term is 1 for every node: the pulse itself. The second is the sum of the squared weights of the node's connections, which for an unweighted graph is its degree. Later terms count longer walks, and the scaling makes them shrink with each step. So unless the later terms are large, average controllability is a restatement of degree.

Start at a horizon of one step and every neuron scores exactly 1: the pulse never leaves its neuron. At two steps the score is 1 plus the scaled sum of squared weights, and with weights ignored the points fall on a single curve, a rank correlation of exactly 1 with degree. As the horizon grows the later terms join in, the cloud spreads, and the correlation falls, but slowly. Across the whole grid, horizons from 2 to infinite and eigenvalues from 0.5 to 0.999, the rank correlation never drops below about 0.6, and the ordinary correlation, the one usually reported, stays between about 0.8 and 0.93. Ignore weights and both stay above about 0.74. At the default setting the neurons that score highest are the command interneurons AVA, PVC and AVD, which are also among the neurons with the most synapses.

The lowest correlations sit in one corner: an infinite horizon with the largest eigenvalue scaled close to 1. That corner has its own explanation. When the leading eigenvalue is near 1 its term, 1/(1 − λ2), dwarfs the others, and the score becomes the square of each node's entry in the leading eigenvector. That is eigenvector centrality, another standard measure of how connected a node is. Short horizons and small eigenvalues give degree; long horizons and large eigenvalues give eigenvector centrality. The scaling used in later papers and in the standard code, dividing by one plus the largest eigenvalue, puts that eigenvalue at 0.991 for this weighted network, close to the corner.

None of this was hidden. Gu and colleagues reported a correlation of 0.91 between average controllability and strength, both ranked, across 234 regions of the human brain, and explained it in their supplement with the same first-order expansion. In 2018 Tu and colleagues showed that randomised networks with no brain-like structure give the same relationship. The measure is exact and does what control theory says it does in a linear model. But a finding that high-controllability regions are special has to show that they are special beyond their degree. Before crediting a new statistic, plot it against degree.

Modules smaller than the network can see

The most common summary of a connectivity matrix is a partition into modules, usually found by maximising Newman and Girvan's modularity. For a partition into modules s, with ls edges inside module s, ds the total degree of its nodes, and L edges in all,

The first term is the share of edges inside the module, and the second is the share expected if the same degrees were wired at random. Modularity is itself a comparison with the degree-preserving null of the previous article. It has a blind spot, found by Fortunato and Barthélemy in 2007, and a ring of cliques shows it cleanly.

Five-node cliques, every pair inside joined, each clique linked to the next by one edge. Shading shows whichever partition has the higher modularity: one module per clique, or adjacent cliques merged in pairs. Modularity is computed from the formula above.

No module could be clearer than a clique: every pair inside it is connected, and only two edges leave it. Yet past 22 cliques, modularity scores the partition that merges adjacent cliques in pairs above the one that keeps them separate. The algebra is short. Each clique of k nodes has k(k − 1)/2 edges inside it and one edge to each neighbour. Merging two adjacent cliques gains that one connecting edge as an internal edge and pays a larger expected term, and the gain wins when the number of cliques m exceeds k(k − 1) + 2, which is 22 for five-node cliques. The cause is the null term. Its expected count of edges between two small modules shrinks as the network grows, until even a single edge between them is more than the null predicts, and modularity counts that edge as evidence the two belong together.

Fortunato and Barthélemy showed this is general: whether modularity can resolve a module depends on the size of the whole network, not on how well separated the module is. The rough threshold is a module with fewer internal edges than about the square root of 2L. For the worm's chemical graph, with 1,961 connections when direction is ignored, that is about 63 edges. Ten neurons all connected to each other make only 45. A partition of a connectome found by maximising modularity has a floor on the size of module it can report, set by the size of the network.
