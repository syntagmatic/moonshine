Weyl Reflections: the Computational Engine

Part 5 of 15. One formula moves any root to any other root. Repeatedly applying it starting from one simple root generates all 240. This is the operation every later explainer runs on.

So far we have a set — the 240 roots — and a diagram — the 8 simple roots and their adjacency graph. What we don't yet have is a way to move inside the set. This explainer supplies the move. It is a single formula, a single line of code, and it is the operation that every subsequent explainer will use when it says "apply this transformation to E8." By the end of the explainer you will have seen that starting from any one root and repeatedly applying the move, you reach all 240. That's the useful thing: in a root system this symmetric, one vector and one operation regenerate the whole structure.

Read it as a recipe: given a vector x and an axis α, compute the scalar , then subtract that many copies of α from x. The result is x flipped across the hyperplane perpendicular to α.

Three things to notice about this formula.

(1) It is linear in x. Applying a reflection is a matrix multiply.

(2) It is an involution: applying the same reflection twice returns x to its original position, because the formula is its own inverse.

(3) If α is a root of E8 and x is any root of E8, then sα(x) is also a root of E8. (This is a property of the root system, not of the formula.)

A single reflection in two dimensions

The six roots of the A2 root system form a regular hexagon in the plane. Pick any axis and flip everything across it: three roots stay put (the two on the axis and its negative) and the other four swap in pairs. Play with it to see the mechanism at work before we scale up to 8D.

Figure 2. A single simple reflection applied to all 240 roots. Top panel shows the roots before reflection (indigo); bottom shows them after (amber). Each reflection is its own inverse: applying it twice gives back the original.

Every simple reflection moves exactly 112 roots and fixes exactly 128. (Or maybe 128 and 112, depending on whether α is integer or half-integer.) The fixed roots are those perpendicular to α, and there are 126 of them plus the 2 that equal ±α — 128 total. We'll leave the counts as a verifiable detail and focus on the bigger question: what happens if we apply many reflections?

The orbit: how 240 comes out of 1

Here is the real payoff. Start with a single root — α1, say. Apply some simple reflection, get a new root. Apply another simple reflection, get another new root. Keep going. How many distinct roots can you reach? Answer: all 240. Every root of E8 is reachable from every other root by a sequence of simple reflections. In the language of group theory, the Weyl group acts transitively on the root system.

The Weyl group and its size

Simple reflections are not quite the whole story. Every composition of simple reflections — any word in s1, …, s8 — is also a symmetry of E8's root system. The full set of such compositions forms a group, the Weyl group of E8, written W(E8).

696,729,600
= 214 · 35 · 52 · 7

Figure 4. The order of the Weyl group W(E8). Compare: An has |W| = (n+1)!, so A8 has 362,880 — E8 is about 1,920 times larger. D8 has |W| = 27·8! = 5,160,960 — E8 is about 135 times larger.

The Weyl group is enormous, but every single element of it is a specific composition of at most a few dozen simple reflections. The formula is the only operation at play; the 696-million-element group emerges from iterating that one operation.

What we take with us

  • Reflection formula. for E8 (squared length 2). Linear in x, an involution, and maps roots to roots.
  • Weyl group. The group of compositions of simple reflections. Order 696,729,600 = 214 · 35 · 52 · 7.
  • Transitive action. The Weyl group acts transitively on the 240 roots: starting from one root, you can reach every other by applying simple reflections.
  • Computational heart. Every later explainer's interactive math — rotating projections, peeling subsystems, tracking Petrie polygons — is built on the reflect-and-orbit primitives from this explainer.
