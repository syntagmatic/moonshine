Meet E8: the 240 Roots

Part 3 of 10. Two families of vectors, 112 + 128 = 240, every vector of squared length 2, every pairwise inner product in {−2, −1, 0, 1, 2}. Nothing mystical, all checkable.

E8 has a reputation for being hard. It isn't. It is a specific finite set of 240 vectors in , and you can write them all down on a single page. The vectors come in two families: 112 integer vectors that you already met as the D8 minimum vectors, and 128 half-integer vectors that are new. The sum is 240. Every vector in the combined set has squared length 2, and every pair has inner product in a tiny discrete set. The whole structure can be verified by hand.

By the end of this explainer you will have a coordinate workbench in front of you: pick any two of the 240 vectors, see their coordinates side by side, watch the inner product and squared length compute live. The workbench is the point of the explainer. Everything before it is setup.

The first family: 112 integer vectors

From the previous explainer we know the minimum vectors of D8. They are all integer vectors in with exactly two nonzero coordinates, each chosen to be . Counting: choose two positions out of eight (28 ways), then choose signs for the two nonzero entries (4 ways), giving vectors.

Every integer root has squared length . This is the entire family — nothing hidden.

The second family: 128 half-integer vectors

The new family is the one that turns D8 (with 112 minimal vectors) into E8 (with 240). These are the vectors whose eight coordinates are all equal to , subject to one constraint: the number of negative signs must be even.

Let's count. Eight coordinates, each ±½, gives sign patterns. Exactly half of these have an even number of minus signs (a standard parity argument), so the half-integer family has vectors.

The squared length of any half-integer vector is — the same as an integer root. So every vector in both families has squared length 2, which will be the uniform minimum squared length of the combined 240-vector set.

128 half-integer roots displayed as polylines. Every coordinate is ±½.

Figure 2. The 128 half-integer roots. Every polyline visits both horizontal lines at ±½ across all 8 axes — no zeros anywhere. The constraint "even number of minuses" halves the possible sign patterns.

Why the even-minus constraint? Without it, we would have 256 half-integer vectors, and the resulting set would not be a root system: some inner products would fall outside the tight {−2, −1, 0, 1, 2} window we are about to verify. The even-sign constraint picks out exactly the 128 vectors that fit. The odd-sign counterparts are related to E8 as well, but they are not roots — they live in a different lattice coset. We'll mention them again when we build the E8 lattice in explainer 10.

Combining: 112 + 128 = 240

Put the two families together and you have E8's root system:

That is the complete recipe. The two families together — and nothing else — form the set of 240 roots. Every root has squared length 2; every pair of roots has an inner product drawn from a very short list. The "structure" of E8 that will fill the next twelve explainers is, at the vector-by-vector level, already determined by these two formulas.

Try a few specific pairs to convince yourself of the structural claims:

  • Pick root 0 (the integer root (1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0)) and root 1 (which will be (1, −1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0)). Their inner product is 1 − 1 = 0 — they are perpendicular.
  • Pick root 0 and root 2 ((−1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0)). Inner product = −1 + 1 = 0. Still perpendicular.
  • Pick root 0 and root 3 ((−1, −1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0)). Inner product = −1 − 1 = −2. This is as negative as it gets, and it means root 3 equals −root 0.
  • Pick root 0 and root 112 (the first half-integer root, (½, ½, ½, ½, ½, ½, ½, ½)). Inner product = ½ + ½ + 0 + … = 1.

Try to find two roots whose inner product is NOT in {−2, −1, 0, 1, 2}. You won't — but going through enough pairs to believe it is what makes the constraint memorable.

Four bars occupied, one empty. Every pair of distinct roots sits in one of the buckets −2, −1, 0, 1. The −2 bucket is exactly 120 pairs: the 120 antipodal pairs (α, −α). The +2 bucket is empty because no two distinct roots can have inner product 2 (that would force them equal). The zero bucket is largest by far: more than half of all pairs of roots are perpendicular, which makes the geometry of the 240 roots surprisingly open.

What we take with us

The root system of E8 is concrete, small enough to enumerate, and can be verified with plain arithmetic. Four claims carry forward:

  • 240 vectors. 112 integer vectors of the form (±1, ±1, 0, …, 0), plus 128 half-integer vectors of the form (±½, …, ±½) with an even number of minuses. The two families are disjoint and together form the root system.
  • Squared length 2. Every single one of the 240 vectors has squared length exactly 2. This is the "minimum-length shell" of the E8 lattice.
  • Inner products in {−2, −1, 0, 1, 2}. Every pair of distinct roots has an inner product drawn from this five-element set. Most pairs have inner product 0.
  • Closure. The set is closed under sign flips (−α is a root whenever α is) and under certain reflections (next explainer defines the operation).

We now have something computable, countable, and visible. The next explainer shows how all 240 of these vectors can be generated from just 8 of them, using one formula applied in a small diagram.
