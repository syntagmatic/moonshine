Climbing Dimensions: the Dn Family

Part 2 of 15. We generalize 2D and 3D into a family of lattices that works in any dimension — then discover the pattern breaks at dimension 8.

The hexagonal lattice is optimal in two dimensions. The face-centered cubic is optimal in three. Both are instances of a single family that works in every dimension, called . This explainer defines the family, computes its kissing number in closed form, watches the numbers climb, and notes the one place where the climb stops matching the best answer.

One rule, every dimension

The Dn lattice is the set of integer points in whose coordinates sum to an even number:

That is the entire definition. One rule: the coordinate sum must be even. A point like has coordinate sum 2 and is in D3; a point like has coordinate sum 3 and is excluded. Dn is a sublattice of of index 2: exactly half of all integer points satisfy the rule.

Let's check a small case. In two dimensions, D2 consists of all integer points (a, b) with a + b even. Those points form a sublattice — specifically, a square lattice rotated by 45° and scaled by , with minimum vector length . In three dimensions, D3 is a lattice that turns out to be equivalent (up to rotation and scaling) to the face-centered cubic we saw in explainer 1. Same lattice, different recipe.

Counting minimal vectors

In any dimension, the minimal vectors of Dn all look the same: two coordinates are , all others are zero. The squared length is then , which is the minimum. So counting the minimal vectors reduces to:

  1. Choose which two of the n coordinates are nonzero: ways.
  2. Choose signs for each of the two nonzero coordinates: ways.

Let's verify this for the dimensions we already know. For n = 2 the formula gives 2·2·1 = 4, matching the four minimal vectors of a (rotated) square lattice. For n = 3 it gives 2·3·2 = 12, matching FCC's kissing number. For n = 4 it gives 2·4·3 = 24. We'll see that number — 24 — a lot in dimension 4.

D₄ has 24 minimal vectors. Each has two coordinates set to ±1 and the rest zero.

The figure uses parallel coordinates: each n-dimensional vector becomes a polyline that touches axis k at height equal to its k-th coordinate. A minimal vector of Dn has exactly two spikes (at ±1) and the rest of its polyline runs along zero. This is the first time in the series that we give up trying to draw a high-dimensional object as points in space and instead draw its coordinates as polylines. The convention will come back in explainer 3 when we meet the 240 roots of E8.

Climbing

With a closed-form formula we can tabulate the kissing numbers for Dn and compare them against what is known to be the best kissing number in each dimension. The comparison is where the D-family stops being the answer.

Dn climbs as 2n(n−1). In dimensions 3, 4, 5 it is proven optimal. In 6, 7, 8, something beats it.

Figure 2. Kissing numbers climbing with dimension. Blue: Dn's count 2n(n−1). Red: best known kissing number. For n = 3, 4, 5 the two lines overlap. For n = 6, 7, 8, the red line jumps ahead. The gap at n = 8 is the largest: D8 gives 112, but the best known answer is 240.

Where the D-family stops winning. In dimensions 3, 4, 5, the D-family achieves the proven optimal kissing number. In dimension 6 the best-known lattice is not D6 (which gives 60) but the exceptional lattice E6, which gives 72. In dimension 7, E7 gives 126 instead of D7's 84. In dimension 8, E8 gives 240 instead of D8's 112. That last gap is the largest — a jump of 128 additional minimal vectors.

Where do those extra 128 vectors come from? D8's minimal vectors are integer vectors with two nonzero coordinates. The 128 new vectors that push the count to 240 are not integer vectors at all. They are vectors whose coordinates are all ±½, with an even number of minuses. We meet them in the next explainer.

Why Dn is sometimes the right answer

The D-family is not an accident. It is the unique lattice that generalizes both the 2D and 3D patterns cleanly: "take integer points whose coordinate sum is even." Its Weyl group — the group of symmetries that map the lattice to itself — has order , and for n ≥ 3 this makes Dn a quite symmetric object.

The optimality proofs are hard. For n = 3 (where D3 ≅ FCC), the result was conjectured by Kepler in 1611 and proven by Hales in 1998 for packing density, and by Schütte and van der Waerden in 1953 for kissing number. For n = 4 the kissing-number optimality was proven by Musin in 2003. For n = 5 it was proven alongside the 4D result by different methods. For n ≥ 6, proving optimality of any specific lattice kissing number is open in most dimensions.

A first look at D4, which we will leave behind

D4 deserves special mention. It has 24 minimal vectors, which happens to equal the number of vertices of a particularly beautiful 4D polytope called the 24-cell. The 24 vertices of the 24-cell are exactly the 24 minimal vectors of D4. This is the only dimension where a "self-dual regular 4-polytope" exists outside the usual families, and for that reason D4 is itself an exceptional object — just not exceptional enough to beat the E-family once we climb past it.

We will not linger on D4. The point of this explainer was to give you a lattice family that works in every dimension, watch it climb, and see where it stops being the answer. Now we have our baseline: D8 offers 112 minimal vectors in 8 dimensions, and something else offers 240. The next explainer writes down that something else, vector by vector.

What we take with us

  • Dn definition. Integer vectors in whose coordinates sum to an even number.
  • Dn kissing number: 2n(n−1). Derived by counting the (±1, ±1, 0, …, 0) vectors.
  • Parallel coordinates. A way to draw high-dimensional vectors as polylines across a set of parallel axes, one per dimension. Minimal vectors of Dn show up as two-spike polylines.
  • The gap at dimension 8. D8 has 112 minimal vectors. The best lattice in dimension 8 has 240. The extra 128 come from half-integer vectors, and the resulting lattice is E8.
