Lattices and Kissing Numbers

Part 1 of 15. We start in familiar dimensions — 2D and 3D — to build the vocabulary the rest of the series runs on.

This series is about a specific object called E8 that lives in eight dimensions. Before meeting it, we need words for two questions it answers well. Both questions can be asked in any dimension, but both are easiest to see in dimensions 2 and 3, where we can draw them.

The first question: how tightly can equal spheres pack without overlapping? The second: how many spheres can simultaneously touch a single central sphere? These sound like the same question — and they are related — but they are not the same. The arrangement that packs most tightly is not always the one whose central sphere has the most neighbors. Distinguishing them cleanly is the first thing we need to get right.

What is a lattice?

A lattice in is a discrete, periodic set of points generated by taking all integer combinations of some fixed set of vectors. In two dimensions, the simplest lattice is the square lattice : all points with integer coordinates. Pick any point; its closest neighbors sit at distance 1 in the four cardinal directions.

A different 2D lattice is the hexagonal lattice: the one you get from stacking pennies on a table. Its points sit at the vertices of a honeycomb's hexagons. Each point has six equidistant nearest neighbors, arranged at 60° intervals around it.

Every lattice has a minimum vector length: the length of the shortest nonzero vector you can reach from the origin. For the square lattice that length is 1. For the hexagonal lattice, with the basis we drew, it's also 1. The minimal vectors — the vectors achieving this length — are what we'll be counting all series.

Kissing in 2D

If we place a disk of radius at every lattice point, two disks at neighboring points just touch when the distance between them equals twice the radius. At the minimum distance, each disk is kissed by one disk from each direction a minimal vector points to. The number of minimal vectors at the origin is called the kissing number of the lattice.

Square lattice kissing number: 4. Hexagonal lattice kissing number: 6.

Six is more than four, and in two dimensions that is as high as you can go. Prove it to yourself: if seven or more disks of equal radius could all touch a central one, the arc each occupies on the central disk's circumference would average less than 60°, but the minimum angle between two touching disks is exactly 60° (any less and they'd overlap). So the maximum is six.

Kissing is a local question. We are asking how crowded the immediate neighborhood of one point can get. This is different from asking how tightly we can fill all of space with disks, which is a global question. We will see in a moment that the two questions can have different answers.

Packing density: a different question

Imagine covering the entire plane with equal non-overlapping disks. What fraction of the plane's area do the disks occupy? The fraction that gets filled is called the packing density. For each lattice, we compute it by placing a disk of maximal radius at every lattice point and dividing the disk's area by the area of a fundamental cell.

Both numbers are close to 1, but the hexagonal lattice wins: it fills about 90.7% of the plane, versus the square lattice's 78.5%. In 1773, Lagrange proved the hexagonal lattice is the densest lattice packing in the plane, and in 1940, Fejes Tóth extended the result to all packings, not just lattice ones.

In the plane, the same lattice (hexagonal) wins both optimization problems — it has the best kissing number and the best packing density. This convenient coincidence does not survive the climb to higher dimensions.

The 3D jump: face-centered cubic

In three dimensions the natural analog of the square lattice is the cubic lattice , with integer coordinates and kissing number 6 — each point touches its six axis-direction neighbors. But the best 3D lattice is a subtler construction called the face-centered cubic (FCC). It consists of all points in together with points at the centers of each cube face. Equivalently, it's the set of points whose three coordinates are either all integers or all half-integers, with the constraint that their sum is an integer.

In the FCC lattice, the minimal vectors are , and there are twelve of them. The twelve neighbors of the origin sit at the centers of the faces of a cuboctahedron. FCC's kissing number is 12.

Is 12 the best possible kissing number in 3D? Kepler conjectured yes in 1611, while studying stacked cannonballs for his patron. The question was not settled until 1953, when Schütte and van der Waerden gave the first proof that no arrangement — lattice or otherwise — can exceed 12. A second proof due to Leech in 1956 strengthened the result. So the 3D kissing champion is 12, achieved by FCC.

About 74%. Like the 2D case, FCC wins both the kissing and the density competitions in 3D. Kepler conjectured in 1611 that FCC is the densest sphere packing; Hales proved it in 1998 via a massive case analysis and a long verification. So in 3D, kissing and packing are still aligned: the best lattice for one is the best for the other.

Several things in this table are striking. Most dimensions have open problems — we think we know the best lattice, but we can't prove it. And there is a curious structural break around dimension 8: the numbers grow smoothly from dimension 4 (24) to dimension 7 (126), then jump to 240 at dimension 8, a much larger increase than the pattern suggests. Dimension 24 shows an even bigger surprise, jumping to 196,560.

Dimensions 8 and 24 are not normal. Their kissing numbers are proven optimal, they achieve the densest possible sphere packings (the 8D result was proven in 2016 by Maryna Viazovska, who won the Fields Medal for it; the 24D result followed two weeks later by Cohn, Kumar, Miller, Radchenko, and Viazovska together). The lattice behind the dimension-8 entry is E8. The lattice behind dimension 24 is the Leech lattice, which is closely related to E8 in ways we'll see in the last act of the series.

In dimensions 2 through 5, this family is the best we know how to build. Then, in dimension 8, something remarkable happens: a better lattice exists, with twice as many minimal vectors as the D-family provides. What we take with us

From this explainer, four words and three facts travel forward into every explainer that follows.

  • Lattice. A discrete, periodic set of points in , closed under addition.
  • Minimal vector. A shortest nonzero vector of the lattice. The set of minimal vectors is central to everything that follows.
  • Kissing number. The number of minimal vectors; equivalently, the number of spheres touching a central sphere in the packing.
  • Packing density. The fraction of space filled when we place a ball of maximal radius at every lattice point.
  • In 2D, the hexagonal lattice is optimal for both kissing (6) and density (~90.7%).
  • In 3D, the face-centered cubic is optimal for both kissing (12) and density (~74%).
  • In higher dimensions, these two questions can have different answers, and most of them are still open.
