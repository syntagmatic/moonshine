The Gosset Polytope 4₂₁

Part 6 of 15. The 240 roots are not a loose cloud — they are the vertices of a single 8-dimensional uniform polytope with edges, faces, and cells we can count and visualize.

So far we have treated E8's 240 roots as a set: 240 vectors you can list. But the set has combinatorial structure. Certain pairs of roots are "neighbors" in a geometrically meaningful sense, those neighbor relations form edges, the edges bound faces, the faces bound cells, and the whole tower of incidences defines a specific uniform polytope in 8 dimensions — the Gosset polytope 421. Discovered by Thorold Gosset in 1900, it is the largest member of a small family of exceptional uniform polytopes and it is the "natural habitat" of the E8 root system. This explainer gives the polytope its edges, projects a 3D shadow you can rotate, and walks through its face counts.

Which pairs are neighbors?

Two roots α and β are connected by an edge of the Gosset polytope iff their inner product is exactly 1. Since every root has squared length 2, the angle between neighboring roots is:

Sixty degrees. From any fixed root, the neighbors are exactly those roots at a 60° angle from it. How many such neighbors does a given root have? From the inner-product histogram in explainer 3, we know each root has the same distribution of angles with the others:

With 240 vertices each of degree 56, the total number of edges is:

Six thousand seven hundred and twenty edges. That is a lot of edges for an 8-dimensional object whose vertex set we can write down by hand, and it is one of the reasons the Gosset polytope is visually striking in any projection — even a poor one.

Vertex figure: the neighborhood of any root

The vertex figure of a polytope is the shape you get when you take the edge neighbors of a single vertex and ask what polytope they form. For the Gosset polytope 421, the 56 neighbors of any vertex are themselves the vertex set of a smaller uniform polytope called 321 — which happens to be the root polytope of E7. The naming convention 421, 321, 221 comes from Gosset's 1900 enumeration and encodes the branch structure of the underlying Dynkin diagram: the three subscripts count the three arms of the E8-family Dynkin diagram.

We're not going to rigorously build the vertex figure here — the claim "56 neighbors form the E7 root polytope 321" can be verified by computing pairwise inner products and checking they match E7's root system, but that's work for a dedicated E7 explainer. For now, take it as a promise: the local neighborhood of any E8 root is an E7 root system, and the polytope's face structure is a recursive refinement of that same pattern.

The bottom row is notable: 19,440 facets split into two orbits — 17,280 copies of the 7-simplex and 2,160 copies of the 7-cross-polytope — confirming that 421 is a uniform polytope, not regular. Regular would require one facet type; uniform allows multiple.

Gosset's 1900 enumeration

The Gosset polytope gets its name from Thorold Gosset's 1900 paper On the Regular and Semi-Regular Figures in Space of n Dimensions, which enumerated all uniform polytopes in dimensions 4 through 8. Gosset discovered that there are exactly three exceptional uniform polytopes that arise from root systems of rank ≥ 6: 221 (the E6 polytope, 27 vertices), 321 (the E7 polytope, 56 vertices), and 421 (the E8 polytope, 240 vertices). They form a short family, indexed by the exceptional Lie groups E6, E7, E8.

Gosset's work was rediscovered and extended by Elte and Coxeter, who gave us the modern combinatorial treatment, the Coxeter-plane projection we'll see in explainer 8, and the broader classification of uniform polytopes we rely on today. The 421 polytope still makes appearances in contemporary physics (the gauge structure of E8×E8 heterotic strings, for instance), but its original form — as a combinatorial record of the 240 roots' incidence structure — is still the most honest way to hold it.

What we take with us

  • Gosset polytope 421. A uniform 8-dimensional polytope whose vertex set is exactly the 240 roots of E8.
  • Edge rule. Two roots share an edge iff their inner product is 1 (angle 60°). Each vertex has degree 56, and the polytope has 6,720 edges.
  • Vertex figure. The 56 neighbors of any vertex form an E7 root polytope 321. The local structure of E8 at any root is an E7.
  • Face counts. All from Gosset (1900). Facets split into 17,280 7-simplices and 2,160 7-cross-polytopes — 421 is uniform, not regular.
