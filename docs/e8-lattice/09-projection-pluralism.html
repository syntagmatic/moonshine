Projection Pluralism

Part 9 of 15. The Coxeter plane is one shadow out of infinitely many. Here you get to rotate the projection plane yourself and watch E8 deform in real time.

Explainer 8 built the Coxeter plane — a specific 2-plane in ℝ8 that produces the iconic 8-rings-of-30 mandala. The Coxeter plane is canonical, but it is one shadow among infinitely many. The space of 2-planes in ℝ8 is a 12-dimensional manifold called the Grassmannian Gr(2, 8); each point in that Grassmannian is a different projection, and different projections reveal different structure in E8.

This explainer gives you a tool for moving inside the Grassmannian. Two sliders parameterize a 2D slice through the 12-dimensional space; a set of preset buttons jumps to specific planes that lie elsewhere. You watch the 240 roots rearrange themselves in real time. The lesson is not "here is another picture" — it is that every picture of E8 is a choice, and moving between pictures is the exercise of genuinely seeing 8 dimensions.

What the sliders do

At slider positions (0, 0), the projection plane is the Coxeter plane. Starting from that baseline, slider 1 ("tilt") applies a Givens rotation in the plane spanned by u (the first basis vector of the Coxeter plane) and a specific vector w1 orthogonal to both u and v. Slider 2 ("twist") does the same with v and a different orthogonal vector w2.

At slider position (θ, φ) = (0, 0), the rotated basis is just (u, v) — the Coxeter plane. At (π, 0), u has been flipped to −u (still in the Coxeter plane). At (π/2, 0), u has been replaced by w1 — so the projection plane is now (w1, v), which is not the Coxeter plane and typically has much less symmetry.

Why this is only a 2D window. The Grassmannian Gr(2, 8) has dimension 12. Two sliders expose 2 of those 12 dimensions; the other 10 dimensions of freedom are frozen at values we chose when we picked w1 and w2. So the "slice" you can reach with the sliders is 2-dimensional. To reach projections outside the slice, you use the preset buttons — they set the projection plane directly and the sliders grey out, signaling that you're now in a region they don't control.

What to notice

A few patterns worth seeking:

  • At (0, 0): the Coxeter plane. 8 rings of 30, 30-fold rotational symmetry, the familiar mandala.
  • Tilt small but not zero: the 8 rings "smear" into ellipses, the 30-fold symmetry breaks but the overall structure is still recognizably derived from the Coxeter plane.
  • Tilt = π/2: the projection plane is (w1, v), which is in a completely different region of Gr(2, 8). The shadow typically has few distinct radii and low symmetry.
  • Integer-axis preset: projection onto (e1, e2) — the first two coordinate axes. The half-integer family clusters at the 4 corners (±½, ±½), and the integer family fills a small cross pattern. This is deliberately boring; it's what a "non-symmetric" projection looks like.
  • Generic random preset: a random orthonormal 2-plane chosen once at page load. Usually produces an unstructured blob with no apparent symmetry. This is what most 2-planes look like — the highly symmetric ones are a measure-zero subset.

Invariants across projections

Some properties of E8 do not depend on which 2-plane you project onto; they are invariants of the root system itself. The stats panel in the rotator tracks a few observable ones: the number of distinct radii the projected points split into, the tightest ring count, the strongest rotational symmetry the computer can find.

What stays invariant across all projections? The underlying root system — the set of inner products between pairs of roots — is unchanged. So is the Weyl group action. What the projection forgets is the geometric embedding: two different projections can produce shadows that look wildly different despite being views of the same 8-dimensional structure. The projection is a lens, not the object itself.

Why the Coxeter plane is the good lens

Now we can say precisely what makes the Coxeter plane special. It's the 2-plane where the Coxeter element acts as a pure rotation, which forces the projected image to inherit the full Coxeter number of rotational symmetry (30-fold for E8). No other 2-plane, no matter how carefully chosen, inherits that much symmetry — and among all "small" symmetric projections, only a handful (H3, F4, and a few related ones) come close.

The Coxeter plane is also where the Petrie polygons — the orbits of the Coxeter element — appear as regular 30-gons rather than skewed polygons. In any other 2-plane the orbits project to ellipses or worse; only in the Coxeter plane do you see them as perfect regular polygons.

What we take with us

  • Gr(2, 8) is 12-dimensional. There are a huge number of ways to project E8 into 2D, and the "nice" ones are a small subset.
  • The projection lens does not change the object. Root inner products, Weyl group action, and the underlying combinatorics are invariant. Only the visual changes.
  • The Coxeter plane is the single most symmetric lens. It inherits h-fold rotational symmetry from the Coxeter element acting as a pure rotation. Every other 2-plane has strictly less visible symmetry.
  • To really see E8, move between projections. A single picture, even the Coxeter-plane mandala, is always incomplete.
