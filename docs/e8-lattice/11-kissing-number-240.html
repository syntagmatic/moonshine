Kissing Number 240 and Why It's Optimal

Part 11 of 15. E8 gives a lower bound of 240 for the 8D kissing number. A 1979 linear-programming argument gives the same upper bound. We walk through the shape of the proof.

The kissing number problem asks: how many unit spheres can simultaneously touch a central unit sphere in ℝn without overlapping? We met the question in explainer 1 for n = 2 (answer: 6, hexagonal arrangement) and n = 3 (answer: 12, face-centered cubic). For n = 8, the answer is 240, achieved by the E8 root system. This explainer treats the claim "the 8D kissing number is exactly 240" as a two-sided theorem — a lower bound and an upper bound that meet — and walks through each side.

The lower bound: E8 gives 240

The 240 roots of E8 all have squared length 2, so they have length √2. Normalize them to unit length by dividing by √2: the resulting 240 vectors all have length 1, and their pairwise inner products are , because dividing vectors of squared length 2 by √2 reduces their squared length by a factor of 2 and their inner products by the same factor.

For 240 unit vectors to be the kissing configuration of a packing, they must be mutually at angle ≥ 60°, which translates to inner product ≤ 1/2. The E8 unit vectors satisfy this: their distinct pairwise inner products are {−1, −½, 0, ½, 1}, all ≤ 1/2 (the maximum ½ is achieved by pairs of roots at 60° to each other).

So placing a unit sphere at each of the 240 normalized E8 roots (plus one at the origin) produces a valid kissing configuration with 240 spheres touching the central sphere. The 8D kissing number is therefore at least 240.

The upper bound: linear programming

The harder direction is proving that no configuration of 241 unit vectors in ℝ8 can all be mutually at angle ≥ 60°. This was proved independently by Odlyzko–Sloane and Levenshtein in 1979, using a technique called the linear programming bound (or Delsarte bound), which turns the geometric kissing problem into a statement about positive-definite functions on the sphere.

The witness function for n = 8

Odlyzko and Sloane constructed a specific f for n = 8 whose bound comes out to exactly 240 — matching the E8 lower bound. Both sides equal 240. The kissing number in dimension 8 is exactly 240, and E8 is the configuration that achieves it.

Why this works for 8 and 24 and no one else. For most dimensions, the Delsarte bound is strictly greater than the best-known lower bound — there is a gap between "what linear programming can prove" and "what we can build." For dimensions 8 and 24, by a mathematical miracle, the LP bound is exactly tight. This is related to the existence of specific self-dual lattices (E8 and Leech) with the right kind of extreme combinatorial structure. The same LP machinery applied to dimensions 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11, …, 23 leaves gaps that are still open today.

Historical arc

The 1979 proof has a dramatic backstory. In the late 1970s, three groups — Odlyzko and Sloane at Bell Labs, Levenshtein in Moscow, and Kabatyanskii–Levenshtein — were working on upper bounds for spherical codes, of which the kissing problem is a special case. Odlyzko and Sloane cranked through the explicit 8D and 24D polynomial computations by hand and with early computer-algebra assistance and obtained the tight bounds. Levenshtein, working independently, used a more refined version of the Delsarte framework and arrived at the same conclusion nearly simultaneously.

The 1979 proof solved two major open problems in a single stroke. But it did not solve the related sphere packing problem, which asks about density rather than local kissing count. That problem stayed open for another 37 years, and its resolution — by Maryna Viazovska in 2016 — is the subject of the next explainer.

What we take with us

  • Kissing number in 8D = 240. Lower bound from E8. Upper bound from the 1979 Delsarte LP argument.
  • LP bound. A function f with specific positivity properties produces an inequality of the form N ≤ (Σ fk) / f0; tight witnesses exist in dimensions 1–4, 8, and 24.
  • 8 and 24 are the exceptional tight cases. In dimensions 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, …, 23 there is a gap between the best known lower bound and the LP upper bound, and closing it is an open problem.
  • The 1979 proof is 37 years younger than the packing problem. Kissing got proved in 1979; packing stayed open until Viazovska's 2016 proof.
