E8's Bigger Sibling: the Leech Lattice

Part 13 of 15. In 24 dimensions there's a lattice that stands to its dimension the way E8 stands to 8. It has 196,560 minimum vectors, no roots at all, and its symmetry group gave three of the 26 sporadic simple groups.

We finished Act II with E8 in a distinguished position: the root system of an exceptional Lie algebra, the vertex set of a uniform 8-polytope, the densest sphere packing in 8 dimensions, and the kissing champion. All of that is true, and it earns E8 its fame in dimension 8. But if you ask "is there anything like E8 in higher dimensions?" the answer is yes, and what you find in dimension 24 is even stranger: a lattice with no minimum-length shell analog to the "roots," a kissing number nearly a thousand times larger than E8's, and a symmetry group that turned out to be a vein running into the 26 sporadic finite simple groups — one of the weirdest classification results in 20th-century mathematics.

This is the Leech lattice, discovered by John Leech in 1967. We are not going to construct it here — the honest construction needs the binary Golay code and a careful Construction B or a hexacode-based gluing, which is outside this series' scope. What we are going to do is explain what makes Leech "E8's bigger sibling," walk through the parallels between their stories, and point at where the construction lives if you want to chase it further.

The striking differences: Leech's minimum squared length is 4 rather than 2 (there are no vectors of squared length 2 at all — no "roots" in the classical sense) and its kissing number is 819 times larger.

No roots, and why that matters

The single most unusual property of the Leech lattice is that it has no vectors of squared length 2. This is different from E8, whose 240 minimum vectors all have squared length exactly 2 and whose whole structure is built around the root system. In 24 dimensions there are exactly 24 even unimodular lattices (the Niemeier lattices), classified by Niemeier in 1973. Twenty-three of them have a non-trivial root sub-lattice — some vectors of squared length 2. The twenty-fourth does not: the Leech lattice is the unique even unimodular 24-dimensional lattice with no roots.

"No roots" is a surprising property because it forces the minimum squared length to jump from 2 (the normal minimum for even lattices) to 4. The 196,560 minimum vectors of Leech all have squared length 4, which is why the packing density looks small in absolute terms (small ball radius relative to unit cell volume) but is still optimal in its dimension.

Why rootless matters. In the presence of roots, a lattice's automorphism group always contains the Weyl group of the root sub-system, which is a lot of structure that is essentially "coming from the roots." A rootless lattice has to get its symmetry from somewhere else entirely, and Leech's symmetry comes from an astonishing coincidence involving the binary Golay code's automorphism group. The resulting group — called Co₀ or the "Conway group" — is large enough to contain three of the sporadic simple groups as quotients.

Of the twenty-three "rooted" Niemeier lattices, E8³ is one — it is the direct sum of three orthogonal copies of E8, sitting in ℝ²⁴ = ℝ⁸ ⊕ ℝ⁸ ⊕ ℝ⁸. This is not the same as Leech, even though both involve E8.

Leech and three copies of E8

There is a folk-description of the Leech lattice as "three copies of E8 glued together by the binary Golay code." This is a rough but roughly-correct summary of a real construction due to Turyn (1967), later refined by Conway and others. The technical picture: take the direct sum E8 ⊕ E8 ⊕ E8 inside ℝ²⁴ and enlarge it by adding specific "glue vectors" whose positions are controlled by the hexacode — a [6, 3, 4] linear code over the field 𝔽₄. The resulting lattice is Λ₂₄, and the gluing produces exactly the conditions that kill all squared-length-2 vectors and promote the minimum to squared length 4.

We're not going to reproduce the construction. The hexacode needs its own setup, the gluing rules are intricate, and doing it honestly requires pages of careful bookkeeping that would distract from the broader story. If you want the full construction, Conway & Sloane's Sphere Packings, Lattices and Groups, chapter 24, walks through it step by step. What we are going to take away: Leech is genuinely built from E8 components, the "three copies" framing is not wrong, but the framing hides the fact that the gluing is the hard part and the E8 pieces are the easy part.

The symmetry cascade: Conway and the sporadic groups

The automorphism group of the Leech lattice is a finite group of order ≈ 8.3 × 1018, called Co0 (sometimes written Co₀). In 1968, John Conway computed this group and discovered that it has a remarkable quotient structure: its center has order 2, and the quotient Co0/Z(Co0) is a simple group called Co1. Moreover, Co1 contains two additional sporadic simple groups: Co2 (the stabilizer of a certain vector) and Co3 (the stabilizer of a different vector). These three groups — Co1, Co2, Co3 — are three of the 26 sporadic finite simple groups that, together with the infinite families, make up the complete classification.

From there, Leech and its automorphism group lead deeper into the sporadic landscape. The Leech lattice modulo a specific sub-lattice produces the Mathieu group M24, another sporadic. And the vertex operator algebra constructed from the Leech lattice turns out to be related to the monster group — the largest sporadic simple group, order ≈ 8 × 1053 — through the unexpected phenomenon of "monstrous moonshine," which Richard Borcherds proved in 1992 using vertex algebras. A full discussion belongs elsewhere, but the chain is clear: Leech lattice → Conway group → Mathieu group → moonshine → monster.

1979
Odlyzko–Sloane and Levenshtein independently prove the kissing numbers 240 and 196,560 are optimal (for E8 and Leech respectively).
1992
Borcherds proves the "monstrous moonshine" conjectures, connecting the Leech-lattice vertex algebra to the monster group; awarded the Fields Medal 1998.
2016 (Mar 14)
Viazovska proves the E8 lattice achieves the densest sphere packing in 8D.
2016 (Mar 21)
Seven days later, Cohn–Kumar–Miller–Radchenko–Viazovska prove the Leech lattice achieves the densest sphere packing in 24D.

The 8D and 24D packing proofs were seven days apart, and they use the same infrastructure (modular forms, Cohn–Elkies bound, Viazovska's magic function technique). The two-week window in March 2016 cleared two of the longest-standing open problems in discrete geometry. In no other dimensions has the analogous technique been made to work — whether it can be made to work in any other dimension remains an open problem.

Why not build the Leech lattice ourselves?

A fair question, given that this series built E8 from scratch in explainer 3. The honest answer is scope. To build Leech we would need: (a) the binary Golay code or equivalently the hexacode, (b) Construction B (or a direct gluing), (c) a way to display 24-dimensional vectors, (d) enough new interactive figures to support all of the above. Each of (a)–(d) is its own climb, and doing them inside one explainer would sacrifice the computational fluency we've been building for E8. A Leech-first series is a worthwhile project, but it's a different project.

What we can do in this explainer is be honest about what Leech is and what makes it special, use E8 as the "frame of reference" the reader already understands, and point at where to go for the technical details.

What we take with us

  • Leech lattice. The unique even unimodular 24-dimensional lattice with no roots. Minimum squared length 4, kissing number 196,560, packing density π¹²/12!.
  • Niemeier family. There are exactly 24 even unimodular 24-dimensional lattices; Leech is the one without a root sub-system.
  • E8's bigger sibling. Both are exceptional, both were proven packing-optimal in March 2016, both have enormous automorphism groups, and both are connected to the sporadic simple group landscape (Leech directly, E8 through its Monstrous-moonshine-adjacent vertex algebras).
  • Conway groups. Co0, Co1, Co2, Co3 — three sporadic simple groups that come out of Leech's automorphism group.
  • Reading further. Conway & Sloane, Sphere Packings, Lattices and Groups, chapters 4 and 24, for the construction and the group theory. Ronan's Symmetry and the Monster for the narrative arc into the sporadic groups.
