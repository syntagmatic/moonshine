From Root System to Lattice

Part 10 of 15. The 240 roots are the shortest nonzero vectors of an infinite object — the E8 lattice. We build the lattice, write it in two different forms, and count the vectors at each shell distance.

Up to now the series has treated E8 as a set of 240 vectors. That treatment is complete at the level of the root system, but it is also misleading: the root system is a small, finite visible layer on top of a much larger object — an infinite, periodic, 8-dimensional discrete subgroup of ℝ8 called the E8 lattice. The root system is the minimum shell of that lattice, meaning the set of its shortest nonzero vectors. The rest of the lattice extends outward in shells of increasing squared length, and the structure of those shells is one of the most beautiful aspects of E8.

A lattice is an infinite set

The E8 lattice is the integer span of the 240 roots — the set of all sums where each ci is any integer and αi is a choice of 8 simple roots. This is the definition we came closest to in explainer 4 when we wrote every root as a non-negative integer combination of simple roots. For the lattice, we drop the non-negativity requirement: every integer combination counts, whether or not the result is itself a minimum-length vector.

Equivalently, the lattice is closed under addition: if x and y are lattice vectors, so is x + y, and so is any integer multiple. That is the definition a number theorist would give. Both definitions produce the same set, and that set is infinite.

A cleaner description: D₈⁺

While "integer combinations of the simple roots" is a correct description, it isn't the nicest. There is a cleaner way to write the E8 lattice that makes computation much easier: the D₈⁺ construction.

In words: a vector is in the E8 lattice iff either all 8 of its coordinates are integers or all 8 are half-integers (of the form n + ½ with n ∈ ℤ), and in either case the coordinate sum is an even integer.

Let's verify this recovers the 240 roots. Integer part: all integer vectors of squared length 2 with even coordinate sum are exactly the 112 minimum vectors of D8 — the (±1, ±1, 0, …, 0) vectors we've been using since explainer 2. Each such vector has squared length 2 and even sum (since the sum is 0 or ±2). That's 112 of the 240. ✓

Half-integer part: all half-integer vectors of squared length 2 with even coordinate sum are the 128 vectors (±½, …, ±½) with an even number of minuses. (Each coordinate contributes 1/4 to squared length, so squared length 2 forces all 8 to be ±½; "even number of minuses" is a constraint equivalent to "coordinate sum is an even integer.") That's the other 128. ✓

Why D₈⁺ is nicer. In the D₈⁺ form, checking if a vector is in the E8 lattice reduces to three clauses: is it in ℤ8 or (ℤ + ½)8? Does its coordinate sum fall in 2ℤ? That's all. You can write the check as 10 lines of code and test any vector in ℝ8 in O(n) time. The "integer span of the simple roots" formulation is harder to use in practice because you'd have to solve a linear system to figure out if a given vector is in the span.

Shell structure: counting by squared length

Pick a squared length k ∈ {0, 2, 4, 6, 8, …}. How many lattice vectors have exactly that squared length? For k = 0 there is one (the zero vector). For k = 2 there are 240 (the roots). For k = 4 it turns out to be 2,160. These counts form the shell structure of E8:

Dots, not more terms — the series has infinitely many. The theta series of E8 is a modular form of weight 4, which is a very specific technical claim with very specific consequences. Among them: ΘE₈(q) equals the weight-4 Eisenstein series E4(τ) (with q = e2πiτ), which is one of the fundamental building blocks of modular form theory.

We will not prove that E8's theta series is modular — the proof requires the Poisson summation formula, Fourier analysis on lattices, and a careful argument about unimodularity. What matters for this series is that the theta series records the shell structure, and that record happens to have extraordinary transformation properties under the modular group. Those properties are precisely what Viazovska's 2016 proof of sphere packing optimality leverages, so this is the last stop before we meet the packing theorem in explainer 12.

This is unusual: there are only 2 even unimodular lattices in 8 dimensions (E8 and its rotations), only 24 in 24 dimensions (the Niemeier lattices, which include the Leech lattice we'll meet in explainer 13), and none at all in dimensions 1–7. Unimodularity is what makes the theta series a modular form rather than "almost a modular form," and it's what forces E8's fundamental domain to have volume exactly 1.

What we take with us

  • E8 lattice. An infinite discrete subgroup of ℝ8, closed under addition and integer multiplication.
  • D₈⁺ form. All integer vectors with even coordinate sum, plus all half-integer vectors with even coordinate sum.
  • Shells. Lattice vectors grouped by squared length. Shell counts: 1, 240, 2160, 6720, 17520, 30240, 60480, 82560, 140400, …
  • Theta series. The generating function ΘE₈(q) = Σ q⟨v,v⟩, which is a weight-4 modular form (E4).
  • Unimodularity. E8 is self-dual, with fundamental-domain volume 1. This is what makes the theta series modular.
