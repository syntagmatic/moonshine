Viazovska and Optimal Sphere Packing in 8D

Part 12 of 15. The E8 lattice has packing density π⁴/384. In 2016, Maryna Viazovska proved this is the maximum possible density in 8 dimensions. We compute the density from scratch and sketch the structure of her proof.

Explainer 11 settled the kissing number in 8D — the local question of how many spheres can touch a central one. This explainer settles the packing density — the global question of how tightly equal spheres can fill all of 8D space. The answer, for E8, is π⁴/384 ≈ 0.2537, and for 37 years after the 1979 kissing proof, it was an open question whether any 8D packing could beat that number. In 2016, Maryna Viazovska proved the answer is no. Two weeks later, she and four collaborators proved the analogous result for the Leech lattice in 24 dimensions.

The 2016 proof is one of the most celebrated pieces of 21st-century mathematics. We cannot derive the proof here — it rests on deep modular-form machinery that would take a semester to teach — but we can compute the lower bound (the density of E8) from elementary arithmetic, state the upper bound theorem (Cohn–Elkies 2003), and describe the structure of Viazovska's "magic function" that pinches the two bounds together. Honest scope: we do the lower bound in full, and we narrate the upper bound.

Packing density is not kissing number

First, let's be precise about what "packing density" means. Consider an infinite configuration of equal non-overlapping balls in ℝn — a packing. The packing's density is the fraction of ℝn's volume occupied by the balls, in the limit as you integrate over a large region. For a lattice packing (balls centered at lattice points), density is easy to compute: it's the volume of one ball divided by the volume of the lattice's fundamental domain.

The kissing question was "how many neighbors can one sphere have?" — a local question about a single sphere's immediate neighborhood. The packing question is "how densely can the whole space be filled?" — a global question about the entire infinite arrangement. For lattice packings, the local kissing number and the global density are both determined by the lattice's minimum vector length and its fundamental domain volume, but they answer different questions and their optimal values can in principle be achieved by different lattices.

We just computed a lower bound. Placing spheres at every E8 lattice point fills π⁴/384 ≈ 25.37% of 8D space. Any better packing would have to beat that density. The 37-year open question was: can you? Before 2016, the best-known upper bound (from Cohn–Elkies 2003) was close to π⁴/384 but not exactly equal, so there was room for something denser — even something non-lattice. Viazovska's theorem closed the gap.

For most dimensions this optimization produces a bound that is close to but not equal to the lattice lower bound; the gap remained for decades.

Once she had the function, the rest of the argument followed within a few months.

Historical timeline

1611
Kepler conjectures the densest 3D packing is the face-centered cubic (24.05% × √2 · π ≈ 74%).
1905
Minkowski initiates the modern study of lattice packing; the packing problem in general dimensions is posed explicitly.
1929
Blichfeldt proves the first non-trivial upper bounds for high-dimensional packings.
1967
John Leech constructs the Leech lattice in 24 dimensions; its kissing and packing properties immediately attract attention.
1979
Odlyzko–Sloane and Levenshtein independently prove the 8D and 24D kissing numbers (240 and 196,560) are optimal.
1998
Thomas Hales proves the 3D sphere packing density (FCC / 74%) is optimal — a massive computer-assisted case analysis, confirming Kepler's 1611 conjecture.
2003
Cohn and Elkies introduce the linear-programming bound for sphere packing density in arbitrary dimension. For n = 8 and 24, the bound gets tantalizingly close to the known lattice lower bound, but not exactly equal.
2016 (Mar 14)
Maryna Viazovska posts The sphere packing problem in dimension 8 to arXiv. She constructs an explicit "magic function" using modular forms and proves that E8 is optimal. Her paper is 23 pages.
2016 (Mar 21)
Seven days later, Cohn, Kumar, Miller, Radchenko, and Viazovska post The sphere packing problem in dimension 24, extending the argument to prove the Leech lattice is optimal. The 24D proof is similar in spirit but more technically involved.
2022
Maryna Viazovska receives the Fields Medal at the International Congress of Mathematicians, becoming the second woman to do so.

Two weeks. The 8D and 24D proofs arrived within seven days of each other, 37 years after the kissing problem in those dimensions had been settled. Both used the same modular-form infrastructure. Both closed the Cohn–Elkies gap exactly. For dimensions 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11, …, 23, the gap is still open and no analogous magic function is known. It remains an active open problem whether Viazovska's technique extends to any dimension other than 8 and 24, or whether those two dimensions are uniquely special in some deep way.

What we take with us

  • Packing density of E8 = π⁴/384 ≈ 0.2537. Computable in five elementary steps from the 8-ball volume formula and the unimodularity of E8.
  • Upper bound from Cohn–Elkies (2003). A linear-programming framework producing an upper bound from any admissible function f. The tightness depends on how cleverly f is chosen.
  • Viazovska's theorem (2016). There exists an explicit f, built from modular forms of weight 8 and 12, whose Cohn–Elkies bound equals π⁴/384 exactly. Therefore E8 is the densest 8D packing.
  • Only 8 and 24. The magic-function technique works exactly in dimensions 8 and 24 (where E8 and Leech live). In all other dimensions the sphere packing problem remains open.
