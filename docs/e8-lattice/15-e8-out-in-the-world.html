E8 Out in the World

Part 15 of 15. The capstone. Where E8 shows up outside lattice theory, what we deliberately skipped, and where to go next if you want to keep climbing.

We started this series with a promise that E8 is a concrete object you can compute with. Fourteen explainers later, you have. You can generate the 240 roots, apply a Weyl reflection, compute an inner product, project onto the Coxeter plane, peel off a sub-root-system, and check the 5-step derivation of the packing density π⁴/384. The computational goal is met, and so is the geometric one: you have rotated a 3D shadow of the Gosset polytope, seen 8 rings of 30 materialize in the Coxeter projection, and watched a projection-plane rotator distort E8 in real time.

This final explainer is different in register. It is the narrative capstone: the place where E8 connects outward to the rest of mathematics and physics, and where we are honest about the parts of the story we did not cover. You will not need any new computation. Instead you get five vignettes on places E8 shows up in the wider world, one honest accounting of the gaps in our treatment, and a reading list for what to do next.

Vignette 1: heterotic string theory

In 1984 and 1985, the "heterotic string" model of David Gross, Jeffrey Harvey, Emil Martinec, and Ryan Rohm appeared as part of the first superstring revolution. A heterotic string is a 10-dimensional theory in which the left-moving and right-moving modes live in different-dimensional spaces: 10 dimensions for the right-movers, 26 for the left-movers, with the 16-dimensional difference compactified onto a 16-torus. Anomaly cancellation — a consistency condition for the quantum theory — requires the gauge group of the theory to have dimension exactly 496, and the two options that satisfy the condition are E8 × E8 and SO(32). Both theories are internally consistent; the E8 × E8 version is more popular for phenomenological reasons related to how it breaks down to Standard Model physics.

The 16-dimensional torus that the left-movers live on is literally the E8 × E8 lattice, as a 16-dimensional even unimodular lattice. Compactifying onto this lattice is how the E8 × E8 gauge symmetry becomes the gauge group of the heterotic theory. This is a direct application of the lattice structure we've built: the lattice is the mathematical object that makes the string theory work.

On "applications" in physics. The heterotic string is a mathematically elegant model of quantum gravity. It is not a verified description of reality. No experiment has confirmed string theory, and whether it will ever make a testable prediction remains an open question. When we say "E8 appears in physics," we mean it appears in physicists' models, not that those models are known to describe the universe. The same caveat applies to every physics application in this explainer.

Vignette 2: the 2007 AIM computation

In January 2007, the American Institute of Mathematics announced that a team of 18 mathematicians led by Jeffrey Adams had completed a four-year computation of the Kazhdan–Lusztig–Vogan polynomials for the split real form of E8. These are specific polynomials whose coefficients encode the structure of the unitary representations of the associated real Lie group — an enormous classification project that had been pending for decades. The computation produced 60 gigabytes of output, with individual polynomials occasionally having coefficients into the tens of millions (the largest was 11,808,808).

The project made headlines because the output file was briefly the largest single piece of mathematics ever computed at the time, and because the announcement included the sentence "the calculation took about 77 hours on a supercomputer, producing 60 gigabytes of output; if written out by hand, it would cover an area the size of Manhattan." The press latched onto the size. What actually matters is what the computation enables: it fills in a detailed picture of E8's representation theory that was previously beyond reach, and makes possible detailed study of the unitary representations of the real Lie group of E8.

The computation is a direct descendant of Kazhdan–Lusztig's 1979 conjectures, which proposed a combinatorial formula for certain representation-theoretic quantities. Proving and computing Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials for large root systems is mechanically demanding but conceptually systematic. E8 was the hardest case because its Weyl group has 696,729,600 elements and the polynomial computation scales badly.

Vignette 3: Viazovska's Fields Medal

We met Maryna Viazovska in explainer 12 as the author of the 2016 sphere packing proof. In 2022, she received the Fields Medal at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Helsinki (the ICM was originally scheduled for St Petersburg and moved in the wake of Russia's invasion of Ukraine). She became the second woman to receive the Fields Medal, after Maryam Mirzakhani in 2014, and the citation specifically recognized the 8D and 24D packing theorems.

Viazovska was born in Kharkiv, Ukraine, in 1984, trained in Kyiv and Bonn, and is now at the École Polytechnique Fédérale de Lausanne. Her work is notable not just for the specific theorem but for its technique: the "magic function" approach using modular forms has since been extended to related problems in discrete geometry (spherical designs, sphere packings in other geometries) and has opened new directions in the interplay between modular forms, Fourier analysis, and lattice theory. The 2016 paper is 23 pages long and famously readable — an unusual virtue for a paper that settles a 37-year-old open problem.

Vignette 4: Garrett Lisi and "an exceptionally simple theory of everything"

In November 2007, physicist Garrett Lisi posted a paper to the arXiv titled "An Exceptionally Simple Theory of Everything," proposing that E8 could serve as the unified gauge group for a theory combining the Standard Model of particle physics with general relativity. The idea was that elements of the E8 Lie algebra could be identified with known fundamental particles and forces, with the exceptional structure of E8 somehow constraining the physics in ways that matched observation.

The paper attracted unusual attention because Lisi was an unaffiliated surfer-physicist living in Hawaii at the time, and because the claimed unification was dramatic. It also attracted serious critical attention. In 2009, Jacques Distler and Skip Garibaldi published a technical paper titled "There is no 'Theory of Everything' inside E8," which showed that no embedding of the Standard Model's fermion content into the E8 Lie algebra can produce the chiral spectrum actually observed. The argument uses representation theory: E8's fundamental representations simply do not contain the right structure to accommodate three generations of chiral fermions with the correct charges.

The Distler–Garibaldi result is a genuine obstruction. It does not mean E8 is "wrong" — E8 is a beautiful mathematical object and is not obligated to describe the Standard Model — but it does mean that the specific unification Lisi proposed cannot work in the form he wrote down. Variant proposals have appeared since, using other exceptional groups or modified embeddings, but none have produced a theory that simultaneously accommodates Standard Model phenomenology and passes all the consistency checks. The Lisi episode is a reminder that beautiful mathematics does not automatically become correct physics.

Scope of the disagreement. Distler and Garibaldi's critique is specifically about the embedding Lisi proposed, not about the idea of using E8 in some future theory. There is no consensus that E8 has no role in unified physics — the heterotic string already uses it — only that the particular 2007 proposal does not work. Whether some future refinement can rescue the idea is an open question.

Vignette 5: quasicrystals and E8

A quasicrystal is a solid whose atomic arrangement is highly ordered but not periodic — the atoms sit at specific positions that come from a long-range ordering rule that does not repeat. Shechtman and collaborators discovered physical quasicrystals in 1982 (confirmed 1984), eventually earning the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. One way to construct quasicrystal patterns mathematically is to project a higher-dimensional periodic lattice onto a lower-dimensional irrational subspace — so-called cut-and-project constructions.

Some quasicrystal models, especially those with icosahedral symmetry (5-fold rotational, which is forbidden in crystalline solids), are obtained by projecting the E8 lattice onto a 3- or 4-dimensional subspace chosen to capture the desired symmetry. The E8 lattice's 120 "icosahedral" symmetries become visible as the rotational symmetries of the quasicrystal pattern. This is genuinely a role for E8 in materials science — the lattice provides the "mother lattice" from which quasicrystalline patterns are cut.

The quasicrystal application is smaller than the string theory one but more empirically grounded. If you want a specific place where E8 touches the physical world in a verified way, quasicrystals are it.

Cut-and-project pattern from ~26,000 E8 lattice points through a golden-ratio 2-plane with a bounded 6D window.

Figure 1. A cut-and-project quasicrystal sample derived from the E8 lattice. We generate all E8 lattice points up to squared length 8 (~26,000 vectors), split each one into a "visible" 2D part (projection onto a 2-plane containing golden-ratio entries) and a "hidden" 6D part (projection onto the orthogonal complement), and keep only the points whose hidden part has norm ≤ 1.5. The surviving points form an aperiodic pattern — not a perfectly symmetric quasicrystal (for that you'd need the specific Elser–Sloane construction), but a genuine example of what cut-and-project produces when you project E8 through a golden-ratio window.

What we did not cover

This series is about E8 as a concrete geometric and combinatorial object. We deliberately left out several directions that would have doubled its length. In the interests of being explicit about scope, here they are — with pointers to where you can learn them.

Lie algebra representation theory

E8 has an associated Lie algebra 𝔢8, a 248-dimensional real vector space with a bracket operation. The identity 248 = 240 + 8 reflects the decomposition of 𝔢8 into its 240 "root spaces" (one per root) and its 8-dimensional "Cartan subalgebra." The Lie algebra's representations — spaces on which 𝔢8 acts linearly — are a vast classical subject that we did not touch. In particular, we did not discuss weights, characters, highest weight representations, or the Weyl character formula. The adjoint representation (248-dimensional) is E8's smallest nontrivial irreducible representation, which is unusual among Lie algebras.

Start with: Fulton & Harris, Representation Theory: A First Course, part III. For E8 specifically: Humphreys, Introduction to Lie Algebras and Representation Theory, chapters 9–12.

Vertex operator algebras and monstrous moonshine

Attached to E8 (and the Leech lattice) is a vertex operator algebra (VOA), a sophisticated algebraic structure introduced by Frenkel, Lepowsky, and Meurman. The E8 VOA is related to the moonshine module and ultimately to the monster group — the largest sporadic simple group, of order roughly 8 × 1053. Richard Borcherds proved the "monstrous moonshine" conjectures in 1992, establishing a deep and originally mysterious connection between the monster group, modular forms, and these lattice-based VOAs. A series about E8 could have spent ten explainers on moonshine alone; we chose not to.

Start with: Ronan, Symmetry and the Monster (narrative, accessible). Then: Frenkel, Lepowsky, & Meurman, Vertex Operator Algebras and the Monster.

Octonions

E8 has an alternate realization using the octonions, the largest of the four normed division algebras (after ℝ, ℂ, and ℍ). Specifically, the 240 roots of E8 can be identified with 240 unit octonions forming a specific finite subset closed under multiplication up to sign. This construction is beautiful but requires teaching octonion arithmetic first, which is its own climb of about three explainers — not worth the distraction given our spine.

Start with: John Baez, The Octonions (Bull. AMS 2002), freely available online. The final section discusses E8 and the exceptional Lie algebras from the octonionic perspective.

The real forms and their classification

Every complex Lie algebra has several "real forms" — real Lie algebras whose complexification is the given complex one. E8 has three real forms: the compact form (realizing all of E8's symmetries in a compact space), the split form (all symmetries realized as unbounded linear operators), and E8(−24) (an intermediate form). The 2007 AIM computation was specifically about the split form E8(8).

Start with: Helgason, Differential Geometry, Lie Groups, and Symmetric Spaces, chapter X. For the AIM story specifically: the AIM E8 project page at aimath.org/E8/.

Donaldson theory and the E8 manifold

In 4-manifold topology, a specific smooth 4-manifold called the E8 manifold has intersection form given by the Cartan matrix of E8. Simon Donaldson used gauge-theoretic techniques in the early 1980s to show that this manifold has exotic smooth structure that cannot be detected by classical topology. This was a landmark result in low-dimensional topology and earned Donaldson the Fields Medal in 1986.

Start with: Donaldson & Kronheimer, The Geometry of Four-Manifolds. For a narrative: Freedman & Quinn, Topology of 4-Manifolds.

Further reading

For sphere packing, lattices, and codes

  • Conway & Sloane, Sphere Packings, Lattices and Groups (3rd ed., Springer 1999). The standard reference. Chapter 8 is E8, chapter 24 is Leech.
  • Cohn, "Sphere packing," Notices AMS 2023. A survey of the sphere packing problem including Viazovska's proof.
  • Viazovska, "The sphere packing problem in dimension 8," Annals of Mathematics 185 (2017). The original proof, 23 pages, unusually readable.

For Lie theory and root systems

  • Humphreys, Reflection Groups and Coxeter Groups (Cambridge 1990). The cleanest introduction to Weyl groups, Coxeter elements, root systems.
  • Bourbaki, Groupes et algèbres de Lie, chapters 4–6 (Planches). The source of the Bourbaki simple root choices we used throughout this series.
  • Fulton & Harris, Representation Theory. Classical reference for Lie algebra representations.

For the wider E8 story

  • Baez, The Octonions (Bull. AMS 2002). Contains a lovely discussion of E8 via octonions.
  • Ronan, Symmetry and the Monster. Narrative account of the monster group and moonshine; E8 and Leech are along the way.
  • Adams et al., the AIM E8 project page at aimath.org/E8/. The 2007 Kazhdan–Lusztig–Vogan computation, documented.

Where you stand at the end

Fourteen explainers ago, E8 was probably a name you had seen attached to T-shirts, arxiv papers, or physics blog posts. You now have the following:

  • A specific coordinate-level construction of the 240 roots you could rebuild from memory: 112 integer ±ei ± ej vectors plus 128 half-integer (±½, …, ±½) vectors with even sign count.
  • A second description as the D8+ lattice and a third as the extended Hamming code under Construction A.
  • An operation (the Weyl reflection sα(x) = x − ⟨x, α⟩α) that takes any root to any other, and a BFS intuition for how the whole Weyl group of order 696,729,600 is generated by just 8 of these.
  • A geometric intuition for the Coxeter-plane projection as 8 rings of 30, and an understanding of why 30 specifically (the Coxeter number h = 30).
  • A proof in your head of E8's packing density π4/384, via the five-step elementary derivation.
  • A narrative understanding of why 2016 was the year the kissing problem's 37-year-older sibling (the packing problem) was finally settled, and of Viazovska's modular-form-based "magic function" technique.
  • Pointers to the many directions you could go next: Lie algebra rep theory, VOAs and moonshine, the octonionic picture, real forms, Donaldson theory.

What you have is not a complete picture of E8 — there isn't one, E8 is too rich — but a coherent, computationally grounded picture you can keep building on. That was the plan. Thanks for climbing it with us.
