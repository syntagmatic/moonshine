Codes and the Leech Lattice

Part 9 of 10. Sphere packing and error correction are the same problem. E8 is what you get from the extended Hamming code; the Leech lattice is what you get from the binary Golay code. Both were proved packing-optimal in March 2016, seven days apart.

Packing and error correction as the same question

A binary block code of length n is a subset C ⊆ {0, 1}n — a set of valid "codewords" out of all 2n possible binary strings. When you transmit a codeword over a noisy channel, some bits get flipped; the receiver's job is to figure out which codeword was originally sent. Good codes have the property that codewords are "spread out" in the Hamming metric: no two codewords share too many bits, so a small number of bit-flip errors can always be corrected by finding the nearest codeword to what the receiver saw.

Sphere packing asks a geometric version of the same question. A sphere packing is a set of points in ℝn whose pairwise distances are all at least some minimum value (so that unit spheres placed at each point don't overlap). When noise perturbs a packing point into an arbitrary vector in ℝn, the receiver decodes by finding the nearest packing point. Good packings have the property that packing points are spread out in the Euclidean metric.

The two problems become identical when you connect them through Construction A.

The extended Hamming [8, 4, 4] code

Our worked example is the extended Hamming code, sometimes written [8, 4, 4] or H8. The name encodes three numbers: length n = 8, dimension k = 4 (so there are 24 = 16 codewords), and minimum Hamming distance d = 4 (so any two distinct codewords differ in at least 4 positions). It is self-dual: the code equals its orthogonal complement under the standard bilinear form on 𝔽28.

Why the parameters line up

The extended Hamming code's three parameters — length 8, dimension 4, minimum distance 4 — combine to produce the E8 lattice under Construction A in a way that is not coincidence. The argument, step by step:

  1. Construction A gives a lattice in ℤ8.
  2. The lattice's minimum squared length is min(4, d) where d is the code's minimum Hamming distance. For extended Hamming, d = 4, so the minimum squared length is 4.
  3. The number of minimum-length lattice vectors is the sum of (number of integer vectors with ±1 in the "one" positions of each codeword of weight exactly 4) plus (number of integer vectors of squared length 4 whose parity is the zero codeword — these are all-even vectors of squared length 4, e.g. (±2, 0, …, 0)).
  4. Count: 14 codewords of weight 4, each giving 24 = 16 sign choices = 224. Plus 8 positions × 2 signs = 16 from the zero codeword's ±2 vectors. Total: 224 + 16 = 240.
  5. Scale by 1/√2: minimum squared length drops from 4 to 2, matching our earlier description of E8's minimum shell.

Three descriptions of E8. Roots (112 integer + 128 half-integer vectors), D8+ cosets, and Construction A on extended Hamming. All three are exactly the same lattice, arrived at from very different vocabularies: geometric, algebraic, combinatorial.

From 8 dimensions to 24

Construction A maps any binary code to a lattice. The extended Hamming code gives E8 in 8 dimensions. What happens in 24 dimensions? The answer is the binary Golay code — a [24, 12, 8] self-dual code with 4,096 codewords — and its lattice is the Leech lattice.

The Golay code is to 24 dimensions what extended Hamming is to 8. Its minimum distance is 8, which under Construction A yields a lattice whose minimum squared length is 4 rather than 2 — meaning no vectors of squared length 2 at all. No roots, and a kissing number of 196,560. The Leech lattice was discovered by John Leech in 1967, and its symmetry group turned out to contain three of the 26 sporadic simple groups.

No roots, and why that matters

The single most unusual property of the Leech lattice is that it has no vectors of squared length 2. In 24 dimensions there are exactly 24 even unimodular lattices (the Niemeier lattices). Twenty-three of them have a non-trivial root sub-lattice — some vectors of squared length 2. The twenty-fourth does not: the Leech lattice is the unique even unimodular 24-dimensional lattice with no roots. "No roots" forces the minimum squared length to jump from 2 to 4. The 196,560 minimum vectors all have squared length 4, which is why the packing density looks small in absolute terms but is still optimal in its dimension.

Why rootless matters. In the presence of roots, a lattice's automorphism group always contains the Weyl group of the root sub-system. A rootless lattice has to get its symmetry from somewhere else entirely, and Leech's symmetry comes from the binary Golay code's automorphism group. The resulting group — Co0, the "Conway group" — is large enough to contain three of the sporadic simple groups as quotients.

Leech and three copies of E8

There is a real construction (due to Turyn, 1967) that builds the Leech lattice from three copies of E8. You take E8 ⊕ E8 ⊕ E8 inside ℝ24 and enlarge it by adding "glue vectors" controlled by the hexacode — a [6, 3, 4] linear code over 𝔽4. The gluing produces exactly the conditions that kill all squared-length-2 vectors and promote the minimum to squared length 4. The E8 pieces are the easy part; the gluing is the hard part.

The symmetry cascade

The automorphism group of the Leech lattice, Co0, has order ≈ 8.3 × 1018. In 1968 John Conway computed this group and discovered that its quotient by a center of order 2 is a simple group, Co1, and that Co1 contains two further sporadic simple groups: Co2 (stabilizer of a type-4 vector) and Co3 (stabilizer of a type-6 vector). These three — Co1, Co2, Co3 — are three of the 26 sporadic finite simple groups. The chain continues: Leech lattice → Conway group → Mathieu group M24 → monstrous moonshine → the monster.

What we take with us

  • Sphere packing ⇔ error correction via Construction A. Code → lattice, Hamming distance → packing radius. The two optimization problems are the same problem in different vocabularies.
  • E8 ≅ (1/√2) · Λ(extended Hamming [8, 4, 4]). Three descriptions of E8: roots, D8+, and now Construction A. All the same object.
  • The Leech lattice is the unique rootless even unimodular 24D lattice. Kissing number 196,560, packing density π12/12!.
  • Leech from the Golay code. The 24-dimensional analog of E8 from Hamming: apply Construction A to the binary Golay [24, 12, 8] code and scale.
  • Conway groups. Co1, Co2, Co3 are sporadic simple groups from Aut(Λ24).
  • Both proved optimal in March 2016, seven days apart. Viazovska proved E8 optimal on March 14; Cohn–Kumar–Miller–Radchenko–Viazovska proved Leech optimal on March 21. No other dimensions are known to be solved.
