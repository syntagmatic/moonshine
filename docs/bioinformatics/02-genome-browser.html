Genome Browser

A genome browser lines up genes, binding sites, conservation and reads on one coordinate axis, so that evidence at the same stretch of DNA can be read together.

Part 1 measured methylation at CpG sites in the CDKN1A promoter. Where those sites sit relative to the gene, to p53's binding sites and to neighbouring genes is a question of position, which is what a genome browser shows: a stretch of the reference genome drawn as a ruler, with tracks of intervals and values stacked under it. This essay stays at CDKN1A, the series' p21 gene, because Parts 1 and 4 study the same promoter, one its methylation and the other p53 binding to it.

Two ways to count

Positions count bases from the left end of a chromosome, in two conventions. The browser's location box, VCF and Ensembl count from 1 and include both ends: CDKN1A's first exon is . Nothing in a bare number says which convention it uses, and Figure 3 shows what a wrong guess does.

An exon is not the same as coding sequence. CDKN1A's canonical transcript has three exons, and exon 1 is all 5′ untranslated region: the start codon lies bases into exon 2, and the stop codon ends bases into exon 3, ahead of bases of 3′ UTR. Splicing out introns of and bases leaves a -base mRNA, bases of which code for p21's amino acids.

Of Ensembl's two other transcripts, one adds a non-coding exon and the other starts bases upstream, with the 5′ p53 site inside its first exon: the same 20 bases are promoter in one transcript and exon in another. "Exon 1" means nothing until the transcript is named, usually the MANE Select one that Ensembl and NCBI agree on.

Down to the reads

Every signal track from sequencing starts as reads, strings of 100 to 150 bases each aligned where it best fits the reference; in repeats, of Figure 1's window, several places fit equally well. Stacked, the reads form a pileup. Many reads disagreeing with the reference the same way at one base suggest a variant; a lone disagreement is more likely a sequencing error. Part 4's ChIP-seq tracks are pileups counted in bins.

Figure 3 is a pileup at TP53 codon 175, the R175H hotspot of Parts 1 and 4. The reference and conservation are real; the reads are simulated, each with a chance of carrying the mutation and about one base in miscalled. At the mutated base of reads carry T.

The same mutation is written two ways: VCF 17 7675088 . C T (1-based position, reference C, new base T) and BED chr17 7675087 7675088. Read the VCF number as a BED start and a program lands one base right; read the BED start as a 1-based position and it lands one base left. Both neighbours are G. A program that checks the reference base stops. One that doesn't finds of reads differing on the right and of on the left, and calls a hotspot mutation absent.

TP53 is on the minus strand, so it runs right to left in the genome view and its exon 1 has the highest coordinates. VCF describes the plus strand, where the change is C to T; the clinical name c.524G>A describes the transcript, where the same base is G. In the transcript view codon 175 reads CGC, arginine, which the mutation makes CAC, histidine. That base scores on phyloP, against for exon 5 as a whole and in the flanking introns.

One window at a time

A browser panel shows one contiguous stretch of one chromosome. IGV and the UCSC browser can split the screen into several regions, and IGV colours a read whose mate aligned to another chromosome, which is enough to follow a link or two. To see every link across a genome at once, the next part bends the chromosomes into a circle.
