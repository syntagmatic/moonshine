Differential Expression

Which genes changed between two conditions? Two plots of the same test results, the MA plot and the volcano plot, and what their thresholds let through.

The five-views essay predicted that CDKN1A expression should fall when p53 is broken. Checking a prediction like that, or finding genes nobody predicted, starts with a differential expression experiment: RNA sequencing of a few samples per condition, a count of reads for each of about 20,000 genes, and one statistical test per gene.

Everything on this page comes from one simulated experiment that runs in your browser. It has 2,000 genes. Each gene gets a baseline expression level, spread over several orders of magnitude as in real tissue, and 10% of the genes are given a true change between conditions. Read counts for each sample are drawn from a negative binomial distribution, the standard model for RNA-seq counts, whose variance grows faster than its mean. Each gene is then tested with a moderated t-test on log counts, and the p-values are adjusted for multiple testing with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Because the simulation knows which genes truly changed, it can also say which calls were right.

Two views of one table

The test produces, for each gene, an estimated log fold change and an adjusted p-value (often called a q-value). The two standard plots arrange the same table differently.

The MA plot puts each gene's average abundance on the horizontal axis (A, the mean log count) and its log fold change on the vertical axis (M, the difference in mean log counts between conditions):

\[ \begin{gathered} M = \overline{\log_2 \text{treated}} - \overline{\log_2 \text{control}} \\ A = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\overline{\log_2 \text{treated}} + \overline{\log_2 \text{control}}\right) \end{gathered} \]

The volcano plot puts the same log fold change on the horizontal axis and the strength of evidence, \(-\log_{10} q\), on the vertical. Hover or tap a gene in either panel to find it in the other.

Figure 1. The MA plot (left, or on top on a narrow screen) and the volcano plot. Both show the same 2,000 simulated genes. Red and blue genes pass both thresholds (up and down in the treated group); gray genes fail at least one. Dashed lines mark the thresholds. Changing the number of replicates reruns the experiment.

The MA plot shows a funnel: the spread of fold changes is widest at the left, among genes with few reads. A gene seen 3 times in one sample and 9 times in another has an apparent fourfold change that means little, while the same ratio between 3,000 and 9,000 reads is hard to get by chance. The volcano plot shows the consequence. Its two arms rise only where a large change and enough reads come together, and the low-count genes with large apparent changes stay near the bottom, because their tests have little power.

More replicates narrow the funnel and lift the arms, since each group mean is estimated from more samples. With only three samples per group, a gene's own variance estimate is itself very noisy, and a plain t-test on 2,000 genes finds almost nothing after correction. The test used here borrows strength across genes: each gene's variance is pulled toward the typical variance of genes with similar abundance before computing t, as in limma-trend (Ritchie et al., 2015). DESeq2 and edgeR do something similar within a negative binomial model.

What the thresholds let through

A candidate list usually requires both a minimum fold change and a maximum q-value. Either filter alone lets through a different kind of mistake. Figure 2 applies each filter to the same experiment and colors the genes that pass by whether they truly changed.

Figure 2. The experiment from Figure 1 under three filters, using its current thresholds. Green genes passed and truly changed; orange genes passed but did not change; gray genes failed the filter.

The fold-change filter alone admits many unchanged genes, around a third of its list at three replicates, mostly low-count genes whose noisy estimates happened to land far from zero. The q-value filter controls that. Benjamini-Hochberg is designed so that, on average, no more than a fraction q of the genes it calls are false discoveries, and the orange share in the middle panel stays below q. Here it is usually well below, because 90% of the genes are unchanged and the procedure is conservative in that case. The price at three replicates is power: the q filter finds only about a quarter of the truly changed genes.

Raise the replicates to 8 and the balance shifts. The q filter now finds most of the changed genes, including some whose change is real but small, and the fold-change threshold earns its place by trimming those to a shorter list of large, well-supported changes.
