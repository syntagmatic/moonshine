Differential Expression

Finding the genes that changed between two conditions takes one statistical test per gene. The MA and volcano plots show the results, and fold-change and q-value thresholds pick the candidates.

ChIP-seq showed where p53 binds near CDKN1A, but binding is not the same as transcription. Five Views predicted that CDKN1A expression should fall when p53 is broken. Testing that prediction, and finding the other genes p53 keeps switched on, starts with a differential expression experiment: RNA sequencing of a few samples per condition, a read count for each of about 20,000 protein-coding genes, and one statistical test per gene. The candidate list that comes out depends on two thresholds, one on the size of the change and one on the evidence for it, and each fails in its own way.

The figures use one simulated experiment: cells with working p53 (wildtype) against the same cells with TP53 knocked out, three samples of each to start, and 2,000 genes. Each gene gets a baseline expression level, spread over several orders of magnitude as in real tissue, and 10% of the genes are given a true change between conditions. Ten of the changed genes carry real names. CDKN1A, MDM2, BAX and seven others are direct p53 targets from a census of such genes (Fischer, 2017), and each is planted with a fall in the knockout whose size we chose. Read counts are drawn from a negative binomial distribution, the standard model for RNA-seq counts, with variance \(\mu + 0.08\,\mu^2\) for mean \(\mu\), so the variance grows faster than the mean. Each gene is tested with limma's moderated t-test on log counts, and the p-values are adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. The code records each gene's planted change, so every call can be graded.

MA and volcano plots

The test produces, for each gene, an estimated log fold change and an adjusted p-value (often called a q-value). The two standard plots arrange the same table differently.

The MA plot puts each gene's average abundance on the horizontal axis (A, the mean log count) and its log fold change on the vertical axis (M, the difference in mean log counts, knockout minus wildtype):

\[ \begin{gathered} M = \overline{\log_2 \text{knockout}} - \overline{\log_2 \text{wildtype}} \\ A = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\overline{\log_2 \text{knockout}} + \overline{\log_2 \text{wildtype}}\right) \end{gathered} \]

The volcano plot puts the same log fold change on the horizontal axis and the strength of evidence, \(-\log_{10} q\), on the vertical. Hover or tap a gene in either plot to find it in the other, or tab to a plot and step through the genes from the smallest q with the arrow keys.

Figure 1. The MA plot and the volcano plot of the same 2,000 simulated genes. Genes that pass both thresholds are colored by direction: higher or lower in the knockout; gray genes fail at least one. Dashed lines mark the thresholds. CDKN1A is ringed and labeled; the other nine named p53 targets have a thin ring. Changing the number of replicates measures the same genes with more or fewer samples; New experiment draws new genes.

The MA plot shows a funnel: the spread of fold changes is widest at the left, among genes with few reads. A gene seen 3 times in one sample and 9 times in another has an apparent threefold change that means little, while the same ratio between 3,000 and 9,000 reads is hard to get by chance. So the arms of the volcano rise only where a large change and enough reads come together, and most low-count genes with large apparent changes stay near the bottom, because their tests have little power.

CDKN1A is the ringed gene on the left arm: a fall of 2.38 log₂ units (planted 2.6) with q = 3.5e-5. That is the prediction from Five Views, found by a test that did not know where to look.

More replicates narrow the funnel and lift the arms, since each group mean is estimated from more samples. With three samples per group, a gene's own variance estimate rests on four degrees of freedom and is itself very noisy. A plain t-test on these 2,000 genes, followed by the same correction, found 4.9 genes on average over 200 simulated experiments, and none at all in 49 of them. limma's moderated t-test (Smyth, 2004) borrows strength across genes. It fits a prior variance \(s_0^2(A)\) that follows abundance, the trend of limma-trend (Law et al., 2014), and estimates from the spread of all the genes' variances how much that prior should count, as \(d_0\) extra degrees of freedom. Each gene's variance \(s^2\), on \(d = 2n - 2\) degrees of freedom, is replaced by

\[ \tilde s^2 = \frac{d_0\, s_0^2(A) + d\, s^2}{d_0 + d}, \]

and t is computed with \(\tilde s^2\) on \(d_0 + d\) degrees of freedom. In the experiment shown, \(d_0\) = 18.9, so the prior counts for about five times as much as the gene's own four degrees of freedom. The estimate is noisy in its own right: over 200 experiments with three replicates its median was 19.7, and in 11 of them it was infinite, which gives every gene the trend's variance. The JavaScript here gives the same \(d_0\) and p-values as limma 3.68.5 run on the same counts, to at least ten significant digits. DESeq2 and edgeR model the counts directly as negative binomial and shrink each gene's dispersion toward a trend fitted against mean count (Love et al., 2014). DESeq2 also shrinks the fold changes of low-count genes toward zero, which narrows the funnel itself, and by default sets aside genes too faint ever to reach significance before correcting, so the correction is paid over fewer tests (independent filtering, Bourgon et al., 2010).

Fold-change and q-value thresholds

A candidate list usually requires both a minimum fold change and a maximum q-value. Figure 2 grades each filter on its own and the two together, on the experiment of Figure 1 at three replicates and on the same genes measured with eight.

Figure 2. Volcano plots of the Figure 1 genes under three filters (|log₂ FC| ≥ 1.0 and q ≤ 0.05, from Figure 1's sliders), at three replicates and at eight. Each gene is colored by whether it passed and whether its change was planted. Three and eight replicates each have their own vertical scale, since eight give far smaller q-values.

The fold-change filter alone admits many unchanged genes: 59 of the 209 on its list at three replicates, and 26.8% of the list on average over 200 experiments. Nearly all of them are low-count genes whose noisy estimates happened to land far from zero. The q filter keeps those out. At three replicates it is also the only filter doing any work: every gene with q ≤ 0.05 already has |log₂ FC| ≥ 1.08, because with so few samples only a large change can reach significance, so the "both" panel repeats the "q only" panel.

At eight replicates the two filters part. The q filter finds 186 of the 221 changed genes, including real changes smaller than twofold, and requiring |log₂ FC| ≥ 1 as well cuts the list to 140. That cut is a choice about effect size, not about error: the genes it removes are mostly real, small changes. A list of genes whose change exceeds a threshold, with the error still controlled, comes from testing against the threshold directly (TREAT, McCarthy and Smyth, 2009) rather than filtering afterwards.

How Benjamini-Hochberg draws its line

With 2,000 tests, a p-value cutoff of 0.05 would let through about 90 unchanged genes by chance alone. Benjamini and Hochberg's procedure works on the ranked p-values instead. Sort the m p-values from smallest to largest, \(p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}\), find the largest rank k with

\[ p_{(k)} \le \frac{k}{m}\, q, \]

and call the k genes with the smallest p-values. Because it takes the largest such k, a gene can sit above the line and still be called, as long as a gene further down the ranking falls under it. Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) proved that for independent tests the false discovery rate, the expected unchanged share of the list, is at most q; their proof gives the tighter \((m_0/m)\,q\), where \(m_0\) is the number of unchanged genes. The histogram in Figure 3 shows where \(m_0\) can be seen: p-values of unchanged genes are spread evenly between 0 and 1, so they form a flat floor, while changed genes pile up near zero. Storey's estimate reads \(\pi_0 = m_0/m\) off the right half of the histogram, where nearly every gene is unchanged (Storey, 2002): here 0.900, against a true 0.890. With about 90% of the genes unchanged, the bound at q = 0.05 is 4.5%.

The histogram stacks the p-values of changed genes on those of unchanged genes (gray); the dashed line is Storey's estimate of the unchanged floor, \(\hat\pi_0 m / 20\) per bin. The rank plot shows each gene's p-value against its rank on log scales, with the line \(p = qk/m\); the genes ranked at or before the last crossing are called, colored changed or unchanged, and the vertical line marks that crossing, k. The strip reruns the simulation with 50 seeds (1 to 50) at the current replicate count and q, one dot per run, with the experiment shown above ringed.

The bound is a promise about the average over repeated experiments, not about one list. Over 200 simulated experiments at three replicates and q = 0.05, the unchanged share of the q list averaged 2.6%, under the 4.5% bound. In 25 of the 200 runs it went above 5%, the worst reached 9.2%, and 25 lists had no unchanged gene at all. At eight replicates the average rose to 3.8%, closer to the bound. With few replicates the test is a little conservative in the far tail: at three replicates, unchanged genes reached p < 0.001 in 0.047% of tests rather than the nominal 0.1%.

With three replicates, that control costs power, because most real changes cannot beat the noise of three samples. The q filter found 48.5% of the truly changed genes on average, against 84.4% at eight replicates. At two replicates it found 7.1%, and it missed CDKN1A, a planted sixfold fall in a well-expressed gene, in 105 of 200 experiments.
