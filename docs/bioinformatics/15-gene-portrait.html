Gene Portrait

A compact dashboard that collapses everything we have learned about a single gene into one printable page.

Every chart in this series has answered one question about one piece of evidence. A real-world read on a gene — the kind you would see on a clinical decision-support dashboard, a GeneCards entry, or the first page of a collaborator's slide deck — collapses all of those questions into a single page. The Gene Portrait is that summary view: six labeled mini-panels, each inheriting its vocabulary from one of the standalone techniques, arranged so that a biologist can read the whole story in under a minute.

The portrait below is built for TP53. The panels are not independent — the same patients are represented in each, sliced differently. When they move in the same direction, the story converges.

What makes the portrait a portrait — and not just a grid of unrelated charts — is that all six panels share a cohort axis. A biologist looking at this page has a single question in mind: "is the story consistent?" Every panel is an independent test of the same hypothesis, and the portrait lets the reader see at a glance whether evidence converges.

Reading the portrait

When the mutant cohort is selected, the story is categorical: the variant map lights up at the hotspot positions, the expression distribution of CDKN1A shifts downward, the promoter methylation track climbs toward fully silenced, the pathway's activating edge breaks, survival separates downward, and the forest plot's pooled effect is both significant and consistent across studies. Each of these on its own could be noise. Together they are a diagnosis.

A portrait is always specific to a claim. This one is built for "TP53 loss silences CDKN1A and worsens outcomes." A different question — say, "MDM2 amplification as an alternative route to the same functional state" — would need its own portrait with a different cohort axis. The template generalises; the conclusions do not.

Where this series leaves you

A reader who has followed every article now has the full bioinformatics visual vocabulary: heatmaps and dendrograms for raw matrices; MA and volcano plots for statistical filtering; UMAP for high-dimensional landscapes; genome browsers and circos plots for spatial and long-range context; survival curves, dot plots, and lollipops for clinical and protein-level reads; a consolidated triangulation article that walks one regulatory story through five modalities; ChIP-seq for physical binding; multi-omics heatmaps and waterfalls for integrating across layers; forest plots for meta-analytic rigour; and this portrait for the synthesis that lands on a single page.

The dashboards and tools you will encounter in practice — cBioPortal, GeneCards, TCGA's PanCancer Atlas viewers, your lab's in-house portal — are all elaborations on the same primitives. The vocabulary is finite; the questions you can ask with it are not.
