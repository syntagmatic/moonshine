MA Plot

How to visualize differential expression by separating magnitude of change from the average intensity of the signal.

When comparing gene expression between two conditions, a simple difference might be misleading. Genes with low expression levels often show high variability, producing large fold changes by chance alone. Bioinformaticians use the MA plot to identify real biological signals while accounting for this intensity dependent noise.

The MA plot transforms the data into two components:

\[ M = \log_2(C_1) - \log_2(C_2) \] \[ A = \frac{1}{2}(\log_2(C_1) + \log_2(C_2)) \]

Here, \( M \) (minus) represents the log fold change, while \( A \) (average) represents the average expression intensity. This rotation allows us to see how the magnitude of change behaves across different expression levels.

Intensity and Variation

In a typical experiment, most genes do not change. They cluster around the \( M = 0 \) horizontal line. As the average intensity \( A \) decreases, the spread of the data usually increases, creating a characteristic funnel shape. This reveals why a high fold change is more significant when it occurs in a highly expressed gene.

Figure 1. An MA plot of simulated gene expression. Points represent individual genes. Highlighting helps distinguish between genes that exceed a specific fold change threshold across different expression levels.

The funnel shape is a technical artifact of sequencing. Low intensity measurements are less precise, leading to higher observed variance. By visualizing this, researchers can avoid over-interpreting noisy data from rare transcripts.

Comparing Perspectives

A direct scatter plot of two conditions can also show differences, but it often obscures the most interesting outliers. The MA transformation effectively flattens the diagonal of a standard scatter plot, making it easier to see deviations from the norm.

Figure 2. A direct comparison of control versus treated expression. While the diagonal represents unchanged genes, the MA plot in Figure 1 provides a clearer view of the magnitude of change relative to the baseline signal.

Identifying Candidates

Selection often involves filtering for genes that show both high expression and a large fold change. These candidates are the most likely to be biologically meaningful and technically reproducible.

Figure 3. Interactive selection. By brushing a region of the plot, you can extract a list of candidate genes for further investigation or pathway analysis.
