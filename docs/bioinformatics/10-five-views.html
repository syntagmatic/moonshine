Five Views of a Broken Pathway

One regulatory story, TP53 silencing CDKN1A, and the five data modalities that triangulate it across scales.

So far the series has walked through individual visualisations one at a time. Real genomics is rarely so neat. When a lab is investigating a candidate oncogenic event, say, why TP53 dysfunction drives so many cancers, they pull data from every layer they can get their hands on: population-scale association studies, tumor cohort mutation maps, epigenomic silencing signals, literature pathway topologies, and curated interaction databases. No single plot answers the question. The triangulation is the answer.

This explainer assembles one such triangulation. We take TP53's loss of activation of the cell-cycle brake CDKN1A (p21) as a running example, and look at it five different ways. Each view adds a layer of evidence the others cannot provide.

View 1, Population: a Manhattan plot

Scale: millions of people · billions of base pairs

Population genetics starts the zoom: before asking about any one tumor, does inherited variation near our gene of interest associate with disease risk? A genome-wide association study (GWAS) tests millions of variants in parallel. The Manhattan plot lays each variant out by its genomic position (x) and statistical evidence (\(-\log_{10}(p)\), y). Loci that rise far above the noise floor are candidate risk regions.

Figure 1. Genome-wide scan across 22 autosomes. Drag the threshold. The two named peaks sit near TP53 (chr17) and CDKN1A (chr6). Hover any SNP for its position and p-value. Synthetic data.

Two loci stand above the genome-wide significance line: one on chromosome 17 near TP53, another on chromosome 6 near CDKN1A. That is suggestive, GWAS hits are statistical, not mechanistic. We still do not know what is broken in these regions or how it contributes to disease. But the hits tell us where to look next.

View 2, Cohort: an oncoprint

Scale: hundreds of patients · dozens of genes

Zoom in from population variation to tumor samples. Each tumor has its own cocktail of somatic alterations: point mutations, truncations, copy-number gains and losses. The oncoprint compresses a whole cohort into a single grid, columns are patients, rows are genes, colored marks are alteration events. Sort the columns by TP53 status and the story pops out visually.

Sort columns:

Figure 2. 80 synthetic patients × 8 genes. Sorted rows show gene alteration frequency on the right. Hover a cell for patient + gene + alteration type. Sorting on TP53 exposes co-occurrence: MDM2 amplifications cluster in the TP53-wildtype stripe (amplified MDM2 is itself a way to inactivate TP53 — a second route to the same broken function).

The oncoprint answers frequency. It shows that TP53 is altered in a large fraction of patients and that those alterations are mutually exclusive with MDM2 amplification, a classic signature of convergent inactivation. What it does not tell us is whether the downstream consequence (silencing of CDKN1A) actually happens in these patients. For that we need a different modality.

View 3, Epigenome: DNA methylation

Scale: a single gene promoter · hundreds of CpG sites

Mutation is not the only way to silence a gene. Even in tumors with intact TP53, the CDKN1A promoter can be hyper-methylated, blocking transcription factor binding directly. The β value at each CpG site reports methylation level on a 0–1 scale; zoom into the promoter and compare groups of patients.

Show:

Figure 3. Top: CpG-level β values across 1.5kb of the CDKN1A promoter. Dots are individual CpGs (hover for position and β). The TSS sits at 0. Bottom: per-sample heatmap — rows are patients, columns are CpGs — revealing that not every sample in the "methylated" group silences the entire promoter.

The methylation view uncovers an alternative route to the same functional outcome. A patient whose CDKN1A is hypermethylated may look mutation-normal in the oncoprint but behave exactly like a TP53-deficient tumor functionally. Multimodal evidence catches what any single modality misses.

View 4, Signaling: pathway topology

Scale: a handful of proteins · known biochemistry

The first three views were quantitative. The fourth is structural: a literature-curated picture of how the signal is supposed to flow. The pathway diagram encodes activation and inhibition relationships from decades of biochemistry. Press the damage trigger to watch the signal propagate, and to see which branches are lost when TP53 cannot activate its targets.

Figure 4. The canonical TP53 response. Activating edges are arrows; inhibitory edges end in bars. The mutant toggle disables TP53's transcriptional activity — watch CDKN1A and BAX fail to light up. Click any node for a one-line description.

The topology explains the consequence. If TP53 cannot bind and activate, DNA damage fails to produce the cell-cycle arrest signal, and the cell keeps dividing through the damage. The oncoprint told us the lesion is frequent; the pathway tells us why it matters.

View 5, Interactome: the PPI neighborhood

Scale: one hub · its first-degree neighbors

The curated pathway above is a clean story. Reality is messier: TP53 participates in dozens of direct physical interactions, and its first-degree neighborhood in the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network reveals how much collateral wiring depends on it. The force-directed layout lets the connections speak for themselves.

Highlight:

Figure 5. Drag any protein. Node size scales with degree. Edge thickness scales with reported evidence weight. TP53 and CDKN1A are highlighted as the anchors. "Shared partners" finds proteins that both anchors talk to — these are the bottleneck nodes whose behaviour is most constrained by the axis.

Five scales, one story

None of these views, taken alone, settles the question of how TP53 loss drives disease. The Manhattan plot names candidate regions but cannot assign mechanism. The oncoprint counts events but cannot tell you which mechanism inactivated the gene. The methylation panel catches a silencing route invisible to mutation calling. The pathway explains the functional consequence. The PPI shows how many other proteins are affected indirectly. Evidence that converges across all five is far more defensible than evidence from any one modality, and the practice of assembling these triangulations is what most of a bioinformatician's day actually looks like.
