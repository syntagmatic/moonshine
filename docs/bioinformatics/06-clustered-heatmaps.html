Clustered Heatmaps

Clustering a matrix of measurements finds genes that move together and patients that look alike. Stacking several kinds of data can show two routes to the same phenotype.

After normalization, an expression experiment is a matrix with genes as rows, samples as columns and one abundance estimate in each cell. Differential expression tests the rows one at a time. A heatmap looks at the whole matrix at once, to find genes that move together and samples that look alike. This part covers how those groups are found and how to check whether they are stable.

A heatmap replaces the numbers with colors. In genomics the most useful first view is often row-scaled: each gene is centered on its own average, so the color shows that gene's pattern across samples and ignores its absolute abundance.

Clustering

Hierarchical clustering starts with a distance between expression profiles, then repeatedly joins the closest genes or clusters. The result is a tree called a dendrogram, in which nearby leaves have similar profiles under the chosen distance and linkage rule.

Here the clustering uses row-scaled expression and a correlation distance with average linkage, a common choice when the pattern across samples matters more than a gene's absolute abundance.

Row z-score: red means above that gene's average, blue means below.

Reading the dendrogram

Each leaf of the tree is a gene, and the height where two branches merge is the dissimilarity at which those groups were joined. Low merges mean tight groups. High merges join groups that stayed apart until late.

Figure 2. Slicing the dendrogram. Lowering the cut height asks for tighter groups, so the same tree breaks into more clusters.

Nothing in the tree says where to cut it. Every cut height gives a valid set of clusters, and hierarchical clustering will produce a tree even from data with no groups at all. Deciding how many groups the data supports needs another tool, such as the consensus clustering described below.

Multi-omics heatmaps

A gene can be inactivated by mutation, promoter hypermethylation, deletion of its chromosome region, or overactivation of its negative regulator. A multi-omics heatmap stacks these measurements with patients as the shared columns. Expression and methylation are not comparable in raw units, so each row is normalized on its own scale before it is drawn, and the aligned patterns are read side by side.

The cohort below is synthetic. Its 80 patients were generated from four planted subtypes, built to echo the routes to p53 pathway failure in the five-views essay: TP53-mutant tumors with low CDKN1A and a methylated CDKN1A promoter, MDM2-amplified tumors with wildtype TP53, hypermutated tumors with a high mutation burden, and quiet tumors with few alterations and a better outcome.

Sorting twenty of these patients by CDKN1A expression alone produces a tidy continuum. Stacking methylation, mutation, and copy number underneath splits the low-CDKN1A half into two different groups: TP53-mutant patients and MDM2-amplified patients.

Figure 3. Twenty synthetic patients sorted by CDKN1A expression, low on the left. Thin grey lines between the panels join each patient's column in both views. Hover, tap or tab to a column for the full multi-modal profile. The left-side bracket shows the two planted subgroups that the single "CDKN1A-low" label lumps together.

Consensus clustering

The full cohort matrix needs a column order, and the order is itself a claim about which patients belong together. Consensus clustering (Monti et al., 2003) makes that claim with a measure of its own stability. Here it runs on all 23 features (six expression values, six promoter CpGs, three copy-number loci and eight mutation calls; the continuous ones standardized, the mutation calls left as 0 or 1). It draws 80% of the patients at random, clusters them into four groups with k-means, and repeats that 100 times. For every pair of patients it records the fraction of the draws containing both in which they landed in the same cluster. A final average-linkage clustering of those fractions gives the subtypes. The planted labels are never used.

Sorting by consensus cluster keeps the TP53-mutant and MDM2-amplified patients apart even though both lose p53 function. A sort by TP53 status alone hides the MDM2 amplifications inside the wildtype columns.

The consensus matrix itself shows how firm the grouping is. Each cell is the fraction of resamples in which two patients clustered together; patients are ordered by their computed subtype, and the strips along the edges show computed subtype (left) and planted subtype (top).

Figure 5. The consensus matrix: the more a cell stands out from the background, the more often those two patients clustered together; background-colored cells mean never. Hover or tap a cell for the value.

The procedure recovers the planted subtype for 74 of the 80 patients. Most of the six misses are hypermutated tumors whose profiles sit close to the quiet group.

Dark, sharp-edged blocks on the diagonal mean the four groups come back in nearly every resample. Gray cells between blocks mark patients whose assignment depends on which other patients were drawn. With real data, running the same procedure for several values of k and comparing how clean the blocks are is one common way to choose the number of clusters.

The clinical strips are context and were kept out of the clustering. If survival status or stage leaks into clustering, the subtype labels become a circular explanation of the outcome they later claim to predict. Whether the subtypes differ in outcome is a separate question, for the survival analysis essay.

The heatmaps here show a few dozen genes or features. Dimensionality Reduction handles single-cell data with around 20,000 genes per cell by placing each cell as a point, so that cells with similar expression land close together.
