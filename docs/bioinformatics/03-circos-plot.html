Circos Plot

A genome browser shows one chromosome at a time. The circos plot wraps the whole genome into a ring, so rearrangements between distant chromosomes can be drawn as ribbons across it.

A genome browser shows one chromosome at a time. Many events span more than that. The Philadelphia translocation in leukemia creates a derivative chromosome 22 carrying BCR::ABL1, other structural variants join distant loci, and blocks of synteny were shuffled between mouse and human over the roughly 75 to 90 million years since their lineages split.

The circos plot, published by Martin Krzywinski and colleagues in 2009, gives every chromosome equal visual access to every other. Chromosomes wrap the circle as banded arcs, concentric tracks inside the ring carry data such as mutations and copy number, and ribbons across the middle connect distant loci.

A cancer genome

Figure 1 is a simulated tumor genome. All 24 chromosomes form the outer ring, sized from rounded GRCh38 lengths and banded with the GRCh38 G-stain ideogram. The chromosomes, bands and gene positions are real. The mutation, copy-number and methylation values are random, and so are most of the rearrangements. Copy number is drawn red for gain and blue for loss. One ribbon, labeled in red, is the BCR::ABL1 t(9;22)(q34;q11.2) translocation that produces the Philadelphia chromosome.

Figure 1. A simulated tumor genome on a real GRCh38 scaffold. Rings (outer-in): cytogenetic ideogram with 25-Mb axis ticks, gene labels for selected oncogenes, mutation-density histogram, copy-number scatter, methylation heatmap. Ribbons across the middle are structural variants, colored by type and sized by each event's simulated size. Track values and the unlabeled ribbons are random; the labeled BCR::ABL1, MYC and TP53 events sit at their real loci.

The figure folds together views a clinical-genomics team might otherwise inspect separately: karyotype, copy-number profile, mutation map, methylation and a list of structural variants. It is poor for looking up exact coordinates. What it shows at a glance is whether a genome is quiet, has a few local events, or is full of long-range breakpoints.

The ideogram

Without the tracks and ribbons, what is left is the ideogram: a ring of arcs, one per chromosome, sized by length in megabases. A full ideogram also draws cytobands, the alternating dark and light Giemsa-stained bands of cytogenetics. They are approximate landmarks from microscopy and FISH mapping and do not have exact base-pair boundaries. This one draws the GRCh38 bands at about the 850-band level, darker for more strongly stained, and marks the centromere in dark red, the boundary between the short p arm and the long q arm.

Figure 2. The bare scaffold: 24 human chromosomes (22 autosomes, X and Y) drawn to GRCh38 lengths, with G-bands and centromeres from the UCSC hg38 cytoband table. The outer ticks mark 25-Mb intervals, labeled every 50 Mb (every 100 Mb on a narrow screen). Hover or tap a chromosome (or focus the plot and use the arrow keys) for a small karyogram in the center showing its arms, centromere and pinned oncogenes.

Layered tracks

Inside the ideogram are data tracks. Circos offers more track types than this, among them text labels, connectors and highlights, but five carry most quantitative data:

  • Histogram, a bar per genomic bin (e.g. mutation density)
  • Scatter, point per measurement (e.g. copy-number log2 ratio)
  • Line, continuous signal (e.g. GC content)
  • Heatmap, colored cells per bin (e.g. methylation %)
  • Tile, discrete features at positions (e.g. genes, repeats)

Figure 1 used four of these. Figure 3 stacks all five. By convention, schematic annotations go outside and quantitative signals inside.

Ribbons

The interior of the disc holds links between pairs of genomic positions. A thin chord shows only that two loci are related. A ribbon also encodes the size of the rearranged segment in the width of each end, and its color gives the type.

Figure 4. The simulated structural variants of Figure 1, drawn two ways. Thin links treat every event equally; ribbons give each end a width from the event's simulated size, capped at a fifth of its chromosome, so the 40-Mb duplication on chromosome 1 draws about four times wider than the 9-Mb translocation end on chromosome 18.

The Philadelphia chromosome

The BCR::ABL1 ribbon in Figure 1 has a long history. In 1960, Peter Nowell and David Hungerford described an unusually small chromosome in chronic myeloid leukemia. In 1973, Janet Rowley showed that it was not a simple deletion: material had been reciprocally exchanged between chromosomes 9 and 22. The derivative chromosome 22 carries BCR::ABL1, an oncogenic tyrosine kinase. Imatinib, designed to inhibit that kinase, helped turn CML into a manageable chronic disease for many patients.

A single event is easier to read when it gets the whole circle. Figure 5 draws chromosomes 9 and 22 alone, with the t(9;22) translocation as one thick ribbon and copy number for context.

Figure 5. The Philadelphia chromosome as a Circos zoom. With only the two chromosomes involved, the breakpoints at chr9q34 (ABL1) and chr22q11.2 (BCR) have room to be labeled. Gene and band positions are GRCh38; the ribbon ends are widened for legibility, and the copy-number track is simulated.

Synteny

Circos was originally built for comparative genomics. The ribbons that connect breakpoints in one genome can also connect conserved blocks between two. Figure 6 puts human chromosomes 1 to 6 on the right and, on the left, the 17 mouse chromosomes their conserved blocks land on. The blocks are real, taken from the UCSC alignment net between the human (GRCh38) and mouse (GRCm39) assemblies.

Where ribbons land in the same rank order, the ancestral order has been kept. Where they cross, inversions and translocations have rearranged it. The first half of human chromosome 4 runs in order along mouse chromosome 5, while human chromosome 3 is split across six mouse chromosomes. Color follows the source human chromosome.

Figure 6. Human–mouse synteny from the UCSC hg38 netMm39 alignment net. Each ribbon is a conserved block covering at least 3 Mb of human sequence, and its thickness encodes block size. Smaller blocks are left out, which is why some stretches of human sequence have no ribbon.

When to use it

Circos plots suit questions about pattern: how many connections there are, where they cluster, and which chromosomes behave like hubs. They are poor for precise quantitative reading, since arc lengths are hard to compare and bar heights on a Cartesian plot are easy. For precise coordinates and values, a genome browser or a Manhattan plot does better.
