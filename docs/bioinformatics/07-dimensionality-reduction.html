Dimensionality Reduction

A cell measured on twenty thousand genes cannot be plotted. A 2D map of cells is a compression, and each method keeps a different part of the data.

Clustered Heatmaps drew every value of a matrix with a few dozen rows. A single-cell RNA-seq experiment measures around 20,000 genes in each of tens of thousands of cells, so each cell is a point in a 20,000-dimensional space. The dot plot in Five Views summarized cells by type; the types themselves are usually found on a 2D map made by dimensionality reduction, which places cells so that those with similar expression land close together. The two common methods keep different things. PCA keeps the large distances and blurs the small ones. t-SNE keeps each cell's nearest neighbors and lets the large distances go. Neither can keep a pattern that the distances themselves no longer contain.

Both run in this page on simulated data: principal component analysis by eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix, and t-SNE by gradient descent on its objective, animated as it runs. The PCA essay of the algorithms series covers the mechanics of PCA step by step.

A t-SNE map of cells

Every point in a single-cell map is a row in a cell × gene matrix. The data here are 500 simulated immune cells, 100 of each of five types, measured on 24 genes: markers for each type and four genes expressed in every cell. The gene names are real markers; the expression levels are invented. T cells run on a gradient from naive (CCR7) to memory (S100A4), and monocytes from classical (CD14) to non-classical (FCGR3A), so cells of one type are not all alike. Each cell's counts are drawn from its library size, the number of its transcripts that were captured and sequenced. Library size differs …-fold between the smallest and largest cell here, for technical reasons, and …% of the matrix is zero because a gene's transcripts were not captured. Real data has more zeros, since most of its 20,000 genes are expressed at low levels.

Before any map is made, each cell's counts are divided by its library size, scaled to 10,000 and log-transformed: a count becomes log(1 + 10,000 × count / library size). This is Seurat's default normalization, and the Scanpy tutorials do the same. Without it, a cell that happened to be sequenced deeper differs from the rest of its type in every gene at once.

t-SNE (van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008) turns distances between cells into probabilities that one cell would pick another as a neighbor, then moves points on the page until the neighbor probabilities there match. The run uses scikit-learn's default optimizer settings (perplexity 30, early exaggeration 12 for the first 250 iterations, a learning rate of the larger of n/48 and 50, which is 50 here, and 1,000 iterations), from a seeded random start where scikit-learn would start from PCA. By the last iteration the objective has stopped falling: it changes by … over the final 100 iterations. The page computes exponentials and logarithms with its own arithmetic rather than the browser's, which can differ in the last digit, so every browser draws this same map. Cells of the same type share markers, so they are each other's neighbors: … of each cell's 10 nearest neighbors on the map are its own type, although t-SNE never saw the type labels.

Switch to raw counts and the five islands survive, but inside each one the cells line up by library size. Within a type, the distance between two cells across the 24 genes now tracks the difference in their library sizes (correlation …, against … after normalization), and t-SNE keeps that order. A map of unnormalized cells sorts each type by how well its RNA was captured. Figure 3 can color the map by library size to show it.

PCA and t-SNE on noisy data

Principal component analysis (PCA) projects onto the directions of largest variance. It is exact, fast and linear, and after normalization it is the usual first reduction step in a single-cell pipeline. It can miss a signal, though, when the directions of largest variance come from something else: batch effects, technical noise, or biology unrelated to the question.

Figure 2 sets that up with a known answer. 200 points lie on two interlocking half-moons in two dimensions, and ten more dimensions of pure noise are added, so the moons occupy two of 12 dimensions. Under each panel, a score counts how many of each point's 10 nearest neighbors on the page come from its own moon: 100% for the original two dimensions, about 50% for a layout that has lost the moons entirely.

At the starting noise of 0.3, PCA keeps the moons: … of neighbors stay on their own moon, because the two moon dimensions have the most variance and the top two components lie close to them. t-SNE on all 12 dimensions scores a little lower (…), because it preserves neighborhoods as measured in the full space, where every distance adds ten noise dimensions to the two that carry the moons. t-SNE cannot recover structure that the distances no longer contain.

Raise the noise to … and PCA's second component stops following the moons' height and becomes a noise direction. The height has the smaller variance of the two moon dimensions, and with 200 points the largest sample variance among ten pure-noise dimensions is about 1.5 times their true variance, so noise overtakes the height first. PCA's score drops in one step to …, while t-SNE on all 12 dimensions, which still sees the height, keeps 82% at 0.4 and 78% at 0.5 (perplexity 30, first random start). At 0.9 PCA loses the width as well and scores …, about chance, against 61% for t-SNE.

Switching the t-SNE input to the top two PCs shows when PCA helps t-SNE and when it hurts. Below the step, t-SNE on two components scores 98 to 100% at every perplexity from 5 to 80: PCA has dropped the noise directions and kept the moons. From 0.4 to 0.5 the height is already gone from the top two components, and t-SNE on them scores 72 to 74%, no better than PCA, against 74 to 88% from all 12 dimensions. Single-cell pipelines run t-SNE or UMAP on the top 10 to 50 principal components rather than on raw genes for the first reason, and keep that many components to avoid the second. Perplexity (roughly, how many neighbors each point tries to keep) and the random start move these scores by a few points. Averaged over three random starts, all 12 dimensions beat the top two PCs from 0.4 to 0.5 at every perplexity, though at noise 0.5 and perplexity 80 only by about one point.

Reading the map

A t-SNE or UMAP map has no meaningful axes. Cluster identity comes from marker-gene overlays: the island that lights up for CD3D is a T-cell island. Both methods try to preserve each point's local neighborhood and make little effort to preserve large distances, so two islands far apart on the page may be similar cell types, and island sizes do not reflect how spread out a population is (Wattenberg, Viégas and Johnson, 2016).

Selective markers like CD3D or MS4A1 light one island each, while PTPRC, expressed by all immune cells, lights all of them. CCR7 is high at one end of the T-cell island and fades toward the other: the island's long axis follows the simulated naive-to-memory gradient (correlation …), so the map keeps a gradient inside a type as well as the types. FCGR3A lights the NK island and part of the monocyte island, whose gradient the map lays out less cleanly. Library size lights nothing in particular on the normalized map; on the raw map it runs from one end of every island to the other. The neighbor lines check the map against the data it came from. … of each cell's 10 nearest neighbors in 24 dimensions are its own type, so the lines stay inside one island. They do not all stay short: on average only … of a cell's 10 nearest neighbors in 24 dimensions are also among its 10 nearest on the map. Within one type, what separates two cells is mostly counting noise spread over many genes at once, and two dimensions cannot hold all of it, so the exact neighbors are partly lost even where the islands are right. The share is about the same in B cells, which have no gradient, as in T cells, which do.

What each method keeps, by scale

The neighbor lines suggest a measure. For each cell, take its k nearest neighbors in 24 dimensions and its k nearest on the map, and count how many are in both. Averaged over cells, that share is the neighbor recall at k. A perfect map scores 1 at every k, and a random layout about k/499. Plotting recall against k shows what a method keeps at each scale, from a cell's closest neighbor to the whole data set.

A t-SNE map answers which cells resemble this one, and a PCA projection answers how far apart the groups are. Clusters and neighbors are read from the first, distances between groups from the second, and both need normalized counts, because each keeps whatever the distances contain, library size included.
