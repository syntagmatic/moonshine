Dimensionality Reduction

A cell measured on twenty thousand genes cannot be plotted. A 2D map of cells is a compression, and it helps to know what each method keeps and what it throws away.

A single-cell RNA-seq experiment measures around 20,000 genes in each of tens of thousands of cells. Each cell is a point in a 20,000-dimensional space, which can't be drawn. Dimensionality reduction produces 2D coordinates such that cells with similar expression land close together on the page.

Every embedding on this page is computed in your browser from synthetic data: principal component analysis by eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix, and t-SNE by gradient descent on its objective, animated as it runs. The mechanics of PCA are covered step by step in the PCA essay of the algorithms series; this page is about what happens when these methods meet expression data.

From a matrix to a scatter

Every point in a single-cell map is a row in a cell × gene matrix. The data here are 200 simulated immune cells of five types, measured on 24 genes: marker genes for each type, a few genes expressed everywhere, and some that carry only noise. The expression values include the zeros that dominate real single-cell data, where a gene's transcripts are often simply not captured.

Figure 1. The 200 × 24 expression matrix, rows grouped by cell type (colored strip), and beside it (below it on a narrow screen) a t-SNE embedding of the same rows, computed from the matrix alone; the colors are added afterwards from the known types. Hover or tap either side to find the same cell in the other.

t-SNE (van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008) turns distances between cells into probabilities that one cell would pick another as a neighbour, then moves points on the page until the neighbour probabilities there match. Cells of the same type share markers, so they are each other's neighbours and end up in the same island. The t-SNE never saw the type labels.

When linear methods miss the structure

Principal component analysis (PCA) projects onto the directions of largest variance. It is exact, fast and linear, and it is usually the first step of a single-cell pipeline. It can miss a signal, though, when the directions of largest variance come from something else: batch effects, technical noise, or biology unrelated to the question.

Figure 2 builds that case on purpose. It places 200 points on two interlocking half-moons in two dimensions, then adds ten more dimensions of pure noise. The points now live in 12 dimensions, and the moons occupy only two of them. Under each panel, a score counts how many of each point's 10 nearest neighbours on the page come from its own moon: 100% for the original two dimensions, about 50% for a layout that has lost the moons entirely.

Third: t-SNE, of either all 12 dimensions or the top two principal components. Hover or tap a point to find it in all three panels. Releasing a slider regenerates the data or reruns t-SNE.

A common story is that PCA fails on curved structure and t-SNE rescues it. On this data the computation says otherwise. At low noise, the moon dimensions have the most variance, PCA's top two components are close to them, and PCA keeps nearly every point's neighbours on its own moon. t-SNE on all 12 dimensions does worse at low and moderate noise, because it preserves neighbourhoods as measured in the full space, and there every distance is a sum over ten noise dimensions and two signal dimensions. t-SNE cannot recover structure that the distances no longer contain. Push the noise up to about 0.9, where each noise dimension has as much variance as the moons, and PCA loses the moons too: its top components are now noise directions and its score falls to about 50%, while t-SNE holds on to a little more, around 60%.

Switching the t-SNE input to the top two PCs shows why single-cell pipelines run t-SNE or UMAP on the top 30 to 50 principal components instead of on raw genes. PCA discards the low-variance directions first, and t-SNE then works on distances that the discarded noise no longer blurs. That only helps when the signal is among the high-variance directions. The perplexity (roughly, how many neighbours each point tries to keep) and the random start change the layout without changing this.

Reading the embedding

A t-SNE or UMAP map has no meaningful axes. Cluster identity comes from marker-gene overlays: the island that lights up for CD3D is a T-cell island. Distances between islands are much less trustworthy than who sits next to whom. Both methods try to preserve each point's local neighbourhood and make little effort to preserve large distances, so two islands far apart on the page may be similar cell types, and island sizes do not reflect how spread out a population is.

Figure 3. The embedding from Figure 1. Click a gene to recolor cells by its expression. Hovering or tapping a cell draws lines to its nearest neighbours measured in the original 24 dimensions: green when the neighbour is the same cell type, red when it is not.

Selective markers like CD3D or MS4A1 light one island each, while PTPRC, expressed by all immune cells, lights all of them. The neighbour lines check the map against the data it came from. In this data, 99% of each cell's 10 nearest neighbours in 24 dimensions are the same cell type, and the lines mostly stay inside one island. They do not stay short: the exact neighbours of a cell are often spread across its island, because t-SNE keeps who belongs to which neighbourhood better than it keeps the order of distances inside one.
