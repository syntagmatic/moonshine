Heatmap & Dendrogram

What gene expression data looks like, and how clustering reveals hidden structure in a matrix of measurements.

Imagine measuring how active every gene is in a cell. Not one gene, but thousands. Not one sample, but dozens: tumor versus healthy tissue, treated versus untreated, different patients. The result is a matrix: rows of genes, columns of samples, and a number in every cell representing expression level.

This matrix is too large to read as a table. Bioinformaticians turn it into a heatmap, replacing numbers with colors. Patterns that were invisible in a spreadsheet become visible instantly.

Hover for expression values

Figure 1. A raw expression matrix. Without ordering, the data looks like noise. Red indicates high expression (upregulation), while blue indicates low expression (downregulation).

Clustering: Letting the Data Organize Itself

Hierarchical clustering solves the ordering problem. It measures how similar each pair of genes is, then groups the most similar ones together. The result is a tree called a dendrogram, where nearby branches share similar expression patterns.

A heatmap is only as good as its ordering. The same data can look like noise or like clear structure depending on how rows and columns are arranged.

Figure 2. The same data reordered by similarity. Blocks of co-expressed genes emerge, revealing biological programs that respond to the experiment's conditions.

Reading the Dendrogram

The tree on the left of the heatmap is a dendrogram. Each leaf is a gene. The height where two branches merge tells you how dissimilar they are. Low merges mean tight, coherent groups.

Figure 3. Slicing the dendrogram. Lowering the cut height increases the number of clusters, allowing you to choose the resolution that best fits your biological question.

Integrated Evidence

In practice, heatmaps are often paired with metadata: sample conditions, patient ages, or known gene functions. When clusters align with these annotations, it suggests that the expression patterns are driven by the biology you're studying.

Figure 4. An annotated heatmap. Colored bars represent sample conditions (top) and gene pathways (left), making it easy to see if a cluster is enriched for a specific biological function.
