Multi-Omics Heatmap

Stacking expression, methylation, mutation, and copy number data on a shared patient axis to reveal biological convergence.

Every earlier explainer in this series examines one kind of molecular data at a time. But real biology is layered. A gene can be silenced by a mutation, by methylation of its promoter, or by deletion of its chromosomal region. A multi-omics heatmap stacks these layers vertically and asks a simple question: do they tell the same story?

The columns represent patients. The rows, grouped into panels, represent different measurements. When we sort the columns by a primary variable, such as TP53 mutation status, patterns emerge across panels that reveal whether expression, methylation, and copy number agree on which samples are disrupted.

Figure 1. Forty patients sorted by TP53 status. The top bar marks mutant (red) and wild-type (blue) samples. Panels below show CDKN1A expression, methylation, mutation, and copy number. Hover a column to highlight a patient across all layers.

Notice how TP53 mutant patients tend to show higher CDKN1A expression and lower promoter methylation. This convergence across independent data types reinforces the signal that might be noisy in any single layer.

Panel Integration

Each panel provides a partial view. Remove one and the picture becomes ambiguous. Use the checkboxes below to show or hide panels and observe how the remaining evidence supports the biological narrative.

Figure 2. Toggle panels to see how each layer contributes. With all four visible, the convergent pattern is clear. The shared patient axis ensures that every measurement is correctly aligned for comparison.

Cross-Omics Correlation

The heatmap visualizes patterns, but a scatter plot quantifies relationships. Click two row labels from different panels below to see how those variables relate across the cohort. For example, clicking CDKN1A expression and then a methylation site reveals the expected anti-correlation between promoter methylation and gene activity.

Click two row labels from different panels above to generate a scatter plot.

Figure 3. Interactive correlation. Points are colored by TP53 status. Anti-correlated variables, such as expression versus methylation, appear as a downward trend.

Cross-omics correlations are the backbone of integrative genomics. A gene whose expression tracks with its copy number is likely dosage sensitive, while anti-correlation with methylation suggests epigenetic regulation.
