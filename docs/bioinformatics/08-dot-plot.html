Dot Plot

Two numbers per dot: which cell types express a gene, and how strongly? The dot plot encodes both in a single matrix.

Once you have clusters and candidate genes, a natural question follows: which cell types actually express each gene? A heatmap could answer this, but it only encodes one quantity per cell. In single-cell data, averages can be misleading. A gene might have a high mean in a cluster because every cell expresses it slightly, or because a few cells express it intensely while most stay silent.

The dot plot solves this by encoding two quantities in every dot. The dot's size shows the fraction of cells in the cluster that express the gene. The dot's color shows the average expression among those expressing cells. Together, these two channels distinguish broadly expressed housekeeping genes from sporadically but intensely expressed markers.

Reading a Dot Plot

Each gene in the matrix below was chosen as a known marker for a particular population. Among these markers, look for TP53 and CDKN1A. TP53 is broadly expressed across many cell types as a general tumor suppressor. CDKN1A, its downstream target, should track TP53 but with variation: some cell types activate the p53 pathway more vigorously than others.

Mutation Disrupts a Pathway

The R175H mutation in TP53 produces a protein that folds incorrectly and cannot activate its transcriptional targets. Critically, the mRNA is still transcribed and may even accumulate. In single-cell RNA-seq, TP53 mRNA looks normal or elevated, while its functional output collapses.

The dot plot below compares cells from a wildtype tumor with cells from a TP53-mutant tumor. Look at CDKN1A: in the mutant panel, both the fraction of expressing cells and the expression level drop across most cell types. This is the pathway disruption made visible. TP53 mRNA itself stays present, masking the functional catastrophe happening downstream.
