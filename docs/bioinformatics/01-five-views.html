Five Views of a Broken Pathway

How TP53 loss can leave CDKN1A (p21) switched off, seen through five kinds of data, from a patient cohort down to single cells.

Investigating a candidate cancer driver pulls in several kinds of data at once: cohort mutation maps, mutation positions on the protein, epigenomic signals, pathway diagrams and expression in single cells. Each answers a different part of the question.

The example here is the tumor suppressor TP53, whose protein p53 switches on CDKN1A (the gene for p21, a brake on the cell cycle) after DNA damage, and the question is how TP53 loss can leave p21 switched off. The datasets are synthetic, built so the same story shows up in every panel. The hotspot positions and the pathway wiring are real, and the patient counts and expression values are simulated.

View 1, Cohort: an oncoprint

Each tumor carries its own set of somatic alterations: point mutations, truncations, copy-number gains and losses. The oncoprint compresses a cohort into one grid, with patients as columns, genes as rows and colored marks for alteration events.

Sort columns:

Figure 1. 80 synthetic patients × 9 genes. Alteration frequency for each gene is on the right. Hover or tap a cell for the patient, gene and alteration type. Sorting by TP53 status puts the MDM2 amplifications in the TP53-wildtype stripe.

The oncoprint shows frequency. TP53 alterations are common, and in this synthetic cohort they rarely coincide with MDM2 amplification. That pattern was planted to mirror one seen in real tumor cohorts: MDM2 marks p53 for degradation, so amplifying it is a second way to switch p53 off, and a tumor seldom needs both. CDKN1A itself is rarely altered, and a mutation grid cannot tell us whether p21 is activated in those patients.

View 2, Protein: where the mutations fall

The oncoprint marks a patient as TP53-altered without saying where in the gene. The lollipop plot lays the 393-residue protein out as a line: the horizontal axis is amino acid position, colored blocks mark functional domains, and each stem's height is how many patients carry a mutation at that residue.

Most cancer mutations in TP53 are missense changes in the DNA-binding domain, and they pile up at a handful of residues: R175, G245, R248, R249, R273 and R282. They do not all break p53 the same way. R248 and R273 touch DNA directly, so a mutation there removes a contact while the fold can stay largely intact. R175H is a structural hotspot: it disrupts the fold near the zinc-binding site that holds the DNA-binding surface in shape.

Focus:

Figure 2. TP53 mutation hotspots. Stem heights come from a simulated cohort. The hotspot positions and their contact or structural class are real, and color separates the two classes.

Missense mutations also explain why p53 often accumulates in tumor cells. A missense mutant p53 can no longer switch on MDM2, whose product normally degrades p53, so the broken protein is not cleared and builds up in the nucleus. Strong p53 staining in a tumor biopsy often signals a mutant rather than an active pathway.

View 3, Epigenome: DNA methylation

A gene's output can also drop without any mutation. Even in tumors with intact TP53, methylation near the CDKN1A promoter can go with a weaker transcriptional response. The β value at each CpG site reports the methylation level on a 0 to 1 scale.

Show:

Figure 3. Top: CpG-level β values across 1.5kb of the CDKN1A promoter. Dots are individual CpGs (hover or tap for position and β). The TSS sits at 0. Bottom: per-sample heatmap, with patients as rows and CpGs as columns. Not every sample in the "methylated" group silences the entire promoter.

A patient with a methylated, low-response CDKN1A promoter can look normal in the oncoprint and still fail to produce a p21 arrest signal.

View 4, Signaling: pathway topology

The first three views are measurements. The fourth is a map of known biochemistry: a pathway diagram of activation and inhibition relationships worked out over decades. Triggering DNA damage in Figure 4 shows which branches are lost when TP53 cannot activate its targets.

Click "Trigger DNA damage" to light the pathway. Toggle the R175H state first to compare canonical TP53 transactivation.

Figure 4. The canonical TP53 response. Activating edges are arrows; inhibitory edges end in bars. The mutant toggle disables TP53's transcriptional activity in this simplified model. R175H can also have gain-of-function effects that are outside this diagram. Click any node for a one-line description.

If TP53 cannot bind and activate its target promoters, DNA damage fails to produce the p21 arrest signal.

View 5, Cells: the readout in expression

The pathway predicts a readout: in TP53-mutant cells, CDKN1A expression should fall. Single-cell RNA sequencing can check that, cell type by cell type. A heatmap of average expression would hide a distinction here, because a middling average can mean every cell expresses the gene a little or a few cells express it strongly. The dot plot encodes both quantities: dot size is the fraction of cells in a group that express the gene, and color is the group's average expression.

Hover or tap a dot to compare wildtype and mutant values.

Figure 5. Comparing wildtype and mutant tumors. CDKN1A shows reduced expression across most cell types in the mutant, even though TP53 levels appear similar.

In these simulated tumors, TP53 mRNA is present at the same level in wildtype and mutant cells. The R175H mutation changes the protein and leaves transcription of the gene alone. CDKN1A drops in both the fraction of expressing cells and the mean level. From the TP53 row alone the mutant tumor would look normal, and the loss shows up only one step downstream.

What each view can and cannot say

Each view has a blind spot. The oncoprint counts events without measuring their effect, the lollipop says nothing about expression, and mutation calling misses methylation entirely. A claim that TP53 loss silences p21 in a tumor type is stronger when several of the views agree.
