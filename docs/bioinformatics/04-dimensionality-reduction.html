Dimensionality Reduction

Twenty thousand genes per cell is invisible. The 2D scatter plot is a compression — and a lie.

A single-cell RNA-seq experiment measures ~20,000 genes in each of tens of thousands of cells. Every cell is a point in a 20,000-dimensional space we cannot plot. Dimensionality reduction produces 2D coordinates such that similar cells land close together on the page. The rest of this article walks through what the compression actually does, where linear methods fail, and how to read the result without being misled by it.

From a matrix to a scatter

Every point in a UMAP is a row in a cell × gene matrix. The marks in the matrix are an expression signature; the 2D position summarises that signature's similarity to everyone else's.

Figure 1. 40 cells × 24 highly variable genes on the left, the UMAP of the same cells on the right. Hover either side — the same cell highlights in both. The 2D coordinates have no intrinsic axis meaning; they encode high-dimensional proximity only.

When linear methods fail

Principal component analysis (PCA) projects onto the top two directions of maximum variance — exact, fast, and fundamentally linear. When the signal lies on a curved manifold embedded in a noisy high-dimensional space, PCA picks the noise and loses the structure. UMAP and t-SNE build a local-neighbourhood graph first and optimise for preserving adjacency, so they recover non-linear structure that PCA cannot.

Figure 2. 200 cells arranged as two interlocking half-moons in 2D, then embedded in 12D by adding 10 noise dimensions with higher variance than the signal. PCA's top-2 directions are dominated by noise; UMAP's neighbourhood graph recovers the moons cleanly. Hover any point — it highlights in all three panels.

PCA is not bad; it is the right tool when the interesting axes coincide with high linear variance. For exploratory single-cell work the non-linear methods dominate, because biological state tends to live on curved manifolds in a noisy high-D space.

Reading the embedding

A UMAP is a coordinate system with no intrinsic meaning. Cluster identity comes from marker-gene overlays: if the group of cells that lights up for CD3D is the same group that lights up for IL7R, it is a T-cell cluster. The inter-cluster distance on the page is not a meaningful measurement — UMAP optimises local neighbourhoods at the expense of global geometry. Two clusters that sit far apart may be biologically similar; two that abut may be quite different.

Figure 3. 200 cells across five cell types. Cluster colouring is the default; click a gene button to recolour by expression. Hovering draws neighbourhood edges (green = in-cluster, red = cross-cluster). Selective markers like CD3D light one cluster; pan-leukocyte PTPRC glows everywhere. Faithfulness of 8 ⁄ 10 is typical inside a cluster; cells on cluster boundaries drop to 5 ⁄ 10.

A UMAP is four things at once: a compressed gene signature, a method-dependent summary, a local-neighbourhood preserver, and a substrate for marker overlays. A UMAP by itself tells you almost nothing; a UMAP plus a curated marker panel is one of the most information-dense visualisations in modern biology.
