ChIP-seq Peaks

ChIP-seq maps where a protein sits on DNA. This page simulates a wildtype and an R175H p53 experiment on the real CDKN1A locus, calls peaks in both, and then checks the p53 motif in the real sequence against real p53 experiments.

Five Views showed several routes by which the TP53 pathway can fail. ChIP-seq (chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing) gives direct physical evidence for one of them: where a protein is bound on the genome at the moment the cells were fixed. The question here is whether the R175H mutant of p53 still binds the CDKN1A promoter. The reads below are simulated, so the answer at CDKN1A is one the simulation was built to give; what the page can show honestly is how the evidence is read, where a caller draws its line, and what the real sequence and real experiments say about the sites themselves.

The assay cross-links proteins to DNA, shears the DNA into fragments of a few hundred bases, pulls down the fragments bound by the target protein with an antibody, and sequences them. Mapping the reads back to the reference gives a pileup: how many fragments cover each position. Because the assay sees whole fragments, a bound site shows up as a hill about a fragment wide, not as the protein's 20-base footprint.

Reading the signal

Any antibody pull-down also collects background: open chromatin breaks more easily during shearing, amplified regions have more copies, repeats confuse the mapping, and some sequences are simply sticky. The input control, sheared DNA from the same cells with no pull-down, measures that local background. A good transcription-factor peak rises above input, appears in biological replicates, and usually has the expected motif near its summit.

The wildtype track rises to fragments at the 5′ site against about in the flanks, and the hill is bp wide at half height, about the length of one fragment, because the site is seen through the fragments that happen to cover it. The two response elements are bp apart and read as two hills. The gene model is worth a look too. CDKN1A's first exon is bp of untranslated sequence, and the coding sequence starts kb downstream in exon 2, so the p53 sites that switch it on sit kb upstream of a gene whose protein-coding part is far away.

Input is flat except at the promoter, where every sample, pulled down or not, has extra fragments from open chromatin. The R175H track shows nothing a reader would call a peak. R175H is a structural hotspot: arginine 175 sits in the loops held by the zinc ion that keep the DNA-binding surface in shape, so the mutation can unfold the surface needed for sequence-specific binding. Contact hotspots such as R248 and R273 instead change residues that touch the DNA.

Peak calling

Motif searches, target lists and differential binding all need a discrete list of bound regions. Peak calling turns the continuous pileup into one. MACS, a widely used caller, compares the ChIP pileup with a background rate estimated locally from the input, and reports coordinates, summits, p-values, fold enrichment and false-discovery estimates. The caller on this page follows that recipe in miniature. For each 20-bp bin it counts the ChIP fragments covering it, \(k\), and asks how surprising \(k\) would be from background alone:

\(p = P(X \ge k), \;\; X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_{\text{local}})\)
\(\lambda_{\text{local}} = \max(\lambda_{\text{BG}}, \lambda_{1\,\text{kb}}, \lambda_{5\,\text{kb}}, \lambda_{10\,\text{kb}})\)

The rate is the largest of the region-wide average and the input's rate in 1, 5 and 10 kb around the bin, so a bin in open chromatin is compared with open chromatin. The tail is summed exactly, in logarithms, so even a summit with p near \(10^{-60}\) gets its own value. The p-values of all bins in the 40-kb simulated region go through Benjamini-Hochberg to give q-values, and runs of bins above the threshold, joined across gaps of up to 40 bp, become peak regions. The summit is the bin with the most fragments.

Figure 2. A toy caller in the spirit of MACS, on the simulated samples of Figure 1. Bars are −log10 q for each 20-bp bin (square-root axis). Bins at or above the dashed threshold are called and joined into regions (shaded), each with a summit dot. The default, q ≤ 0.05, is MACS2's default cutoff; the slider is logarithmic.

The caller's own threshold is not where the decision gets made in practice. ENCODE's pipelines run the caller loose on purpose, so that the weak end of the list still contains noise, and then keep the peaks that independently prepared replicates rank the same way, a cut made on the irreproducible discovery rate (IDR). The same guidelines set the scale of a usable experiment: at least 10 million uniquely mapped reads per replicate, and a fraction of reads falling in peaks (FRiP) of 1% or more, which 787 of the 1,052 ENCODE data sets they reviewed reached.

Lost or moved?

Comparing the two samples at many targets asks whether R175H has lost binding everywhere or only moved it. Figure 3 asks the first half. It runs the same simulation and the same caller at ten canonical p53 targets, with two replicates of each sample, and calls each site from the result rather than from a label.

A table of canonical sites cannot see binding that moved, because it only looks where wildtype p53 was. To see that, you call peaks in the mutant sample on its own and ask what it binds that wildtype does not. Zhu et al. (2015) did this for the gain-of-function mutants R273H, R248Q and R249S, not R175H. The mutants bound the promoters of the chromatin regulators KMT2A (MLL1), KMT2D (MLL2) and KAT6A (MOZ), which wildtype p53 did not bind, while wildtype p53 bound CDKN1A, MDM2 and BBC3 as expected. The mutant peaks were enriched for ETS motifs rather than the p53 motif, and the mutant protein binds the transcription factor ETS2, which the authors propose brings it there. The lower rows of Figure 3 borrow that result. So in this simulation the answer is both, because both were put in; in the published experiments the answer is that binding moved to sites the p53 motif does not mark.

A motif is not a binding site

If p53 finds its sites by reading DNA, why not find them from the sequence? The p53 response element is two copies of the ten-letter pattern RRRCWWGYYY (R is A or G, W is A or T, Y is C or T), separated by 0 to 13 bases. Beyond binding

ChIP-seq says where a protein was bound at fixation, and nothing about whether that binding changed transcription. A bound site counts as regulation only when the nearby gene's expression moves with it, which is why ChIP-seq is paired with RNA-seq from the same cells. The next article takes that second measurement, where CDKN1A turns up among the genes that truly change.
